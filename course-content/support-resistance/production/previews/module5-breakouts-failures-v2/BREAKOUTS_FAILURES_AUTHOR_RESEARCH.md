# Breakouts & Failures — Author Research Notes

**Purpose:** Research brief for expanding TradeThesis Module 5 (Lessons 12–14). This is a synthesis of publicly available author/publisher descriptions, book contents, and author articles. It is not a substitute for a full page-by-page review of each book. All student-facing explanations in the accompanying lesson drafts are original wording.

## The most useful ideas to bring into the module

### 1. John J. Murphy — filters are methods of handling ambiguity, not guarantees

**Book:** *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets*.

The book's contents include the use of closing prices and gaps, volume, price patterns, variations from ideal patterns, and continuation patterns. These subjects are directly relevant to the breakout problem: an intraday penetration can differ from a close beyond a prior extreme, and a real chart may deviate from the diagram a textbook uses to explain it.

**Teaching takeaways for our module:**
- Define the boundary and observation timeframe before deciding whether price has broken it.
- Distinguish a wick beyond an area from a close beyond it; both are evidence, but not the same evidence.
- Filters such as waiting for a close, a specified penetration, or more than one close can reduce some ambiguous cases but introduce delay and still do not guarantee continuation.
- Volume can be a useful piece of context where the data is meaningful, but it should not be treated as a universal yes/no oracle. It is important that the course acknowledge that practitioners differ on how much weight to give volume.
- Real chart patterns are often imperfect; do not reshape the level or relabel the pattern after seeing the outcome.

**Original hook:** “A filter can make a rule more consistent without making it infallible.”

**Use in:** Lesson 12, with a return in Lesson 14 when a breakout fails despite initial closes outside the zone.

**Research limits:** The publicly viewable Google Books listing confirms the book and relevant table-of-contents topics. It does not expose every relevant passage in full. We should not claim that the exact sequence of lesson rules below is Murphy’s own prescribed trading system.

### 2. Adam Grimes — the transition between a range and a trend is a difficult place

**Book:** *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis: Market Structure, Price Action, and Trading Strategies*.

The publisher's chapter summaries cover interfaces between trends and ranges, practical trading templates, failure tests, breakouts entered in the preceding base, breakouts entered on the first pullback after a break, and failed breakouts. Grimes's own “Failure Test” article describes a move beyond a prior pivot or support/resistance area that quickly reverses. His public “Fundamental Trading Patterns” page also distinguishes simple/complex pullbacks and failure tests.

**Teaching takeaways for our module:**
- A breakout is a transition attempt from a range into a directional phase; transitions can be uncertain and noisy.
- Do not teach “the breakout” as one candle. The path leading into the boundary and the behaviour following it both matter.
- Separate the *place* of a breakout from the *way a learner might approach it*: a move out of the preceding base is not the same setup as a first pullback after the break. We will teach this distinction conceptually, without giving a universal entry recipe.
- A failure test is a recognisable event: price moves beyond a meaningful prior point and then quickly returns. It is not proof that every failed test must trigger a large opposite move.
- Study failed examples, not just winners. Reconstruct what was knowable before the outcome, then reveal what happened.

**Original hook:** “The hardest part of a breakout may be identifying what kind of market you have just entered.”

**Use in:** all three lessons, especially Lesson 14.

### 3. Jack Schwager and Mark Etzkorn — a failed signal is information, not merely a bad prediction

**Book:** *A Complete Guide to the Futures Market*, especially Chapter 8 (Support and Resistance) and Chapter 15 (The Most Important Rule in Chart Analysis).

Chapter 8 explains range boundaries, prior major highs/lows and how a former range boundary may change its role after a breakout. Chapter 15 gives failed signals unusually strong emphasis and discusses bull/bear traps and failures across several chart structures—not just horizontal zones. Its publicly available chapter preview notes the possibility of a substantial move in the opposite direction when a market does not follow through on a chart signal.

**Teaching takeaways for our module:**
- A break that cannot hold, then returns into the prior range, weakens the continuation thesis.
- An opposite move becomes a possibility worth considering; it is not guaranteed and does not automatically constitute a trade.
- Failed signals can appear in different structures. Students should learn the reasoning (signal → missing follow-through → re-entry/contradiction → revised interpretation), not memorise a single named pattern.
- Role reversal is conditional: an old boundary may behave differently after price crosses it, but it can also fail to do so.

**Original hook:** “The failure of the expected move is new evidence about the original idea.”

**Use in:** Lesson 13 for conditional role reversal and Lesson 14 for failure analysis.

### 4. Brian Shannon — a breakout only means something at a particular scale

**Book:** *Technical Analysis Using Multiple Timeframes*.

Shannon's public material explains that timeframes act like different levels of magnification and that a local move can look different when placed in a broader context. In a public interview, Shannon also pushes back against a rule that every breakout must first be confirmed by unusually high volume; that is his practice and not a universal consensus.

**Teaching takeaways for our module:**
- Ask whether the level is meaningful on the chart/timeframe being studied, and occasionally show the same sequence within its higher-timeframe context.
- One timeframe may show a breakout while a broader chart shows a larger range or nearby resistance.
- Volume is evidence that some practitioners use, not a universally mandatory gate; a learner should not use it to ignore contradictory price action.

**Original hook:** “A breakout can be real on one chart and still be small inside the bigger picture.”

**Use in:** a short context panel in Lesson 12 and the transfer challenge in Lesson 14. Avoid turning this module into a complete multi-timeframe trading system.

## The four principles we should actually teach

1. **Context before label.** Define the prior area and the market state before naming a breakout.
2. **Evidence, not magic confirmation.** Close, penetration, time outside the zone, volume and follow-through are different kinds of evidence with different costs. None is perfect.
3. **After the break is part of the event.** Hold, return, back-through, no retest and inconclusive sequences should be distinguished rather than compressed into one pattern name.
4. **Failure updates the thesis; it does not dictate a trade.** Re-entry into the prior range weakens continuation. A possible opposite move must still be evaluated, and “wait / unclear” remains valid.

## What we must not import as dogma

- “A breakout must have high volume.” Practitioners disagree; volume can be helpful, but it is not a universal guarantee.
- “Two closes confirm a breakout.” A time filter is a convention, not an assurance. The historical NIFTY 50 example here closes beyond the selected zone for multiple sessions and then moves back below it.
- “A failed breakout guarantees a reversal.” It increases the relevance of an alternative, but price may also chop, recover again, or remain ambiguous.
- “Repeated tests make a level guaranteed to fail” or “make it guaranteed to hold.” Neither follows from a touch count alone.
- “Stop hunters / institutions did this.” OHLC bars alone do not establish hidden intent.
- “A drawn line is the market's exact barrier.” The learner must treat a zone as a defensible description, not an objective law.

## Additional lesson-design conclusions from source review

### Failed signals as a family, not one horizontal-line setup

Schwager and Etzkorn's Chapter 15 has a broad taxonomy: failed signals, bull and bear traps, false trend-line breakouts, returns to wide-ranging-day extremes, counter-to-anticipated or opposite-direction breakouts around flags/pennants, and penetrations of tops/bottoms. The module should borrow the general question—what happened after a signal appeared?—without trying to teach every named setup as a method. The added FIG10 visual is a schematic comparison for transfer, not a catalogue of trading entries.

### Failure test as a defined sequence

Grimes defines a failure test around price pushing beyond a prior pivot/support-resistance area and then quickly reversing. The useful teaching sequence is prior boundary → attempted break → contradiction/reversal → subsequent behaviour. The lesson must not imply the failure is known at the first penetration.

### Volume is a practitioner convention with trade-offs

Murphy's published teaching puts volume among the key confirming signs around price patterns, while Shannon has described why he does not require a volume surge for every price move. These perspectives should be taught as a meaningful difference in analytical practice, not reconciled into a made-up universal rule. A learner should know the instrument's volume field, timeframe and baseline before relying on it.
