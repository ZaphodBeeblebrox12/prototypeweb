# Module 5 — Breakouts, Retests and Failed Signals

**Version:** v3 illustrated internal teaching review  
**Purpose:** Source note for original TradeThesis explanations and chart design. This is not a substitute for the books and does not reproduce their text or diagrams.

## 1. John J. Murphy — *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets*

Murphy's reference is broad rather than built around one proprietary breakout trick. The published contents include closing prices and gaps, volume, continuation patterns, top/bottom formations, and variations from ideal patterns. Those are useful foundations for asking exactly what crossed a boundary, where a bar ended, what the volume series measures, and how real price sequences depart from polished textbook examples.

**Applied in this module:**
- Separate an intrabar penetration from a close outside the area.
- Treat volume as relevant context when the data is suitable, rather than a universal gate.
- Use less-than-ideal examples as part of instruction, not as exceptions after the student has memorised a perfect pattern.

We do **not** attribute a universal one-close, two-close, distance or volume rule to Murphy. Those are conventions that a learner may choose and test, not a guaranteed formula supplied by the lesson.

Source: [Google Books — book record and contents](https://books.google.com/books/about/Technical_Analysis_of_the_Financial_Mark.html?id=ojTgDwAAQBAJ)

## 2. Adam Grimes — *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis*

Grimes emphasises context, the transition between trends and ranges, and the limits of pattern labels. His public article “The Failure Test” describes a failure test as price pressing beyond a prior pivot or support/resistance area and then quickly reversing. His article “Pattern Development in the GBP” examines how an initial pullback after a break can hold while a later pullback appears to break down. This is a useful corrective to the simplistic idea that every pullback after a breakout means the same thing.

**Applied in this module:**
- Define the prior pivot or zone before the attempted break.
- Reveal the first break and the later reversal separately; do not label the attempt a failure before contradictory evidence appears.
- Compare an initial pullback that holds with a later pullback that fails.
- Teach failure tests as a sequence-recognition concept, not a ready-made countertrend entry or a promise of a strong opposite move.

## 3. Jack D. Schwager and Mark Etzkorn — *A Complete Guide to the Futures Market*

Their Chapter 15, “The Most Important Rule in Chart Analysis,” gives failed signals a full treatment. The published contents name bull and bear traps, false trendline breakouts, returns to spike and wide-ranging-day extremes, and failures around flags, pennants, top/bottom formations and curved price patterns. The chapter's key concept is that the absence of follow-through after a chart signal can make the opposite scenario more relevant. That is a conditional observation, not proof that a large reverse move must happen.

**Applied in this module:**
- Expand “failed breakout” beyond a single horizontal resistance line.
- Teach failure families: range-edge excursion and return, trendline penetration/reclaim, pivot break quickly surrendered, continuation-pause failure, and the return after a wide-range or gap move.
- Keep the original signal visible and reveal the contradiction later.
- Ask what happens after the contradiction: move into the old range, chop, or reclaim. Do not promise a target at the opposite range edge.

Sources:
- [Wiley — Chapter 8, “Support and Resistance”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119209713.ch8)
- [Wiley / O’Reilly — Chapter 15, “The Most Important Rule in Chart Analysis”](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/a-complete-guide/9781118853757/c15.xhtml)

## 4. Thomas N. Bulkowski — *Visual Guide to Chart Patterns* (additional empirical perspective)

Bulkowski treats throwbacks and pullbacks as events with their own definitions and data, rather than as a mandatory stage every breakout must complete. The Wiley chapter summary for “Throwbacks and Pullbacks” reports that a throwback happened in 58% of 10,305 identified chart patterns in a sample described as covering patterns since 2000. The same publisher summary notes that in 25% of those throwback cases, price fell below the bottom of the chart pattern.

These figures are useful because they challenge a common assumption: a retest is neither rare enough to ignore nor dependable enough to require. But the statistics belong to a particular sample of identified chart patterns and a particular definition of a throwback. They are **not** the probability of a retest on every manually drawn support/resistance zone, on every asset class, or on every timeframe. Do not present the percentages as a universal law or a trade expectancy.

**Applied in Lesson 13:**
- Teach “no return”, shallow return, deep return and failed return as distinct paths.
- Explain that waiting for a retest changes which situations a learner sees; it does not automatically make the decision safe.
- Use empirical statistics as scoped evidence, followed by limitations—not as marketing claims of predictive certainty.

Sources:
- [Wiley — “Throwbacks and Pullbacks”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119203537.ch6)
- [Wiley — “The Throwback Buy Setup”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119203537.ch20)

## 5. Brian Shannon — timeframe context and confirmation trade-offs

Shannon's public material uses multiple timeframes as different levels of magnification: a local break may remain inside a broader range. His public commentary on volume also illustrates that practitioners can differ on whether an unusually high volume reading must be present before a move is considered meaningful.

**Applied in this module:**
- Compare the local breakout with the same move inside a wider structure.
- Explain volume as context with a source and baseline, not an absolute pass/fail gate.
- Avoid expanding this module into a full multi-timeframe trading system.

Sources:
- [Brian Shannon — “6 Ways to Trade Using Multiple Timeframes”](https://alphatrends.net/archives/6-ways-you-can-trade-with-multiple-timeframes/)
- [Brian Shannon interview on price and volume](https://alphatrends.net/archives/interview-stockbsessed-03112023/)

## 6. Carol L. Osler — a bounded empirical result, not a universal proof

Osler's “Support for Resistance: Technical Analysis and Intraday Exchange Rates” evaluated support/resistance levels supplied by six foreign-exchange firms in a specific historical setting. The study found predictive value for intraday trend interruptions, with differences across currency pairs and firms. It helps explain why levels merit study, but it does not validate every manually drawn zone, all markets or every retail breakout rule. Related work examines the way stop-loss and take-profit orders clustered in a large FX dealing bank; those findings still do not let a student infer hidden intent from an ordinary candle chart.

**Applied in this module:**
- Teach that levels can be useful hypotheses without being exact, guaranteed barriers.
- Do not say a specific historical breakout was caused by stop hunting, institutions or a deliberate trap unless independent evidence is available.

Sources:
- [Federal Reserve Bank of New York — “Support for Resistance”](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.html)
- [Federal Reserve Bank of New York — “Currency Orders and Exchange-Rate Dynamics”](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports/sr125.html)

## Concepts added in v3 that were underdeveloped in the first pass

1. **Signal taxonomy:** the prior boundary can be a range edge, trendline, swing pivot, gap or chart-pattern boundary. The failure description should name the structure.
2. **After-the-break evidence:** no retest, delayed return, shallow touch, deep entry, close back inside and reclaim are different cases.
3. **Pullbacks are not interchangeable:** a first pullback may hold; a later pullback may challenge the continuation thesis.
4. **Failure can fail too:** a reclaim can challenge the failure interpretation. Preserve both observations rather than rewriting history.
5. **Gaps and wide-range bars:** price may jump beyond the band or extend through it intraday, so the path cannot be inferred from the close alone.
6. **Range travel is a scenario, not a destination:** a failed upper break may be followed by movement into the old range, chop or reclaim; the opposite edge is not a guaranteed target.
7. **Volume is an independent measurement:** its relationship to the price outcome is informative but not deterministic, and the data feed matters.
8. **Process versus outcome:** preserve the initial judgement and state what changed, rather than reward hindsight.

## 7. Stop placement in failed-breakout examples — a bounded teaching point

This addition is intentionally narrow: the learner-facing text marks a structural stop only in Lesson 14's hypothetical failed-breakout examples. It does not add stop markers to ordinary breakout, retest or role-reversal charts.

Adam Grimes's public “The Failure Test” article is unusually specific about the structural risk point. For a short failure test after price trades above resistance and reverses, it discusses a stop just above the high of the failure bar; if the reversal is recognised on a following bar, it notes that the new high set by the move may become the relevant reference. The long-side example mirrors the logic below support. The same article warns that gaps can move through a stop, including in range conditions or when price continues beyond a trend extreme.

Schwager and Etzkorn's chapter “Choosing Stop-Loss Points” states a broader principle: technical stop points should be related to the point at which price action changes the technical picture. That supports teaching a stop as a structural invalidation point rather than a convenient fixed distance. It does not give us a universal buffer, guarantee exact fills, or justify the same stop convention for all markets and timeframes.

**TradeThesis adaptation:** demonstrate only the structural idea in a failed-breakout context. For a hypothetical failed-upside break, the idea is contradicted if price re-establishes itself beyond the failure extreme; for a hypothetical failed-downside break, use the mirror extreme. The chart labels are schematic and not trade signals. The teaching text explicitly says to reduce exposure or pass if the structural distance is too large, rather than artificially tightening the stop. Position sizing, order type, slippage model and an entry/exit strategy are outside this lesson's scope.

Sources:
- [Adam Grimes — “The Failure Test”](https://www.adamhgrimes.com/failure-test-2/)
- [Wiley — “Choosing Stop-Loss Points,” *A Complete Guide to the Futures Market*, Chapter 13](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119209713.ch13)

## V5 — failure taxonomy, instructor voice and simple-to-complex retest sequence

The latest pass expands the reader-facing taxonomy without naming sources in the lessons. The intent is to make the learner look at a chart and know which boundary was being tested, what an actual failure sequence would look like, and what evidence would challenge the label.

### Additional source-supported boundaries

- **Range-edge upthrust / false upside break:** identify a pre-existing upper range boundary, the excursion above it, the return inside, and whether a later reclaim can hold. Do not label the initial crossing as a failure before contradictory evidence arrives.
- **Spring-like downside failure:** use the corresponding sequence below a prior range floor. The label is descriptive shorthand for a downside probe that returns into the range; it does not prove deliberate trapping or a guaranteed advance.
- **Trendline penetration and reclaim:** distinguish a sloping reference from a horizontal range. Check whether important pivots also change and whether the wider swing structure agrees with the line-crossing interpretation.
- **Prior swing break quickly surrendered:** define the prior pivot before the move; distinguish an excursion from a close back through the pivot and the follow-through that strengthens or weakens the failure-test description.
- **Continuation-pause failure:** a flag/pennant or compact pause may break with or against the prior move. The pattern label does not override the price response.
- **First pullback versus later failure, neckline reclaim, gaps and wide-range bars:** these are additional cases in which the subsequent path can contradict the initially attractive pattern interpretation. They are now explained in full instructor prose and paired with a diagram where helpful.

### Stop placement: specifically scoped to failed-breakout examples

Adam Grimes's public “The Failure Test” article describes a move beyond a prior pivot or support/resistance area that quickly reverses. For a possible short after an upside failure, it discusses a structural stop just above the failure bar's high; where the reversal becomes clear only on the next bar, the new high may be the relevant reference. The downside/long-side situation mirrors that reasoning. The article also warns that gaps can move through stops and cause worse execution.

Schwager and Etzkorn's Chapter 13, “Choosing Stop-Loss Points,” gives the broader principle: the stop point should relate to the point at which price movement changes the technical picture. The course adapts this as a structural invalidation idea, not a fixed buffer or recommendation. The chart marker is intentionally confined to Lesson 14, where the learner is analysing a hypothetical failed-breakout setup. No stop marker appears in Lessons 12 or 13.
