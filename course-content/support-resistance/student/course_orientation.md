## Welcome

Support and resistance can make a chart easier to read—but only when we use them carefully.

This course is about learning to notice areas where price has responded before, describe what the chart actually shows, and think through what might happen next without treating any level as a promise.

You will not be given a list of magic prices or a universal entry signal. Instead, you will build a repeatable way to look at a chart, make your reasoning visible, and reconsider it when the evidence changes.

A useful starting point is simple: **a zone is an area of interest, not a prediction.**

## What you will learn

By the end of the course, you should be better able to:

- Describe the broad structure before drawing anything.
- Select a small number of meaningful turning points and zones.
- Draw boundaries that you can explain, without pretending there is one exact answer for every chart.
- Distinguish an observable reaction from an interpretation of what it might mean.
- Assess a break, retest or failed attempt as a sequence rather than judging one candle in isolation.
- Build conditional scenarios, define what would weaken them, and recognise when the chart is too unclear or offers too little room.
- Review a decision based on the evidence available at the time, separately from whether its outcome was profitable.

These are chart-reading and decision-process skills. They are not a guarantee of trading results and are not a substitute for a complete trading plan or risk management.

## How the course is organised

The course moves through six modules:

1. **Understand** — learn what a zone can and cannot tell you; read the structure first.
2. **Find** — identify the turns and areas that deserve attention, then reduce clutter.
3. **Draw and evaluate** — make defensible boundaries and assess zones in context.
4. **Read behaviour** — study the approach, possible rejection, acceptance and changing evidence.
5. **Breakouts and failures** — distinguish a crossing from more sustained movement and examine retests and failed attempts.
6. **Apply and review** — build conditional scenarios, review real decisions, and complete a final unfamiliar-chart assessment.

The intended guided course time is approximately **5–6 hours**, plus your own practice. You do not need to rush. Taking a little time to explain why you marked an area is more useful than marking many areas quickly.

## A useful way to work through each activity

When you are given a chart, use this sequence:

1. **Observe.** Describe visible structure, turns, pauses and movement. Avoid explaining why they happened before you have described them.
2. **Interpret.** Say what those observations may suggest. Keep the difference between evidence and inference clear.
3. **Make it conditional.** Use language such as “If price does X, then Y becomes more plausible.” Do not phrase a scenario as a certainty.
4. **Name what would change your view.** State what would weaken or invalidate the idea, and what alternative remains possible.
5. **Review the reasoning.** When a reference explanation becomes available, compare the logic—not just the exact placement of a line or zone.

Some activities ask you to mark a chart. Others ask you to compare examples, write a short explanation, or complete a scenario card. In the three ChartLab exercises, the order matters: make your own attempt before opening the reference and explanation.

## It is acceptable not to know yet

A chart may support more than one defensible reading. The available excerpt may be too short. A boundary may be unclear. A break may not yet have enough follow-through to judge. You may not have enough room to form a sensible scenario.

In those situations, “unclear,” “wait for more evidence,” or “stand aside” can be reasoned answers. You are not expected to manufacture certainty.

The reference marking in an activity is an example of a defensible interpretation, not a claim that every other boundary is wrong. When you compare your response with it, ask: Did I use visible evidence? Was my convention consistent? Did I explain what I left out? Did I allow the evidence to change my view?

## Knowledge checks and progress

Short knowledge checks reinforce the lesson's core idea. They are there to help you identify what needs another look, not to predict whether you will make money. Read the explanation when it is presented and return to the relevant lesson concept if you missed a question.

The final assessment uses an unfamiliar chart so you can apply the method without copying an example you have already seen. The proposed scoring and completion thresholds are still subject to pilot testing; the course should reward coherent reasoning and appropriate uncertainty, not pixel-perfect zone placement.

## Scope and expectations

This course focuses on support/resistance interpretation and the decisions that can be organised around it. It does not establish one universal entry trigger, position-sizing rule or complete strategy for every market. Any real instructor case is an example of that person's actual process, not a rule that every learner should copy.

All controlled schematic charts are illustrative, not historical market data. Where a real instructor example is used, its source and any reconstruction must be described honestly.

**For educational purposes only. Trading involves risk.**

## Start here

Begin with Lesson 1. Look at the unlabelled chart before reading its explanation. Write down what you can actually see—even if your answer is only that several reactions appear to cluster in a similar area.

The goal is not to guess the next move. The goal is to get better at reading the evidence in front of you.
