## 1. Executive decision

Build an 18-lesson, six-module self-paced course. The course is not a line-drawing tutorial and not a ready-made trading system. It teaches learners to read price structure, identify defensible support/resistance areas, interpret price behaviour around them, develop conditional scenarios and review a trade decision without confusing outcome with process quality.

The learning sequence is deliberate:

1. **Look at simple, controlled charts first.** Give learners one question at a time and let them observe before explaining the answer.
2. **Teach structure before zones.** Do not introduce complex trade decisions while learners are still learning to identify swings and ranges.
3. **Introduce ChartLab only after the learner has been shown what a zone is and why boundaries can vary.** The first attempt is guided and low-stakes.
4. **Use ChartLab to practise a narrow skill, not as a substitute for teaching.** Three studies form a practice progression: Clear W, Uneven Lows and Wider Neckline.
5. **Introduce the instructor's own charts after learners have attempted controlled examples.** First use a short “micro-case” that reveals only one decision at a time. Keep the entry and outcome hidden until students have reasoned about the chart.
6. **Show full real-trade case studies only after learners understand zones, approaches, rejection/acceptance, breakouts and invalidation.** Reveal the trade chronologically rather than displaying a fully annotated winning chart first.
7. **Finish with an unfamiliar chart and a written rationale.** Grade the evidence and reasoning, not pixel-perfect drawings or whether price later went in the predicted direction.

### Recommended course workload

Approximately 5–6 hours of guided lesson time, plus independent practice and optional replay. The time is a planning estimate and should be adjusted after the content and activities are tested. Avoid adding filler to hit a duration target.

### Course promise

By the end, a learner should be able to analyse an unfamiliar chart and explain the structure; choose and defend relevant zones; interpret the approach, reaction or break; describe conditional scenarios and invalidation; consider the next opposing area; and recognise when the chart is too ambiguous or offers too little space to justify a trade.

### What this course does not promise

No zone is guaranteed to hold. Touch count, wicks, closes, retests and breakouts are not standalone guarantees; a candle does not reveal institutional intent by itself; and no single set of boundaries is always uniquely correct. Sound analysis also does not guarantee a profitable outcome.

This is educational chart-reading material, not personalised financial advice or a signal service. Keep the disclaimer clear but unobtrusive.

## 2. Source research: what the books and research should contribute

There is no single universally accepted book that settles every support/resistance question. The curriculum should triangulate established technical analysis, a critical treatment of market structure and the limits of empirical findings. Use the books to inform original teaching; do not copy their prose, charts, diagrams or proprietary examples.

### A. John J. Murphy — *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets*

**Role in our curriculum:** Broad foundation and terminology. The book includes a section on support and resistance alongside trend, price patterns and volume.

**Use in lessons:**
- Lesson 1: introduce support/resistance as conventional chart-analysis concepts.
- Lesson 2: teach trend and swing structure before asking learners to draw levels.
- Lesson 4: explain the practical convention of areas rather than demanding perfect alignment to one price.
- Lesson 5: introduce major highs/lows and chart context.

**Teaching note:** Murphy is a foundation, not the only authority. Teach the traditional concepts clearly, then show where a trader must test assumptions and where interpretation varies.

**Reference:** Penguin Random House, book page and publication details: https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/350647/technical-analysis-of-the-financial-markets-by-john-j-murphy/. The Google Books contents listing also identifies “Support and Resistance” as a section: https://books.google.com/books/about/Technical_Analysis_of_the_Financial_Mark.html?id=ojTgDwAAQBAJ.

### B. Jack D. Schwager and Mark Etzkorn — *A Complete Guide to the Futures Market*, Chapters 7–8

**Role in our curriculum:** The most directly focused textbook treatment in this reference set.

**Publisher chapter summaries cover:**
- trading ranges and their upper/lower boundaries;
- major prior highs and lows;
- concentrations of prior relative highs and lows;
- the possibility that a broken range boundary changes role; and
- caution around assuming every envelope-style method is reliable.

**Use in lessons:**
- Lesson 2: range structure.
- Lesson 5: major highs/lows and clusters of turning points.
- Lessons 12–14: breakouts, role reversal, retests and failures.
- Lesson 15: opposing zones and potential price objectives as context rather than automatic targets.

**Teaching note:** Present range boundaries and role reversal as possibilities that need evidence. Avoid wording such as “old resistance always becomes support.”

**References:** Wiley, Chapter 7 “Trading Ranges”: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119209713.ch7; Chapter 8 “Support and Resistance”: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119209713.ch8 (Chapter 8).

### C. Adam Grimes — *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis*, Chapters 4–6

**Role in our curriculum:** The key critical-thinking reference. It challenges conventional assumptions about support/resistance and examines transitions between ranges and trends.

**Publisher summaries and contents highlight:**
- price action around support/resistance is complex;
- some levels may have little value beyond arbitrary levels;
- trading ranges may function as continuation or reversal structures;
- transitions between trends and ranges involve uncertainty; and
- breakout trades often fail, requiring traders to understand failed moves and pullback behaviour.

**Use in lessons:**
- Lesson 1: do not present a zone as a guaranteed barrier.
- Lesson 5: select a few meaningful areas rather than label everything.
- Lesson 8: evaluate quality without an invented numerical score.
- Lessons 10–14: assess context and follow-through; consider failure as a normal possible outcome.
- Lessons 16–17: review process and outcome separately.

**Teaching note:** This book stops the course becoming a catalogue of “secret” levels. It should cause us to ask, “What evidence is visible here?” rather than, “Which named rule says this level must work?”

**References:** Wiley, *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis*: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/9781119202837; Chapter 4 “On Trading Ranges”: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119202837.ch4; Chapter 5 “Interfaces between Trends and Ranges”: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119202837.ch5; publisher table of contents: https://www.wiley-vch.de/en/areas-interest/finance-economics-law/the-art-science-of-technical-analysis-978-1-118-11512-1.

### D. Carol L. Osler — empirical research on support/resistance and currency orders

**Role in our curriculum:** Evidence literacy, not a rulebook.

Osler’s 2000 Federal Reserve Bank of New York study examined support/resistance levels supplied by six foreign-exchange firms and found that they helped predict intraday trend interruptions, with predictive power varying by currency pair and firm. Her 2001 research examined a large FX dealing bank’s order data and discussed clustered take-profit and stop-loss orders around round numbers, providing a possible microstructure explanation for some observed effects in that setting.

**Use in lessons:**
- Lesson 1: acknowledge that support/resistance is not purely an arbitrary story, but avoid claiming it works everywhere.
- Lesson 4 or 5: optionally discuss round numbers as a contextual observation, not a rule that must be drawn.
- Lesson 12: explain that price may accelerate after a widely observed level is crossed, but do not promise it will.
- Final review: students should state what is observed versus what they merely infer.

**Critical limitation to state:** This research is specific to FX data, firms, time periods and mechanisms studied. It does not validate every manually drawn zone, every market, every time frame or every retail trading rule. Do not use it to claim that a particular candle reveals the intentions of institutions.

**References:** Osler (2000), “Support for Resistance: Technical Analysis and Intraday Exchange Rates,” *Economic Policy Review*, Federal Reserve Bank of New York: https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.html (research article). Osler (2001), “Currency Orders and Exchange-Rate Dynamics: Explaining the Success of Technical Analysis,” Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Report 125: https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports/sr125.html (staff report).

### E. Brian Shannon — *Technical Analysis Using Multiple Timeframes*

**Role in our curriculum:** Supplementary reference for context across time frames. Do not make this the core authority for the course; it is a useful supporting perspective for reading local action in the context of a higher time frame.

**Use in lessons:** Lesson 5, then reinforce in Lesson 9 and the case studies. Avoid teaching a rigid timeframe hierarchy as a universal formula. Higher-timeframe context informs the analysis; it does not override all local evidence.

**Reference:** Google Books listing and description: https://books.google.com/books/about/Technical_Analysis_Using_Multiple_Timefr.html?id=b-Eo0AEACAAJ (book listing).

### F. Thomas N. Bulkowski — *Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns*, Third Edition

**Role in our curriculum:** Optional supporting reference when discussing pattern failure and the difference between chart-pattern observations and statistically measured historical results. It is not the primary support/resistance textbook.

**Use in lessons:** Lessons 12–14 and 17, as an optional further reading path only. We should not add a large detour into dozens of chart patterns.

**Reference:** Wiley publisher page: https://www.wiley-vch.de/en/areas-interest/finance-economics-law/finance-investments-13fi/trading-13fi4/encyclopedia-of-chart-patterns-978-1-119-73968-5 (publisher page).

### Source policy for TradeThesis

1. Attribute ideas and link to sources in a “Further reading” drawer or lesson reference line.
2. Explain concepts in original TradeThesis language and use our own annotated charts.
3. Do not reproduce a book's chart or extended text without permission.
4. Label a schematic illustration as schematic; never pass generated or schematic prices off as actual historical data.
5. Separate accepted chart-analysis convention, source-backed findings and the instructor's personal trading method.
6. Use empirical results with their limits, sample and market context in view.

## 3. Teaching architecture and chart-release policy

### The five-step teaching rhythm

Every lesson should follow a recognisable rhythm, but lesson formats should vary so the course does not feel like 18 identical text pages.

1. **Observe:** Show the chart or question before explaining the answer. Ask one useful question.
2. **Name:** Introduce only the concept required to answer that question.
3. **Demonstrate:** Add markings progressively and narrate why each one was placed.
4. **Practise:** Give the learner an unlabelled or partially labelled chart to work on independently.
5. **Review and retain:** Reveal the rationale, discuss acceptable alternatives, state one takeaway, and check understanding.

### Chart ladder: controlled chart → ChartLab → instructor's own chart

**Stage 1 — Controlled visual examples (Lessons 1–5).**
- Use clean, short excerpts selected to teach one thing at a time.
- Prefer an uncluttered chart with enough prior candles to understand the context, but not so much history that the student is searching aimlessly.
- Keep asset-specific and indicator-specific distractions low. If the source chart is historical, identify it as such; if it is schematic, label it.
- Show the plain chart first. Explain only after the learner has made an observation.
- Do not show an instructor's entry, target, stop or outcome.

**Stage 2 — ChartLab (beginning Lesson 5/6, integrated through the course).**
- Study 1: **Clear W** — first guided attempt. Students mark support, submit it, mark resistance, submit it, compare both zones with the reference, read tailored feedback and complete a short check.
- Study 2: **Uneven Lows** — practise when turning points do not align neatly. Feedback should discuss boundary logic and reasonable uncertainty, not simply say “wrong.”
- Study 3: **Wider Neckline** — practise a broader structural area, then discuss how the evidence changes as price interacts with it.
- Do not assign all three studies back-to-back with no teaching. Place each after the relevant explanation and follow each with a short debrief.
- A ChartLab submission is evidence of an attempt, not proof of mastery. The lesson should explain why the reference is defensible and when other boundaries may also be reasonable.

**Stage 3 — Instructor's own charts (micro-case after foundational practice; full cases in Module 6).**
- First appearance: a small cropped section in Lesson 9 or 10, after the learner has completed at least one controlled drawing activity. Reveal only the context and the price action leading into a zone. Hide your markings and keep the entry and result off-screen.
- Ask the learner to interpret the approach or distinguish rejection from inconclusive evidence. Then reveal your markings and reasoning.
- Do not yet make the student evaluate your full trade-management sequence. One decision is enough at this stage.
- In Module 6, present two complete case studies in chronological steps. Include at least one failed or invalidated thesis, losing trade or no-trade decision.

### Golden rule: no hindsight before judgement

For exercises and case studies, the student must see the chart as it could reasonably have appeared at the decision point. Do not reveal future candles, the final annotation or the trade outcome before the learner has answered. Sequence the reveal this way:

1. context available at that moment;
2. learner question and response;
3. instructor's observed evidence;
4. instructor's interpretation and alternative scenario;
5. next section of price action;
6. trade/management/outcome, only when that becomes the subject of the lesson;
7. retrospective review.

A retrospective chart can be useful for the explanation, but never use it as the only practice example. Otherwise the learner is unknowingly solving a hindsight puzzle.

### Instructor voice

Calm, specific, transparent, never theatrical. Narrate what is visible, separate it from interpretation, and say when evidence is ambiguous. Avoid “the market wanted,” “smart money hunted stops,” “this level had to hold,” “perfect entry,” and similar statements unless explicitly presented as metaphor and immediately qualified. Prefer: “Price moved above the area, but follow-through was limited”; “That weakens my first scenario”; “Here are two interpretations I considered”; “I would not know the outcome yet.”

## 4. Course map

**Module 1 - Understand (Lessons 1-2):** Understand zones and read structure. Start with clean static examples; do not use personal trades yet.

**Module 2 - Find (Lessons 3-5):** Select meaningful turning points and areas. Use controlled drills, then introduce ChartLab Study 1, Clear W, after the concept of zones and prioritisation is understood.

**Module 3 - Draw and evaluate (Lessons 6-8):** Draw defensible boundaries and reduce clutter. Use progressive demonstrations and ChartLab Study 2, Uneven Lows.

**Module 4 - Read reactions (Lessons 9-11):** Interpret the approach, rejection, acceptance and changing evidence. Introduce one short personal-chart micro-case only after the learner has completed controlled drawing practice.

**Module 5 - Breakouts and failures (Lessons 12-14):** Interpret breaks, retests and failed attempts. Use controlled candle sequences, then ChartLab Study 3, Wider Neckline, and a suitable short personal example if it adds value.

**Module 6 - Apply and review (Lessons 15-18):** Build conditional scenarios and review real decisions. Use full personal case studies and finish with an unseen-chart assessment.

# 5. Detailed lesson plans

Each plan separates student-facing content from instructor notes. The “say/show/ask/reveal” instructions are production guidance, not text that should all be placed on the page at once.

## Module 1 — Understand what you are looking at

### Lesson 1 — What support and resistance actually tell you

**Estimated time:** 12–15 minutes  
**Preceding knowledge:** Basic familiarity with a candlestick chart; no prior support/resistance expertise assumed.  
**Learning objective:** Explain what a support/resistance zone describes and why it is an area of interest rather than a guaranteed barrier or trade signal.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. **Open with an unlabelled chart.** Show a clean historical excerpt where price has reacted in roughly similar areas more than once. Do not draw lines, name the asset, or show a future outcome. Ask: *“Before we label anything, what stands out about where price has paused, turned or moved away?”*
2. **Let the learner observe first.** Offer “I see a repeated area,” “I see a trend,” “I am not sure yet,” or a short free-text response. Do not grade intuition at this stage.
3. **Define the terms in plain language.** Support is an area where falling price has previously found enough response to stop or reverse for a time. Resistance is an area where rising price has previously met enough opposing response to pause or reverse. These are descriptions of price behaviour, not guarantees about what happens next.
4. **Reveal the broad zones, not precision lines.** Explain that the chart records price, not the full reason for every move. We can observe reactions; we cannot identify every participant's intention from a candle alone.
5. **Show a second counterexample.** Price crosses a previously respected zone. Ask: *“Does that mean the original marking was useless, or does it mean the area did not prevent this move?”* Explain that a zone can be useful for organising scenarios even when it fails.
6. **End with one sentence:** “A zone is an area of interest, not a prediction.”

**Chart and asset brief**
- Chart A: simple, two or three visible reactions in a clear area.
- Chart B: a comparable area later crossed decisively.
- Keep only candles and unobtrusive axes; no indicators, entry arrows, targets or stop markers.
- If schematic, label it “Illustrative chart”; if actual market data, record instrument/timeframe/source in the production notes.

**Practice and answer key**
- Question: “Which of these two charts contains a prior area worth watching, and what visible evidence supports your choice?”
- Full-credit reasoning identifies observable reactions/pauses or structure and avoids saying the zone must hold.
- Accept uncertainty where the excerpt is insufficient.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Murphy introduces the conventional framework; Grimes provides the essential caveat that many commonly drawn levels may have little value and that price action is complex.
- Optional “Behind the idea” drawer can mention Osler's FX-specific study, but do not lead with research jargon or numbers for beginners. The student first needs the concept. If mentioned, state the research scope and the variation in effectiveness by firm/currency.

**Website implications**
- One chart, one short prompt, one reveal state and a visible takeaway.
- Avoid a dense text article. Use progressive annotations or an image sequence.
- No course trading rules, entries or setup indicators here.

**Do not teach yet:** precise boundaries, multi-timeframe workflow, breakout confirmation, entries or stop placement. These are later concepts.

### Lesson 2 — Read structure before drawing

**Estimated time:** 15 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Identify obvious swing highs/lows and distinguish a trend, a trading range and a transition without adding support/resistance zones yet.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Show a clean chart with no markings. Ask: *“Is price generally advancing, declining, moving sideways, or changing between those behaviours?”*
2. Teach a swing high/low as a relative turning point in the surrounding price sequence, not a magic candle shape with one universal look.
3. Reveal major turns one at a time. Explain that higher relative lows and highs can describe rising structure; lower highs/lows can describe falling structure; repeated boundaries can describe a range. A transition can be ambiguous at the time.
4. Show one chart that starts in a trend and enters a range. Ask learners to identify where their interpretation changes and what evidence is visible.
5. Only after the exercise, connect structure to zones: the turning points help us locate areas that may matter. Do not draw those zones yet.

**Chart set**
- A clear rising structure; a clear falling structure; a sideways range; a trend-to-range transition.
- Reveal the swing labels only after the first response.
- Keep examples comparable in visual size and candle density.

**Practice**
- Learner labels 3–5 major swing points on a chart and chooses one of four descriptions: trend up, trend down, range, or transition/unclear.
- Answer key explains the chosen structure and notes plausible alternate reads when the chart is genuinely ambiguous. Do not mark every small candle fluctuation as a major swing.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Schwager and Etzkorn's Chapter 7 introduces ranges as corridors with upper and lower boundaries. Grimes's Chapters 3–5 frame trends, ranges and their interfaces as distinct structures.
- The aim is not to teach a full trend-following system; structure is a prerequisite skill.

**Website implications**
- Allow marks or taps on a static chart; keep exercise completion separate from a final quiz score.
- Add a “Show structure” control after the learner tries.

**Do not teach yet:** how to pick the best support/resistance zones, what a breakout means, or how a structure generates a trade.

## Module 2 — Find the important areas

### Lesson 3 — Identify meaningful swing points

**Estimated time:** 15 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Rank visible turning points by structural relevance and explain why minor fluctuations should not all become zones.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Revisit a chart from Lesson 2 with several possible turning points.
2. Ask: *“Which three turning points would you keep if you had to explain this chart to another trader in 30 seconds?”*
3. Teach three practical filters: the point stands out relative to surrounding movement; it relates to a larger piece of structure (such as a range boundary or major reversal); and it matters to current price or the scenario being studied.
4. Show why “largest candle” or “most recent point” alone is not a reliable selection rule.
5. Reveal an intentionally over-marked version, then simplify to the principal points and explain each deletion.

**Chart/asset brief**
- One chart with 8–10 candidate turns, of which only 3–5 are central to the current read.
- Include one difficult candidate so learners learn that relevance is contextual, not binary.

**Practice and key**
- Learner chooses up to four meaningful turns and writes one sentence for each.
- Full credit: uses relative structure and relevance. Partial credit: plausible turn with weak explanation. Incorrect: marks every local candle or claims a point matters solely because it later reversed.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Murphy and Schwager/Etzkorn support using prior major highs/lows. Grimes cautions against taking conventional chart marks as inherently predictive.
- We intentionally do not create an arbitrary 1–10 “strength score.”

**Website implications**
- Click-to-mark or simple selection interaction; allow “I am uncertain” as a legitimate response.
- Do not reveal a numerical rank, badge or “strength score.”

### Lesson 4 — Zones, not perfect lines

**Estimated time:** 15 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Draw a zone with defensible upper/lower boundaries and explain the role of wicks, candle bodies and closes.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Show several reactions that are near—but not exactly at—the same price.
2. Ask: *“Would one thin line describe all of these reactions fairly?”*
3. Explain that a zone represents an area in which related reactions occurred. A useful boundary captures the relevant price behaviour without making the area so wide that it loses meaning.
4. Demonstrate the three common references: wick extremes, candle bodies/closes, and a broader area containing several related reactions. Do not decree that one convention is always correct; explain why it is selected for this chart.
5. Compare an overly narrow line, a defensible zone and an overly wide band. Explain the trade-off: too narrow may ignore normal variation; too broad may provide little actionable information.
6. Ask the learner to defend the boundaries before showing the reference version.

**Chart/asset brief**
- Use a crop where wick/body differences are visible.
- Prepare three overlays: too narrow, defensible and too broad.
- Add an instructor note explaining why the preferred zone includes/excludes specific reactions.

**Practice and key**
- Mark a zone and state: “I used ___ as the main reference because ___.”
- Accept different boundaries if the learner's convention is consistent and captures the same structural area.
- Do not mark an exact numeric match as the only correct answer.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Murphy and Schwager/Etzkorn provide the conventional support/resistance frame. Our focus on zones is a teaching choice that accommodates market variation; state that boundaries are interpretive rather than an objectively exact physical boundary.

**Website implications**
- Provide a static stepwise reveal before the full drawing task.
- Any drawing tolerance must permit reasonable alternatives; feedback should compare reasoning and coverage, not make a narrow pixel box the sole pass/fail rule.

### Lesson 5 — Choose the right areas and reduce clutter

**Estimated time:** 18 minutes, including the first ChartLab study  
**Learning objective:** Prioritise zones using structure and context; deliberately leave minor or redundant areas unmarked.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Show a chart containing several plausible zones. Ask: *“If every area is important, what useful decision does the chart help you make?”*
2. Teach a selection hierarchy: large structural turns/range extremes first; nearby minor areas only when they help answer the current question; higher-timeframe context may matter, but should not be imported without purpose.
3. Introduce chart clutter as a reasoning problem, not just a design problem. Too many zones let a trader explain nearly any outcome after the fact.
4. Give a short worked example. Explain why two nearby areas are combined, why one is retained and why another is omitted.
5. **Launch ChartLab Study 1: Clear W.** Student reads the instructions, marks support, submits and sees that task step lock in; next marks resistance and submits. Only after both submissions does the comparison reveal the reference zones and tailored reasoning. Then a short two-question knowledge check and completion action.
6. Debrief: the goal is not identical drawings; it is a small number of defensible zones.

**Chart/asset brief**
- A cluttered example with at least one redundant area.
- ChartLab Clear W reference overlay and short rationale for both boundaries.
- The chart outcome after the zone is drawn is not revealed until the learner submits.

**Practice and key**
- Learner keeps a maximum of 3–4 areas on the chart and explains what was left out.
- Full credit: prioritises structural relevance and current context; minimises clutter.
- If the chart is ambiguous, the answer key should say so rather than manufacture a single perfect selection.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Schwager/Etzkorn support major highs/lows, range boundaries and concentrations of highs/lows. Grimes is the corrective: levels should be questioned, not accumulated.
- This is the first full ChartLab. Keep earlier lessons controlled and light; do not begin the course with an interactive drawing tool before the concept has been explained.

**Website implications**
- ChartLab should be launched from this lesson step, then return to the same learning-room context.
- Learner attempt → locked next step → second attempt → reference comparison → feedback → 2-question check → completion.
- Preserve the lesson's progress when they navigate away. In later implementation, store progress and attempts to the signed-in account across devices.

## Module 3 — Draw and evaluate zones

### Lesson 6 — Set defensible boundaries

**Estimated time:** 18 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Apply a consistent boundary method and explain how the selected area captures the relevant reactions.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Review the difference between identifying a swing and boxing a zone.
2. Demonstrate boundary selection step by step: begin with the structural turn; examine the related cluster; compare wick extremes and bodies/closes; test whether the band includes the reactions being explained; check that it is not so broad it swallows unrelated movement.
3. Show two legitimate drawing conventions on the same chart. Explain the trade-offs. State when you would choose one over the other.
4. Ask the learner to draw independently before showing any reference annotation.
5. Review the result using a short rubric: structural anchor, coherent boundary logic, useful width, and consistency.

**Chart/asset brief**
- One relatively clear support example and one resistance example.
- One deliberately ambiguous area to model how you communicate uncertainty.

**Practice and answer key**
- Written prompt: “What is the structural anchor? Which prices determine your boundaries? What evidence did you choose not to include?”
- Answer key uses a shaded reference band and rationale; include a “reasonable alternative” note if appropriate.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Build on Murphy's conventional framework and Schwager/Etzkorn's zones. The insistence on explaining the boundary choice is our pedagogical standard, not a claimed universal formula from a book.

**Website implications**
- Reference annotations should be hidden by default until submission.
- If exact-drawing comparison is offered, show a permissive “overlap” visual plus written reasoning; do not assign a confidence percentage without a validated basis.

### Lesson 7 — Keep the chart readable

**Estimated time:** 12–15 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Simplify a chart without losing the most relevant structure.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Start with a screenshot covered by many lines/zones. Ask which decisions the markings actually help answer.
2. Teach three editing questions: Is this area anchored in meaningful structure? Is it relevant to the current price/context? Does it add information not already represented by another nearby zone?
3. Demonstrate removing or merging zones one at a time and state the reason for each edit.
4. Explain that leaving an area unmarked is a decision—not a failure to notice it.
5. Ask the learner to simplify another chart. Reveal the instructor's cleaner version and reasoning.

**Practice and key**
- Learner retains no more than four primary areas (guideline for this exercise, not a universal market law).
- Score the explanations, not only the count. If the chart genuinely requires a different number, allow a reasoned exception.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Grimes's critique of over-reliance on conventional levels is central. The exact “four areas” cap is a teaching constraint for the exercise, not a trading rule.

**Website implications**
- Use a before/after comparison; the learner should be able to reveal each removed area with a “why it was removed” note.

### Lesson 8 — Evaluate a zone without inventing a magic score

**Estimated time:** 18 minutes, including ChartLab Study 2  
**Learning objective:** Evaluate contextual usefulness without relying on an automatic touch count or fixed-strength rating.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Present two zones: one with many noisy crossings and another with fewer but clearer structural reactions. Ask which appears more useful and what information is missing.
2. Teach a qualitative review: structural importance; clarity of prior reactions; movement away from the area; relationship to current price and the next area; how much uncertainty remains. None of these factors guarantees a reversal.
3. Explicitly challenge myths: “three touches makes a level strong”; “a level gets stronger every time it is touched”; “a level always weakens after a retest.” Explain that touch count is not sufficient on its own.
4. **Launch ChartLab Study 2: Uneven Lows.** Learner draws support around lows that do not line up exactly, then resistance around the relevant opposing area. Reveal reference zones and the explanation of why imperfect alignment can still be meaningful.
5. Ask: *“What evidence makes this zone useful, and what still remains uncertain?”*

**Practice and key**
- Compare two candidate zones and write a short qualitative rationale rather than a numeric score.
- Full credit references visible structure and context. A claim such as “it was touched four times” earns little credit unless the learner describes how those touches behaved and why the area matters now.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Grimes is the main critical reference; Schwager/Etzkorn provides the conventional zone concept. Maintain the distinction between a descriptive framework and tested predictive performance.

**Website implications**
- Reuse the ChartLab submit-and-compare flow but tailor feedback to uneven lows. Provide “why a different but defensible zone may still work” as a dedicated feedback state.

## Module 4 — Read price behaviour around a zone

### Lesson 9 — Read the approach to a zone

**Estimated time:** 15–18 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Describe price behaviour before it reaches a zone and identify what remains unknown.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Show two charts approaching similar zones: one with steady directional movement, another with overlapping, hesitant movement.
2. Ask: *“The destination is similar. What differs in the path price takes to get there?”*
3. Teach approach characteristics as observations: speed/overlap, sequence of swings, proximity to another area, and broader trend/range context. Explain that these may influence a scenario but do not determine the outcome.
4. Introduce higher-timeframe context lightly: first know the local chart, then check whether a larger structure alters the interpretation. Do not throw multiple timeframes at the learner as extra complexity before the base chart is understood.
5. **First short instructor-chart micro-case.** Show your own chart cropped to the point before the zone interaction. Hide your markings, entry and outcome. Ask learners to describe the approach and name one uncertainty. Then reveal your zone and explain why you chose it. Stop before discussing the trade itself.

**Chart/asset brief**
- Controlled side-by-side pair plus one actual instructor chart with result masked.
- Record instrument/timeframe and the screenshot timestamp in production notes.

**Practice and key**
- Learner chooses the better description of each approach and writes one possible implication plus one limitation.
- There is no “the fast approach must break” or “slow approach must bounce” answer. Score whether the interpretation is observational and conditional.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Grimes's market-structure chapters; Shannon's multiple-timeframe approach is optional further reading. Avoid making multi-timeframe alignment a mandatory formula.

**Website implications**
- Put the instructor-chart micro-case after the lesson explanation and controlled practice, not in the course introduction.
- Use click-to-reveal annotations and do not expose later candles in the first view.

### Lesson 10 — Rejection versus acceptance

**Estimated time:** 18 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Interpret price behaviour around a zone using the sequence of candles, closes and follow-through, without treating one wick or candle as decisive.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Show a short sequence entering a zone. Ask students what they can say now—and what they cannot say yet.
2. Teach the language: **rejection evidence** (price tests an area and moves away); **acceptance evidence** (price spends time or establishes action beyond an area); **inconclusive** (not enough follow-through or conflicting evidence). Define these as descriptive terms, not absolute mechanical rules.
3. Reveal one candle at a time. First show a wick through the zone; ask whether it proves acceptance. Then show the close. Then reveal subsequent candles. Let the learner update the answer as evidence changes.
4. Compare a strong-looking wick with no follow-through against a less dramatic close followed by persistent action beyond the band.
5. Return briefly to the Lesson 9 personal-chart micro-case if useful, but do not reveal your entry or result.

**Practice and key**
- Classify four sequences as rejection evidence, possible acceptance, inconclusive, or evolving/unclear; give one sentence supporting each classification.
- The key must show what is known at that point, not use candles that occur later in the sequence.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Grimes's warning about complex price action and uncertain transitions matters. We are using “acceptance/rejection” as a disciplined descriptive vocabulary, not asserting universal definitions backed by all markets.

**Website implications**
- Candle-by-candle reveal with a “my current read” selection that can be changed when the next candle appears.
- Make the provisional nature of an answer explicit; do not penalise students for updating after new evidence.

### Lesson 11 — How a zone's significance changes

**Estimated time:** 18 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Update a zone interpretation as new interactions appear instead of treating the original mark as permanent truth.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Start with a zone after the first reaction. Ask learners to describe what the chart supports at that time.
2. Reveal a later revisit, then another move away, then a deeper penetration or break. After each stage, ask what changed.
3. Teach a review framework: what was the original evidence; what new evidence arrived; did the original scenario strengthen, weaken or become inconclusive; what needs to be redrawn or discarded?
4. Explain why repeated tests are contextual. The number of tests alone does not determine outcome; how price behaves and the wider context matter.
5. Show a final chart, but explain that the later outcome must not be used to pretend it was obvious from the beginning.

**Practice and key**
- Students fill a three-row “evidence log”: first contact, subsequent revisit, later break/hold. At each step they choose strengthen/weaken/unclear and write why.
- Award credit for updating a view when evidence changes, even if the final move later contradicts the student's early scenario.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Grimes Chapters 4–5; Schwager/Etzkorn's range and boundary role concepts. Avoid “every touch weakens a zone” and “each touch validates it.”

**Website implications**
- Timeline or stepper is better than a full final chart shown at once.
- The learner's previous answer can remain visible for comparison to show how a sound decision evolves.

## Module 5 — Breakouts, retests and failed attempts

### Lesson 12 — Breakout or temporary penetration?

**Estimated time:** 18 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Distinguish a price movement outside a zone from stronger evidence that price is holding beyond it.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Show price briefly moving through a zone. Ask, *“What would you need to observe before calling this a meaningful break?”*
2. State that crossing the boundary is an observation; a breakout thesis is an interpretation. Explain possible evidence: close beyond the area, follow-through, whether price returns quickly, and broader structure. No single item guarantees continuation.
3. Reveal three sequence examples: quick penetration/reversal, move beyond with follow-through, and conflicting evidence. Ask students to update the label one candle at a time.
4. Explain that widely watched levels and clustered orders may be one possible reason price can accelerate near/through some areas, but this is not directly observable from a single chart. If mentioning Osler, explain that her studies concern specific FX settings.

**Chart/asset brief**
- Three chart sequences that differ in follow-through, not simply candle colour.
- Include at least one breakout that continues and one that fails, with outcomes hidden during first decisions.

**Practice and key**
- Learner writes: “Current evidence suggests ___; I would reconsider if ___.”
- Full credit uses conditional language and identifies what remains unresolved.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Grimes Chapter 5 covers interfaces between trends and ranges and the prevalence of failed breakouts in the author's treatment. Osler gives bounded FX empirical context, not a universal breakout rule.

**Website implications**
- Stepwise chart reveal; “break / not break” binary quiz alone is too crude.
- Keep a visible “inconclusive” option.

### Lesson 13 — Retests and role reversal

**Estimated time:** 15–18 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Explain the possible role reversal of a broken area and assess a return without assuming it will hold.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Show a range and its boundary without revealing a later breakout. Mark the upper boundary only after students identify it.
2. Reveal the break and ask what changed in the chart structure. Do not yet show a retest.
3. Teach the convention: after an upside breakout, former resistance may act as support; after a downside breakout, former support may act as resistance. Use “may,” not “will.”
4. Reveal two outcomes: one retest that holds and one where price re-enters the prior range. Compare their evidence.
5. Ask what makes the retest interpretable and what would weaken that interpretation.

**Practice and key**
- Learner sketches two scenarios after a breakout: a potential retest that holds and an invalidation/failure path.
- Grade the logic and conditional statement; do not grade whether their most likely scenario matches the eventual candle path.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Schwager/Etzkorn Chapter 8 explicitly covers the possible change in role of a broken trading-range boundary. The word “possible” is essential.

**Website implications**
- Reveal the chart in stages; keep the retest path unknown until learner has stated both scenarios.

### Lesson 14 — Failed breakouts and alternate outcomes

**Estimated time:** 18–20 minutes, including ChartLab Study 3  
**Learning objective:** Recognise when a breakout interpretation is challenged and formulate an alternative scenario without inventing certainty.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Begin with a chart where price is approaching a clearly marked boundary. Ask learners to mark the relevant zone and say what would count as a convincing break.
2. Reveal a move beyond the area, but not the next candles. Ask for a provisional interpretation.
3. Reveal a return into the prior range. Ask what has changed and what remains unknown.
4. Teach a failed breakout as a sequence whose later behaviour contradicts the continuation thesis—not something that can always be labelled with certainty at the first tick beyond a zone.
5. **Launch ChartLab Study 3: Wider Neckline.** Students mark support and resistance areas, compare with a reference, read the reasoned explanation, then answer the short check.
6. Close with: “A failed idea is not automatically a failed process. Review whether the original decision followed the evidence available then, and whether you responded properly when evidence changed.”

**Practice and key**
- Learner completes “initial thesis / evidence that supported it / evidence that challenged it / updated scenario.”
- Key should distinguish evidence visible at each moment. Avoid hindsight phrases such as “it was obvious this would fail.”

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Grimes Chapters 5–6 on trend/range transitions and practical templates; Bulkowski can be optional reading for pattern failure statistics, but do not pivot this lesson into an encyclopaedia of formations.

**Website implications**
- Use the third ChartLab study after the explanatory sequence.
- Provide an explicit provisional-versus-updated answer view; learner should be allowed to revise the answer.

## Module 6 — From zones to a complete trade review

### Lesson 15 — From a zone to conditional scenarios

**Estimated time:** 18–20 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Build a conditional plan around a zone, identify invalidation and consider whether there is sufficient room to the next opposing area.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Present a chart with two zones and current price in between. Ask what decisions the chart supports before discussing any entry.
2. Teach a simple scenario template:
   - Context: what structure is visible?
   - Area: which zone matters now, and why?
   - Condition: what must price do for this scenario to become more plausible?
   - Invalidation: what evidence would undermine the thesis?
   - Space: where is the next opposing area, and does it leave meaningful room?
   - Alternative: what if the first scenario does not occur?
   - Stand aside: what makes this chart too ambiguous or too compressed to trade?
3. Explain that a zone alone is not an entry trigger. Entry methods and risk sizing are strategy-specific and outside the scope of a universal S/R rule. The case study can show the instructor's own method, clearly labelled as personal application.
4. Complete one example with learners and leave a second chart for independent work.

**Practice and key**
- Fill a one-page “S/R Scenario Card.” Answers must use if/then phrasing and a clear invalidation condition.
- Correct answer is not a trade call. A well-reasoned “no trade yet” is acceptable.

**Instructor notes / sources**
- Schwager/Etzkorn's chapter on setting objectives discusses S/R among chart-based reference methods for objectives; Grimes offers trade templates/management topics. We should treat zones as context for planning, not automatically as a profit target.

**Website implications**
- A downloadable or in-browser worksheet; do not require a course buyer to share a real open trade.
- Allow the learner to save or export their own notes later in the member-area roadmap.

### Lesson 16 — Case study: a real trade built around a reaction

**Estimated time:** 20–25 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Trace the instructor's actual decision process from context and zone selection to entry, risk, management, exit and review.

**Important prerequisite:** Choose the actual chart only once the lesson architecture is approved. Do not invent a trade. Prefer a case with a clear audit trail of screenshots or notes from the time of the trade.

**Student-facing sequence and exact reveal order**

1. **Pre-trade context only.** Show the instrument and timeframe if relevant, the candles available at the time and any higher-timeframe view you truly used. Hide all annotations, entry, stop, target and later candles.
2. Ask learners to identify structure and one or two candidate zones. Require a short explanation before proceeding.
3. **Your zone.** Reveal your marking, then explain why you chose those boundaries, what alternatives you considered and which areas you ignored. Do not say the student was wrong merely because their zone differs.
4. **Your thesis.** State your conditional expectation and at least one alternative outcome. Clarify what you did not know at that point.
5. **Your trigger and entry.** Reveal the evidence you required and why the timing mattered to your method. If other indicators or timeframes were part of the decision, explain only the necessary context; do not let them obscure the lesson's S/R objective.
6. **Risk and invalidation.** Reveal the planned invalidation/stop and how it related to structure. Explain risk in process terms; do not imply the location was universally optimal.
7. **Management.** Reveal subsequent events in chronological chunks. Ask learners what has changed before disclosing what you did.
8. **Exit and result.** Reveal actual outcome, then discuss whether execution followed the plan.
9. **Review.** Separate zone-reading quality, trade construction, risk management, execution and outcome. Ask: “What would I retain or change next time?”

**What to say**
- Say what you observed and what you inferred, separately.
- If you used a discretionary judgement, call it judgement; do not retrofit it as a mechanical rule.
- State whether the screenshot is contemporaneous or reconstructed later from historical data.
- If the trade lost, do not apologise for using it. If it won, do not treat the result as proof the method works.

**Assets to gather from the instructor**
- Original chart before entry, if available.
- Entry-time screenshot or clear written reconstruction.
- One or more management screenshots.
- Closing/final chart.
- Original thesis, planned invalidation, actual exit reason and brief retrospective notes.
- If no pre-trade screenshot exists, mark the reconstruction clearly and avoid claiming that every annotation was known at the time.

**Practice and key**
- Before each reveal, learner fills a short decision prompt. At the end they compare decisions and write two points they agree with and one difference they can defend.
- Assessment focuses on reasoning, not copying the instructor's trade or matching the outcome.

**Website implications**
- Case study needs controlled reveal gates and a timeline; a static final screenshot alone is insufficient.
- Keep internal production notes separate from public lesson copy.

### Lesson 17 — Case study: a failed thesis, losing trade or decision not to trade

**Estimated time:** 20–25 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Identify when evidence changes, evaluate invalidation and distinguish a losing outcome from poor decision quality.

**Case selection:** Choose one of the following, preferably a trade the instructor genuinely took: (a) the zone was breached and the thesis failed; (b) a breakout failed; (c) price offered no valid trigger and the instructor stayed out; or (d) the trade was exited early for a defensible reason. A losing example is not mandatory if a truthful no-trade example demonstrates the intended skill better.

**Student-facing sequence**

1. Show the pre-decision chart with no result.
2. Ask the learner to identify the zone, the original scenario and what would invalidate it.
3. Reveal the instructor's original rationale.
4. Reveal the first evidence that challenged the thesis. Ask the learner what action or re-assessment is reasonable under the instructor's stated plan.
5. Reveal the actual response and the rest of the trade, in chronological order.
6. Review: Was the original analysis defensible from the evidence then? Was the risk decision coherent? Was new evidence respected? Was the process followed? What was just outcome variance?

**Instructor guidance**
- Be specific about any genuine mistake. Avoid turning every loss into a flattering “lesson” if the decision was simply poor.
- Do not cherry-pick a dramatic failure to claim the method is robust. Explain what this single case can and cannot show.
- If the actual decision changed during the trade, show that candidly rather than making the story artificially tidy.

**Practice and key**
- Learner writes a post-mortem with: thesis, planned invalidation, evidence that changed, response, process grade and one improvement.
- A sound process can still lose. A profitable outcome can still come from a flawed process.

**Website implications**
- Provide a guided review template with free-text entries; any permanent account storage should be a later product requirement, not promised until implemented.

### Lesson 18 — Final chart review and knowledge check

**Estimated time:** 30–35 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Independently apply the full support/resistance method to a new chart and defend the decisions made.

**Assessment setup**
- Use an unfamiliar chart not used in lessons or ChartLab.
- Include enough history for structure, but no pre-drawn zones, entry arrows, indicators or future outcome.
- The chart should contain some meaningful areas and some distracting minor turns; it must also allow a defensible conclusion that a particular zone is ambiguous or that no trade is justified.
- Do not use the instructor's actual trade chart as the only final assessment. Students already know those answers.

**Student-facing task sequence**

1. Describe the broader structure (trend, range, transition or unclear).
2. Mark the most relevant support/resistance zones; state what was intentionally left unmarked.
3. Explain the boundary logic for each chosen zone.
4. Interpret the current approach or reaction, distinguishing observation from inference.
5. Provide two conditional scenarios or one scenario plus a justified stand-aside decision.
6. Identify invalidation evidence and the next opposing zone/available space.
7. Complete a 6–8 question knowledge check.
8. Only after submission, reveal the reference analysis, rationale and reasonable alternative interpretations.

**Suggested scoring rubric (100 points)**

- **Structure reading - 15 points:** Major swings/range/trend described without over-labelling noise.
- **Zone selection - 20 points:** Relevant areas anchored to visible structure; clutter avoided.
- **Boundary rationale - 15 points:** Width and placement are consistent and explained.
- **Interpretation of behaviour - 15 points:** Does not treat one wick/close as a guarantee; updates as evidence changes.
- **Conditional scenarios - 15 points:** Uses clear if/then conditions and an alternative path.
- **Invalidation and space - 10 points:** States what challenges the thesis and considers the nearby opposing area.
- **Reasoning and uncertainty - 10 points:** Separates facts from interpretation and acknowledges ambiguity.

**Proposed completion standard:** 70/100 overall, with no zero in the zone-selection or conditional-reasoning components. This is a proposed instructional threshold; test the task before finalising it. Let learners review feedback and retry a parallel chart rather than simply repeating the exact same chart.

**Question bank examples**
- What does a support/resistance zone tell you—and what does it not tell you?
- Why can two slightly different zone boundaries both be defensible?
- Why is touch count alone insufficient to rank zones?
- Does a wick through a zone prove a breakout? Explain.
- What is role reversal, and why is it not guaranteed?
- What should you write as invalidation for a conditional scenario?

**Answer-key policy**
- Provide a reference chart and written rationale, plus accepted alternatives.
- Reward explanatory evidence; do not over-reward visual similarity to the teacher's bands.
- If the chart has more than one defensible interpretation, explicitly acknowledge it.

**Website implications**
- Assessment response, chart markings, quiz answers and completion status should be separate data concepts in the later member-area design.
- Completion must only be recorded after the learner submits the required work and knowledge check; simply opening the lesson should not count as finishing it.

## 6. The instructor's real-trade case study intake brief

Do not start by collecting dozens of screenshots. First select two contrasting, well-documented examples that teach different decisions. One should demonstrate a reasonable reaction-based thesis; the other should demonstrate invalidation, a failed move or a decision not to trade.

For each candidate trade, record:

1. Instrument/market and timeframe(s) actually used.
2. Date/session and a note of whether screenshots were captured contemporaneously or reconstructed later.
3. What structure you saw before entry.
4. Which zone(s) mattered and why those boundaries were selected.
5. What you expected price to do, stated conditionally.
6. What evidence triggered entry, if any.
7. Planned invalidation/stop and rationale; position size/risk can be discussed at a high level if appropriate.
8. What happened next in time order.
9. What management decisions you made and what changed your view.
10. Actual exit and outcome.
11. What you believe was done well, what was a mistake and what remains uncertain.
12. Which screenshots can be shown publicly and what should be masked (account IDs, broker balances, private data, identifying account details).

**Case-study quality rule:** If the record does not show what was known before entry, we can still teach from it, but must label the pre-trade sequence as a reconstruction rather than represent it as a contemporaneous capture. No invented rationale should be added to make the story look consistent.

## 7. Website and learning-room requirements for simultaneous design

These are content/design requirements, not instructions to code them now.

### A. Public course discovery page

- The public course card and preview should help the visitor explore the curriculum without exposing paid lesson content.
- Preview contents: course promise, learning outcomes, module/lesson titles, format, estimated workload, intended learner, instructor approach and brief risk disclaimer.
- Do not publish the full worked answers, ChartLab reference overlays, private trade cases or final assessment chart in the public preview.
- Prefer a readable editorial outline over a wall of pricing cards. Let a prospective student understand the skill being taught before asking them to buy.

### B. Private learning room

- Separate learning room from the public preview.
- Persistent module/lesson navigation rail on desktop; collapsible or drawer navigation on mobile.
- Main canvas for narrative, chart and the current activity. Do not let navigation occupy more width than the learning content needs.
- Show course progress and current lesson clearly without making completion badges the main event.
- One primary action per step (“Try the chart”, “Reveal the reasoning”, “Continue”) rather than several competing buttons.
- Keep student-facing content distinct from instructor-only production notes and source notes.

### C. Progressive-reveal interaction contract

For every chart activity, define these states before design begins:

1. untouched chart;
2. learner has marked/answered;
3. submission accepted;
4. next step unlocked;
5. reference analysis visible;
6. tailored explanation visible;
7. knowledge check complete;
8. lesson activity complete.

A user should not lose the completed first drawing when proceeding to the next step. Feedback must not imply that an alternate but defensible boundary is automatically wrong.

### D. ChartLab behaviour

- Clear W: introductory support-then-resistance task.
- Uneven Lows: boundary judgement under imperfect alignment.
- Wider Neckline: broader structural zone and subsequent interpretation.
- Each lab has a clear learning objective, instructions, keyboard/mobile usability, a submission action, a reference overlay, explanatory feedback and a short check.
- Students see their work beside the reference, not merely a green/red grade.
- The course text must prepare each activity; don't drop the learner into ChartLab without explaining the concept.

### E. Progress and account requirements for later implementation

The product design should eventually account for real learner activity, not just opened pages. Model these separately:
- course enrolment/purchase access;
- lesson started/completed;
- quiz attempt and result;
- ChartLab attempt/submission;
- reference/feedback viewed;
- saved worksheet or takeaway if supported.

Progress should be associated with the authenticated account and persist across devices once implemented. Until this exists and is tested, do not promise cross-device persistence in public copy. Avoid defining “complete” as merely visiting a route.

### F. Accessibility and low-friction learning

- Text/labels must remain readable on mobile without horizontal scrolling.
- Chart interactions need a non-drag alternative where possible (numeric inputs, keyboard-accessible controls or selecting boundaries) and clear instructions.
- Ensure each chart has alt text or an adjacent textual description for core meaning.
- Colour must not be the only way to distinguish support/resistance or correct/alternative reasoning.
- Preserve visible feedback, allow changing an answer when fresh evidence appears, and do not punish provisional judgement.

## 9. Glossary and wording standards

Use these terms consistently throughout the site, worksheet and ChartLab feedback.

- **Support area/zone:** A price area where prior price behaviour suggests falling price previously paused or turned. It may fail.
- **Resistance area/zone:** A price area where prior price behaviour suggests rising price previously paused or turned. It may fail.
- **Swing high/low:** A relative turning point in the surrounding price sequence; its importance depends on structure and context.
- **Trading range:** An area of relatively sideways price movement with discernible upper/lower boundaries. Boundaries need not be perfectly horizontal.
- **Rejection evidence:** Price tests or moves into an area and then moves away. This is descriptive, not a guarantee of reversal.
- **Acceptance evidence:** Price action that suggests the market is holding or building activity beyond an area. No single candle must be treated as definitive.
- **Breakout:** Movement beyond a previously relevant boundary; the learner still evaluates follow-through and whether the move holds.
- **Retest:** A return toward a previously broken area. It can hold, fail or be inconclusive.
- **Role reversal:** The possibility that former resistance behaves as support after an upside break, or former support behaves as resistance after a downside break.
- **Invalidation:** Evidence that undermines a specified scenario. It should be stated before the outcome whenever possible.
- **Conditional scenario:** An if/then description of what the learner will watch for, what would support the thesis and what would undermine it.
- **Ambiguous/unclear:** A legitimate interpretation when the chart does not support a sufficiently clear conclusion.

### Preferred language

Say: “The evidence supports…”, “One possible reading is…”, “At this point I do not know…”, “If price does X, I would reconsider…”, “This zone is useful as context, but it is not a guarantee.”

Avoid: “This level must hold”, “institutions are defending this price” without evidence, “three touches guarantees strength”, “perfect setup”, “confirmed reversal” based only on one candle, and hindsight claims that a failure was obvious before it occurred.

## 10. Quality gates before publishing a lesson

A lesson is ready only when these questions can be answered.

### Instructional quality
- Is the learning objective one observable skill rather than a vague promise?
- Does the learner see the problem before the explanation?
- Does the explanation state what can be observed and what is interpretation?
- Does the activity test the stated skill without requiring an unannounced concept?
- Does the answer key explain reasoning and acknowledge reasonable alternatives?
- Is at least one common misconception addressed?
- Is the takeaway memorable and specific?

### Chart quality
- Is every chart real historical data or clearly labelled schematic?
- Is the relevant context visible without irrelevant clutter?
- Are future candles/outcomes hidden until the learner has made the decision?
- Is the reference annotation defensible and explained?
- Are asset, timeframe and source documented where applicable?
- Has private/account-identifying information been removed from instructor charts?

### Website quality
- Does the lesson fit the learning-room layout on mobile and desktop?
- Are the controls obvious and keyboard-accessible?
- Does the student's completed work persist during the activity?
- Is completion state clearly different from simply opening a page?
- Are student-facing text and internal notes separated?
- Is the public course preview protected from revealing full lesson content?

### Ethics and truthfulness
- No guarantee language, cherry-picked success narrative or false universal rule.
- No implication that a good trade must win or a bad trade must lose.
- No unsupported claim about institutional intent.
- No book text or diagrams copied without permission.
- No promise of progress persistence or export until implemented and tested.
