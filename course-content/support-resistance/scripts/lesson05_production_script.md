## Shared production rules for this lesson

- Use purpose-built schematic charts with fictional prices. Label them unobtrusively: *“Illustrative chart — not historical market data.”* Do not invent or imply a real instrument, timeframe or instructor trade.
- Keep the learner's first view free of reference annotations. Let them make a choice before revealing the intended reading.
- Lesson 5 is the first ChartLab activity. It should feel like the next step after the concept lesson, not a sudden jump into an unexplained drawing tool.
- A small number of zones is a reasoning choice, not a universal numeric law. The “3–4 areas” limit applies to the exercise and should not be presented as a rule for every chart.
- Do not create a numeric zone-strength score, rank zones as objectively predictive, or say that touch count guarantees quality.
- Do not add entries, stops, targets, trade direction, a signal, or an outcome reveal to the prioritisation exercise.
- Keep instructor notes separate from text intended for learners. Only copy marked **On screen** or **Voiceover** into the lesson itself.
- Do not use personal trade charts yet. The first instructor-chart micro-case remains scheduled for Lesson 9 or 10, once the learner has practised controlled examples.

## 1. Instructional decision

Lessons 1–4 established what a zone describes, how to read the shape of price movement, how to select meaningful turns, and why a zone can be wider than one exact price. Lesson 5 adds the next judgement: **which areas deserve to remain on the chart, and which areas can be combined or left out?**

A chart can contain many plausible places to draw a zone. Marking every candidate is not necessarily more careful. It can obscure the larger structure, make unrelated price movements appear connected, and allow almost any later move to be explained after the fact.

Teach a practical selection hierarchy, not a mechanical algorithm:

1. **Start with the larger structure.** Look first at major turning points and the broad extremes of a recognisable trading range.
2. **Check whether nearby areas tell the same story.** If two candidate zones substantially overlap or describe the same cluster of reactions, consider representing them as one area rather than drawing duplicates.
3. **Add a smaller area only when it contributes to the question at hand.** A minor pause can matter in context, but it does not deserve a zone simply because it is visible.
4. **Use higher-timeframe context with a purpose.** It may help explain the chart being examined, but do not import extra levels merely because another timeframe contains them. Context should clarify the current reading, not overwhelm it.

These are prompts for disciplined interpretation. They are not a zone-strength scoring model, a guarantee of future price behaviour, or a rule that every chart must contain a fixed number of zones.

**Design principle:** The learner should finish with fewer, better-explained areas—not with the belief that fewer lines are automatically correct.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

1. Explain why marking every plausible area can make a chart less useful.
2. Prioritise large structural turns and broad range extremes before considering smaller areas.
3. Recognise when nearby candidate zones may be redundant and can be considered together.
4. Explain why a minor area is being retained or omitted in relation to the current chart-reading question.
5. Complete ChartLab Study 1, *Clear W*, by marking a support area and a resistance area, then compare the attempt with a reasoned reference example.

**Not a learning outcome yet:** a numeric zone-strength score, a universal maximum number of zones, a multi-timeframe trading system, an entry/stop/target, or a decision to take a trade.

## 3. Student-facing lesson script and reveal timing

The following is the proposed learner-facing copy. The chart states and production notes are instructions, not text to place on screen verbatim unless marked as on-screen copy.

### 0:00–0:45 — Welcome and set the task

**On screen — title:**

> Choose the right areas and reduce clutter

**On screen — opening copy:**

> We now know what a zone describes and why its boundaries may vary. The next question is what deserves a place on the chart.
>
> A useful chart does not need to mark every possible area. It needs to make the important structure easier to read.

**Production cue:** Go directly to the chart after the opening copy. Do not introduce a long list of rules before the learner has seen the problem.

### 0:45–2:30 — Chart A: when every area is marked

**Show:** L05-A in its initial state: a controlled schematic chart with visible structure and several candidate areas. The chart should contain a major swing/range extreme, a nearby cluster of reactions that could be represented by overlapping zones, a smaller mid-structure pause and at least one less relevant local fluctuation. Present the cluttered candidate overlay as the current chart being examined, but do not identify which areas should stay.

**On screen — prompt:**

> If every area is important, what useful decision does the chart help you make?

**Options:**
- It becomes harder to tell which areas matter most.
- More marked areas always make the reading more accurate.
- Each marked area should be treated as equally important.
- I am not sure yet.

**After response — feedback:**

> The problem is not that every mark is necessarily wrong. The problem is that the chart no longer helps us distinguish the areas that explain the larger structure from the ones that add little.

**Reveal rule:** Do not immediately erase the zones. First let the learner notice the problem of competing marks; the worked example will show how to simplify with reasons.

**Instructor note:** Avoid scolding the learner for wanting to mark a small fluctuation. The aim is to make selection deliberate, not to create a new rule against all minor areas.

### 2:30–4:30 — Establish a selection hierarchy

**Show:** Keep the same chart. Fade the candidate overlays slightly, then reveal one reference area at a time.

**On screen — heading:**

> Start with the larger structure

**On screen — reveal 1:**

> **First: major turns and range extremes.**
>
> Ask which areas help explain the broad path price has taken. These are usually a more useful starting point than every small pause along the way.

**Reveal:** Highlight a broad structural high or range extreme. Do not label it “strong,” “best,” or “guaranteed.”

**On screen — reveal 2:**

> **Next: remove duplication.**
>
> Two nearby areas may be describing the same cluster of reactions. If combining them gives a clearer and still defensible description, separate marks may not add useful information.

**Reveal:** Highlight two nearby/overlapping candidate zones, then transition to one broader but still focused reference zone. The candles and scale must remain unchanged.

**On screen — reveal 3:**

> **Then: ask what the smaller area adds.**
>
> Keep a minor area only when it contributes something useful to the question you are examining. If it changes nothing about your reading, leaving it out may make the chart clearer.

**Reveal:** Fade a minor local fluctuation's zone rather than crossing it out with a red “wrong” symbol.

**Voiceover:**

> This is a hierarchy for deciding what to examine first, not a promise that large areas always matter more in every situation. Start with the wider structure, then add detail only when you can explain what it contributes.

### 4:30–6:30 — Context should clarify, not multiply marks

**Show:** Use the same chart or a small adjacent panel to represent a broader context area. Do not add a real timeframe label to fictional data. If showing a hypothetical “broader chart” panel, label it as schematic too.

**On screen — prompt:**

> A broader chart shows another possible area. Should it automatically be added here?

**Options:**
- Yes. Every area from another timeframe belongs on this chart.
- Only if it helps answer the current question or changes how the visible structure is understood.
- No. Broader context is never useful.
- I am not sure yet.

**Reveal — explanation:**

> Broader context can be useful, but importing every possible area can make the chart unreadable.
>
> Before adding it, ask: “What does this area help me understand here?” If you cannot explain its relevance, leave it out for now.

**Instructor note:** Do not introduce a full multi-timeframe workflow. The purpose is to teach relevance and restraint. Avoid suggesting that higher timeframes are always decisive or that a zone becomes reliable because it appears on multiple charts.

### 6:30–8:45 — Worked example: combine, retain, omit

**Show:** Keep the same synthetic candle sequence and scale throughout. The current learning-room flow uses five clutter-chart states before the separate Clear W drawing task:

1. **Observe:** the over-marked chart appears without candidate IDs.
2. **Classify:** A–G receive candidate IDs. The learner chooses Keep, Combine, Leave out for this reading, or Not sure yet for every candidate and provides a short rationale before revealing the edit.
3. **Omit one minor mark:** remove candidate D only. A, B, C, E, F and G remain.
4. **Combine nearby areas:** combine B/C and E/F into broader areas while retaining A and distant G.
5. **Compare the worked edit:** show the lower reaction area and upper reaction area in the reduced reference.

The final view retains two primary areas for this particular chart. Do not pad the chart to meet a number, and do not treat two, three or four as a universal target or maximum for other charts.

**Edit 1 — Omit candidate D**

**On screen — explanation:**

> Candidate D adds a small local fluctuation. Leaving it unmarked makes the chart easier to read without removing the larger path or the other candidates that still need review.

**Production rationale:** This edit removes D only. The candles, scale and all other candidates remain unchanged so the learner can isolate the effect of removing one low-value mark.

**Edit 2 — Combine nearby areas**

**On screen — explanation:**

> B/C and E/F describe nearby, related reactions. I can combine each pair into a broader area while keeping the boundaries focused on the behaviour I am explaining.

**Production rationale:** The combined view keeps A and G and combines B/C and E/F. Do not merge areas merely because they look close on screen; the broader band must still explain a related group of reactions.

**Worked edit — retain the two primary areas**

**On screen — explanation:**

> The reduced reference keeps a lower reaction area and an upper reaction area. Each remains because it helps explain the structure of this chart. The example is one defensible reading, not a universal rule about how many areas every chart needs.

**Comparison:** Learners can use the Previous view control to revisit the same-scale clutter and edit states. The current interface reveals one view at a time; do not describe it as a side-by-side L05-A comparison.

**Instructor note:** Ensure the final annotation is justified by the chart geometry that was actually created. Do not write answer rationales before the asset is final and then force the chart to fit them.

### 8:45–9:30 — Introduce ChartLab Study 1: Clear W

**On screen — title:**

> Your first ChartLab study: Clear W

**On screen — copy:**

> You are ready to practise on a fresh chart.
>
> First mark the support area that best describes the related lower reactions. Submit it. Then mark the resistance area around the related upper reactions and submit that too. After both attempts, compare your choices with the reference and read the reasoning.

**On screen — reminder:**

> The goal is a defensible area, not an identical drawing.

**Production cue:** The entry control opens ChartLab Study 1, *Clear W*, in the lesson context. Preserve the lesson state so the learner can return to the debrief. Do not claim cross-device persistence unless account-backed persistence has been implemented and tested.

### 9:30–13:30 — ChartLab: Clear W activity

**Activity design:** ChartLab Study 1, *Clear W*, provides the learner's first complete zone-drawing task. Keep the surrounding lesson context available, but let the chart occupy the main working area.

#### Step A — Mark support

**On screen — instructions:**

> **Step 1 of 2 — Mark support**
>
> Find the broad area that best captures the related lower reactions. Set your upper and lower boundaries, then submit this step.

**Interaction requirements:**
- The learner can create or resize a support zone using the existing ChartLab interaction.
- Undo/reset is available and clearly labelled.
- The student must explicitly submit the support attempt.
- After submission, the support step is shown as submitted/locked for the current attempt, while the resistance step becomes available. Do not show the reference support zone yet.
- If the tool permits changing an earlier submission, explain the interaction clearly; avoid silently changing the user's submitted work.

**Feedback immediately after submission:**

> Support attempt saved for comparison. Now complete the resistance area before viewing the reference.

Do not display whether the drawing “passed” or “failed” at this stage.

#### Step B — Mark resistance

**On screen — instructions:**

> **Step 2 of 2 — Mark resistance**
>
> Identify the broad upper area that best captures the related reactions. Use boundaries you can explain, then submit.

**Interaction requirements:**
- Keep the same chart scale and candles. Do not reveal any later price outcome while the learner is drawing.
- Submit resistance separately.
- Only after both support and resistance have been submitted may the tool reveal the reference overlays and explanations.

#### Step C — Compare the reference

**Show:** The current ChartLab displays the learner's two submitted zones beside a reasoned example. Preserve the learner's marks and keep both versions visible during comparison.

**On screen — comparison introduction:**

> Compare the coverage and reasoning, not just the exact edges. Your zone may be slightly wider or narrower and still be defensible.

**Reference support rationale — current Clear W example:**

> The support area surrounds the two lower reactions. Their wick lows are close but not identical, so the band allows for that variation while staying focused on the repeated-lows area.

**Reference resistance rationale — current Clear W example:**

> The resistance area surrounds the intervening swing high, or neckline, where the recovery previously stalled. It describes that part of this synthetic sequence; it does not promise that price will turn there again.

**Comparison prompts:**
- Does your support zone capture the lower reaction cluster you intended to describe?
- Does your resistance zone cover the relevant upper reactions without becoming unnecessarily broad?
- Can you explain what each boundary includes and excludes?

**Current comparison feedback:**

> Look at both drawings side by side. Does your support area surround both troughs without absorbing much unrelated movement? Does your resistance area cover the intervening swing high (the neckline)? The example is one defensible interpretation, not an exact answer key. Different boundaries can be reasonable when they capture the relevant reactions and you can explain what they include and exclude.

**Critical implementation note:** The current interface does not assign an overlap percentage or a pass/fail label to the learner's zones. It displays the learner's marks beside the example and prompts the learner to compare coverage and explain the boundaries. Do not describe this as an automated assessment of analytical skill.

**Outcome control:** Do not reveal a later price extension, breakout, or “what happened next” as a reward for a correct drawing. The point of this study is identifying and defending zones, not predicting a result.

### 13:30–15:15 — ChartLab debrief: what the study was testing

**On screen — debrief title:**

> What mattered in this study?

**On screen — copy:**

> You were not asked to mark every small turn. You were asked to choose two areas that describe the main groups of reactions.
>
> Your drawing does not need to match the reference tick for tick. It should capture the relevant behaviour and have boundaries you can explain.

**Voiceover:**

> If your zone was too narrow, review which reactions fell outside it. If it was too broad, ask whether it now covers movement that does not belong to the same explanation. If your zone differs from the reference but represents the same cluster consistently, it may still be defensible.
>
> This is the first complete practice. Later lessons will help you refine boundaries and handle less tidy examples. You do not need to master every judgement at once.

**Production cue:** Offer a path back to the reference comparison without forcing the learner to repeat the whole task. Keep the tone calm and specific; do not use success confetti, a “perfect zones” badge or predictive language.

### 15:15–16:45 — Two-question knowledge check

The check should appear after the ChartLab debrief. Give feedback after each response and allow retries.

**Q1. What is the main goal when prioritising areas on a chart?**

- Mark every possible reaction so none can be missed.
- Keep the areas that best explain the structure and current question, and leave out marks that add little. **Correct.**
- Keep only areas at the most recent price.

**Feedback:**

> The goal is a clearer, better-explained chart. More marks do not automatically mean more useful analysis.

**Q2. Two nearby candidate zones overlap and describe related reactions. What is a reasonable next step?**

- Always keep both because two marks are stronger than one.
- Always delete both because overlapping areas are invalid.
- Consider combining them into one focused area if that better represents the shared reactions. **Correct.**

**Feedback:**

> Combining is one defensible choice when the areas explain the same behaviour. Keep separate zones only when the distinction adds something useful to the current reading.

**Completion rule:** Once both questions have been answered and the learner has viewed the ChartLab feedback/debrief, allow the lesson completion action. Do not equate opening ChartLab with completing the task.

### 16:45–18:00 — Close and handoff

**On screen — takeaway:**

> **Keep the areas that explain the chart. Leave out the marks that add little.**

**On screen — closing copy:**

> You have now moved from recognising zones to deciding which ones deserve attention. Next, we will practise setting and defending zone boundaries more deliberately, using the same evidence-first approach.

**On screen — course reminder:**

> A zone organises past price behaviour. It does not guarantee the next move.

**Production cue:** The next action should go to Lesson 6, not automatically reopen ChartLab. The student should be able to return to their submitted study and its feedback when supported by the learning-room implementation.

## 4. Chart pack and annotation specification

### L05-A — Cluttered chart and prioritisation demonstration

**Purpose:** Teach the difference between identifying possible areas and deciding which areas make the chart more useful.

**Schematic requirements:**
- Overall structure must be understandable without labels.
- Include a clear major turning point or recognisable broad range extreme.
- Include two nearby/overlapping candidate zones that can reasonably be combined.
- Include a smaller local pause that can be omitted without losing the main structural reading.
- Include enough additional candidate marks that the initial state feels cluttered, but preserve candle legibility on mobile.
- Do not include entries, stops, targets, arrows, indicator panes, volume, pattern names or a later trade outcome.

**Required reveal states:**
1. Base chart with the candidate areas visible but no answer labels.
2. Major structural area highlighted.
3. Two nearby candidates highlighted as potentially redundant.
4. Combined reference area shown.
5. Minor area faded/omitted with a short rationale.
6. Final simplified chart compared with the initial cluttered version.

**Annotation register:** For each candidate zone, record whether the reference keeps, combines or omits it; what visible structure supports that choice; what the choice adds to the current explanation; and any reasonable alternative interpretation.

**Alt text:** “Illustrative candlestick chart with several candidate zones; the reveal keeps major structural areas, combines overlapping candidates and omits a minor area that adds little to the broad reading.”

### L05-B — ChartLab Study 1: Clear W

**Purpose:** First full support/resistance drawing attempt after concept teaching.

**Requirements:**
- Use the existing approved ChartLab *Clear W* schematic as the starting reference, subject to a final alignment check against this script.
- Keep the chart fictional and labelled: *“Illustrative chart — not historical market data.”*
- Base price action should show a clear W-like sequence with related lower reactions and related upper reactions. The name “Clear W” is the exercise label, not a signal or promise about what happens next.
- Do not show reference support/resistance zones on initial load.
- Student marks and submits support first, then resistance.
- Lock or clearly mark the submitted support step before allowing the second step; preserve the student's own work.
- Reveal both reference zones and written rationales only after both submissions.
- Do not reveal a later outcome as part of scoring.
- Include undo/reset and touch-friendly zone resizing. The current ChartLab supports pointer-drawn zones and a keyboard-operable alternative: enter upper and lower edges, activate “Set zone from prices,” then submit the zone. The submitted support locks before the resistance step.
- Show the learner's locked marks beside the reference after both submissions. The current interaction gives a visual comparison and explanatory prompt; it does not grade the learner's written reasoning.
- Include a two-question knowledge check after the feedback, then a clear completion action.

**Reference-key authoring rule:** Finalise the answer rationales against the actual chart and overlay positions. The reference support area should capture the lower-reaction cluster; the reference resistance area should capture the relevant upper-reaction cluster. If the final chart supports multiple boundary choices, document the acceptable alternative rather than forcing a single exact answer.

**Alt text:** “Illustrative candlestick chart forming a W-like sequence, used for a first exercise in placing one support area around related lower reactions and one resistance area around related upper reactions.”

### L05-C — ChartLab comparison states

- The learner's zones remain visible or can be reopened after submission.
- Reference zones are separate annotation layers.
- Comparison does not obscure candles or make colour the only way to distinguish the two versions. Use labels, line styles or a clear legend in addition to colour.
- The comparison must explain coverage: which reactions were included, what was excluded and why the selected width is defensible.
- Distinguish “close to the reference,” “reasonable alternative,” and “review boundary choice” only when the interaction can support those distinctions. Do not display fabricated confidence values.

## 5. Model answers and scoring notes

### Cluttered-chart exercise

**A strong response:**
- starts with major structural turns or broad range extremes;
- identifies overlapping or nearby areas that describe substantially related reactions;
- combines such areas when the merged band stays focused and remains explainable;
- leaves out at least one minor area because it does not add much to the current reading; and
- can explain the purpose of each retained zone.

**Partial response:** The student removes several marks but cannot explain why the remaining areas are more useful, or keeps a minor area without connecting it to the current question.

**Needs review:** The student marks every candidate equally, combines zones so broadly that the structural cluster becomes indistinguishable, or treats the largest/oldest/recent zone as automatically decisive without examining the chart.

**Acceptable alternatives:** The exact number and location of zones can vary if the learner's choices are supported by visible structure. The “3–4 areas” ceiling is an exercise constraint only. Do not grade every deviation from the instructor's reference as wrong.

### ChartLab Clear W

**Reference outcome:** One defensible support zone around related lower reactions and one defensible resistance zone around related upper reactions, with a short evidence-based rationale for each.

**Full-credit reasoning:** The learner can explain which reactions the zone represents and why its width is useful.

**Partial response:** The zone is near the intended cluster but the learner cannot explain the boundaries.

**Needs review:** The zone misses much of the relevant reaction group or is so broad that it absorbs substantial unrelated movement, and the learner cannot give a coherent reason for the width.

**Do not assess:** Predicting the next move, drawing the exact same edges as the instructor, or selecting the zone later associated with a profitable outcome. This study assesses drawing judgement, not trading performance.
