## The learning promise of this set

Lessons 1–4 have given students a foundation: they can describe prior price behaviour, recognise broad structure, identify useful turns and understand why a support or resistance area is not a perfectly exact line. Lessons 5–8 must now make that understanding feel usable.

The intended progression is:

1. **Lesson 5 — Choose:** From many possible marks, select a small number of relevant areas; then make the first complete attempt in ChartLab.
2. **Lesson 6 — Draw:** Turn a selected area into defensible boundaries and explain the drawing choices.
3. **Lesson 7 — Edit:** Remove or combine marks that do not help answer the current chart question.
4. **Lesson 8 — Evaluate:** Discuss why an area may be useful without inventing a formula or believing that a zone guarantees a future reaction; then practise imperfectly aligned lows in ChartLab.

By the end of Lesson 8, the learner should have made several meaningful decisions, seen their own work beside a reasoned reference, and learned to say not only “this is a zone,” but also “this is why I marked it, this is why I kept it, and this is what I still do not know.” That is the first real feeling of progress in the course.

The emotional tone should be **quietly encouraging, specific and earned**. Do not use generic praise such as “Amazing!” when the learner has only clicked a button. Tell them what they did that was useful: they selected a structural turn, used consistent boundaries, removed a redundant mark or left an uncertain area unmarked. The learner should feel respected rather than marketed to.

## Shared production rules for Lessons 5–8

- Use controlled, schematic charts with fictional prices unless historical market data is sourced, rights-checked and deliberately approved. Label schematic charts unobtrusively: **“Illustrative chart — not historical market data.”** Do not invent an instrument or timeframe for fictional data.
- Keep chart annotations hidden until the specified reveal. The first view must not contain an accidentally visible answer.
- One main question at a time. No indicators, entry/exit arrows, stop/target marks, option/volume overlays or named chart patterns unless a lesson explicitly calls for them.
- Keep the student task small enough to understand before the learner begins. A prompt should explain what they are deciding, not teach the answer before they try.
- The reference annotation is an example of reasoned analysis, not the only valid drawing. Reward structural justification and useful coverage, not pixel-perfect overlap.
- Treat “I’m unsure” as a legitimate response. Give the student a path to inspect the chart again rather than forcing false confidence.
- Keep the distinction between **observation**, **interpretation** and **trade decision** visible. These lessons are about chart reading, not a buy/sell signal.
- Do not promise that a zone will hold, that more touches make it stronger, or that fewer marks automatically improve performance.
- Do not use the learner's own trade charts in Lessons 5–8. The first personal-chart micro-case remains in Lesson 9, once students have practised the fundamentals. Complete trade case studies remain in Lessons 16–17.
- ChartLab studies stay in the approved order: **Clear W in Lesson 5; Uneven Lows in Lesson 8; Wider Neckline in Lesson 14.** Do not add another ChartLab activity here.
- Keep the lesson page focused. Use short text, a readable chart, an answer action and a reveal; put longer reasoning in a collapsed “Why this works” or “Instructor explanation” area where needed.
- If the learner leaves ChartLab before completion, the intended future experience is to return them to Lesson 5 or Lesson 8 in the same learning-room context. This is a requirement, not a claim that account-backed progress is already implemented.

## Cumulative learning device: the Chart Reading Notes

Beginning in Lesson 5, invite students to keep a small **Chart Reading Notes** record. This is a learning device, not a new trading system or performance journal. After each lesson, the learner records one concrete choice and one uncertainty:

- **Lesson 5:** “The areas I kept were…; I left out… because…”
- **Lesson 6:** “I chose these boundaries because…; a reasonable alternative might be…”
- **Lesson 7:** “I removed or combined… because it did not add…”
- **Lesson 8:** “This area is worth watching because…; what remains uncertain is…”

The notes should be available as a simple on-screen recap. If saving or exporting is not yet implemented, do not promise persistent storage or an export button. The content should still be usable as a written prompt in the lesson.

# LESSON 05 — Choose the Right Areas and Reduce Clutter

**Module:** 2 — Find the Important Areas  
**Estimated duration:** 18–22 minutes, including ChartLab Study 1  
**Prerequisites:** Lessons 1–4  
**Format:** Select → justify → simplify → prepare → ChartLab → compare → retain  
**Primary asset:** A deliberately over-marked chart plus ChartLab Study 1, *Clear W*  
**Do not use:** The instructor's own trade chart, entry/exit markers, a multi-timeframe “ruleset,” numeric zone-strength scores, or later candles that reveal the exercise answer prematurely

## 1. Instructional decision

This is the transition from understanding separate concepts to making a useful selection. Students have already learned what a zone describes, how to recognise structure, how to choose swing points and why boundaries can vary. Now they have to decide which possible areas deserve space on the chart.

The lesson should make chart clutter feel like a reasoning problem, not just a visual-design problem. If a learner draws ten zones, they can often explain almost any subsequent move after the fact. The aim is not to impose a universal maximum number of zones. It is to teach the learner to keep only the areas that help explain the current chart and question.

ChartLab comes at the end, not the start. Students first simplify a chart and understand the reason for their choices. Only then do they complete their first support-and-resistance drawing task. The first ChartLab study is deliberately clear enough to let a beginner concentrate on the process rather than struggle with several ambiguities at once.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

1. Select a small set of areas linked to meaningful structure or prior reactions.
2. Explain why a selected area matters to the chart being studied.
3. Identify a redundant, weakly supported or currently irrelevant mark and explain why it can be omitted or combined.
4. Complete the Clear W ChartLab activity by submitting a support zone and then a resistance zone.
5. Compare their boundaries with a reasoned reference without assuming that a visual mismatch automatically means the analysis is wrong.

**Not a learning outcome yet:** independently ranking zone quality with a score, predicting which area will hold, defining breakout confirmation, using an entry trigger, or deciding whether a trade should be taken.

## 3. Student-facing lesson script and reveal timing

### 0:00–0:50 — Make the new challenge feel meaningful

**On screen — title:**

> Choose the areas that matter

**On screen — copy:**

> You can now recognise the structure of a chart and identify places where price has reacted before. The next challenge is deciding what deserves to stay on the chart.
>
> A useful chart does not show everything you noticed. It shows the areas that help you explain what matters.

**Instructor/production cue:** This is a short bridge from prior lessons, not a recap article. Use a quiet progress marker such as “Lesson 5 of 18.” Do not use a streak, trophy or exaggerated celebration. The learner is moving from concept recognition toward application.

### 0:50–2:30 — The cluttered chart

**Show:** A schematic chart with seven or eight candidate horizontal marks or bands. Include a mix of: a broad prior range edge, a major turning area, two nearby marks that may be redundant, a tiny local fluctuation and one mark far from the current chart question. Do not mark which ones are “good” or “bad.”

**On screen — prompt:**

> Imagine you have to explain this chart to another trader. Which areas would you keep, combine or leave out?
>
> You are not being asked to find the one perfect answer. Choose the areas you can explain with evidence.

**Interaction:** Let the learner tap candidate marks to classify each as **Keep**, **Combine with another area**, **Leave out for this reading**, or **Not sure yet**. They should be able to revise a selection before submitting. Avoid dragging all lines around at this stage; the lesson is about selection, not precision drawing.

**After submission — neutral feedback:**

> Your choices give us something to examine. The important part is not how many marks you kept; it is whether each one adds useful information.

Do not grade the initial selection heavily. It is a diagnostic moment and should create a reason to learn the filters.

### 2:30–5:15 — Teach the selection filters

**Reveal the heading:**

> Three questions help you decide what stays

Reveal each one at a time, with one highlighted candidate mark on the chart.

**On screen — filter 1: Is it anchored in structure?**

> Does this area relate to a meaningful turn, a cluster of related reactions or an edge of the range? Or is it simply a small fluctuation that happens to be easy to mark?

**Instructor note:** Link back to Lessons 2 and 3. Do not say that a large candle automatically creates an important level; a swing's relevance comes from its role in the surrounding structure.

**On screen — filter 2: Does it add information?**

> Does this area tell us something different from a nearby area we have already kept? If two marks describe the same broad region, do we need both?

**Instructor note:** Do not merge zones merely because they are close on the screen. A merge is defensible when it captures the same broad reaction region without hiding an important distinction. Keep separate marks when the structure or the question genuinely calls for it.

**On screen — filter 3: Is it relevant to the chart question?**

> Does this area help explain the part of the chart we are studying now? An area can be real in the historical chart and still not be central to the current reading.

**Instructor note:** “Leave out” means “not needed for this analysis,” not “this area can never matter.” Avoid presenting a strict hierarchy where a higher-timeframe level must always override local evidence. Broader context can inform a reading; it does not remove the need to assess the chart in front of us.

### 5:15–7:40 — Watch the chart become easier to read

**Show:** Return to the same cluttered chart. Remove one mark at a time. The learner must see exactly what changed and why; do not replace the entire image instantly with a “perfect” version.

**Reveal sequence:**

1. Remove a tiny local fluctuation that does not explain meaningful structure.
2. Combine two nearby marks that refer to the same broad reaction area.
3. Leave out an area that is distant from the current question, while noting it could be relevant in another analysis.
4. Keep a major area that is tied to clear structure.

**On screen — explanation after each reveal:**

> We removed this mark because it added little beyond the structure already shown.

> We combined these marks because the chart supports treating them as one broader area for this exercise.

> We left this area out of the current reading. That does not mean it can never matter; it simply is not helping answer today's question.

> We kept this area because it connects to a clear structural feature. It is still an area to observe, not a guarantee of future behaviour.

**Instructor note:** Each explanation must match the actual synthetic chart. The production team should maintain an annotation register with a short rationale for every kept, removed or merged area.

### 7:40–8:45 — Give the learner a practical constraint

**On screen — copy:**

> For the next exercise, try to keep no more than three or four primary areas. This is a practice constraint to help you focus—not a rule that every trading chart must contain four zones or fewer.

**Prompt:**

> Before you continue, complete this sentence: “I kept these areas because… I left out or combined these because…”

**Interaction:** Short text entry or selection plus a concise justification. Do not require lengthy prose on mobile.

**Feedback guidance:** Credit reference to structure, redundancy and relevance. Do not award extra credit for using the fewest possible zones if important context was omitted.

### 8:45–9:45 — Prepare for the first ChartLab

**On screen — heading:**

> Now put the ideas together

**On screen — copy:**

> You have practised three decisions: noticing structure, choosing meaningful turning points and treating related reactions as areas rather than perfect lines.
>
> In the first ChartLab, you will make those decisions yourself. Start with support, submit it, then mark resistance. You will see the reference reasoning only after both drawings are submitted.

**Instructor note:** Do not show the ChartLab reference drawing or final answer here. Explain the order and expectations without giving away the study.

### 9:45–15:45 — ChartLab Study 1: Clear W

**Launch label:**

> ChartLab 1 — Clear W

**Student-facing introduction:**

> This is your first full drawing exercise. Look at the price structure before touching the chart. You are looking for the broad area associated with the two lower reactions, then the relevant upper reaction between them.
>
> Your drawing does not need to match a reference pixel for pixel. Make a choice you can explain.

**Required interaction sequence:**

1. **Study opens with the unlabelled chart.** No reference bands, arrows or hint showing the exact boundaries. Keep only the minimum instruction required to begin.
2. **Support task.** Student draws or adjusts a support zone around the relevant lower reactions, then submits. Submission locks the support task and preserves the student's drawing visibly.
3. **Resistance task.** The student then marks resistance around the relevant intervening upper reaction and submits. Do not replace the support drawing with a clean image; the submitted support remains visible in a distinct style while they work.
4. **Comparison state.** Only after both submissions, show the student’s two zones alongside the reference zones. Include concise rationale for support and resistance separately.
5. **Tailored feedback.** Comment on whether the student's zone captures the intended structure, whether its width is useful, and whether the boundary logic is defensible. If the boundaries differ but still capture the reactions coherently, state that clearly.
6. **Two-question knowledge check.** The questions test why the areas were chosen and why the reference is not a guaranteed prediction.
7. **Completion and return.** After the knowledge check and debrief, offer a clear action to return to Lesson 5 in the learning room. The standalone ChartLab prototype must not be described as already saving account-backed progress unless that integration is built and verified.

**In ChartLab, avoid:**

- “Correct line!” if the result depends on one arbitrary boundary.
- a misleading “zone strength: 92%” number;
- instant disclosure of the reference when the first zone is submitted;
- a second drawing task that erases the first attempt;
- a blocking message that implies failure if the student's reasonable boundary is wider or narrower than the example.

**After completion — on-screen debrief:**

> Pause and compare the reasoning, not just the shapes. Which reactions did your support zone capture? Did your resistance zone represent the upper turning area without swallowing unrelated price action?
>
> If your boundary differs from the reference, ask whether the difference changes what the area describes. A defensible alternative can still be a useful reading.

### 15:45–17:15 — Knowledge check

**Q1. Why should a chart not be filled with every possible zone?**

A. A chart is always limited to four zones in real trading.  
B. Too many marks can obscure the structure and add little information.  
C. A chart with fewer zones guarantees a better trade.

**Correct answer:** B.

**Feedback:**

> Exactly. The number is not the rule. The aim is to keep the areas that help explain the chart and leave out marks that add little to the current reading.

**Q2. If your zone differs from the reference drawing, what should you examine first?**

A. Whether your boundary is pixel-perfect.  
B. Whether your zone captures the relevant reactions and has a defensible width.  
C. Whether your zone guarantees the next price move.

**Correct answer:** B.

**Feedback:**

> Compare the evidence and the boundary logic. The reference is a reasoned example, not a promise that only one exact drawing can be valid.

### 17:15–18:00 — Close and give earned encouragement

**On screen — takeaway:**

> **A useful chart is selective. Keep the areas you can explain.**

**On screen — closing copy:**

> You have now moved from recognising a reaction to choosing which areas deserve attention—and made your first complete support-and-resistance drawing attempt.
>
> That is a real step forward. You are not expected to have a finished method yet. The next lesson is about making your boundaries more consistent and explaining exactly why you placed them where you did.

**Chart Reading Notes prompt:**

> I kept these areas because…  
> I left out or combined… because…  
> One part I still find uncertain is…

Keep the encouragement specific and proportionate. It should acknowledge the new skill, not imply proficiency from completing one exercise.

## 4. Chart pack and annotation specification

### L05-A — Over-marked selection chart

- **Purpose:** Teach keep/combine/leave-out choices before drawing precision becomes the focus.
- **Chart state 0:** Seven or eight unlabelled candidate horizontal marks/bands with the synthetic candle series. In lesson display, candidate marks may appear as subdued thin lines so the chart feels cluttered without visually overwhelming the candles.
- **Reveal state 1:** Each candidate becomes selectable; no classification is preselected.
- **Reveal state 2:** Each removal or merge is explained in sequence.
- **Required features:** one major range/turn area, two redundant nearby marks, a minor fluctuation, one far/relevance-limited mark and one important area that must remain.
- **Required assets:** desktop and mobile crops, no answer visible in initial state, alt text and annotation register.

### CL-01 — ChartLab Study 1: Clear W

- **Purpose:** First complete zone-drawing attempt after concepts and chart selection have been taught.
- **Chart:** A clear W-like sequence with two lower reactions forming a broad support region and an intervening upper reaction area. Keep the two lows visually related but not perfectly manufactured; otherwise the lesson could imply that every valid zone has exact symmetry.
- **Task order:** Support submit → lock support → resistance submit → lock both → reference comparison → tailored feedback → two-question check → completion/return.
- **Reference rationale — support:** Explain the structural anchor, the lower reactions captured and how the band avoids being too narrow to describe the area or so broad that it captures unrelated movement.
- **Reference rationale — resistance:** Explain why the intervening upper reaction is relevant and why its band should not be expanded simply to include every nearby candle.
- **Feedback states required:** defensible capture; too narrow to represent both reactions; overly broad; anchored to the wrong local movement; reasonable alternative boundary; incomplete/uncertain submission.
- **Scoring note:** Use a permissive spatial comparison only as a visual aid, not as the sole verdict. The explanation must remain available even when the student's geometry falls outside a simple tolerance.

### L05 annotation register — minimum fields

For each candidate or reference zone, record: asset ID; zone ID; structural anchor; reactions included; nearby movements intentionally excluded; reason retained/combined/omitted; intended reveal step; student-facing explanation; reasonable alternatives; and mobile crop note.

## 5. Model answers and scoring notes

### Clutter selection task

**Strong answer:** Keeps the major structural area and the range edge relevant to the current reading; combines redundant nearby marks if they describe one broad reaction region; removes a tiny local fluctuation with little structural role; leaves a distant area out while noting it may matter in another context.

**Acceptable alternative:** Keeps two nearby areas separate when the student explains how each describes a distinct part of the structure and why merging them would lose information.

**Weak answer:** Keeps every mark because every mark has at least one candle near it; removes everything without explanation; claims a mark is important solely because price later moved away from it.

**Scoring rubric (qualitative):**
- **3 — Defensible:** Selection is selective, structurally anchored and explained; omissions are intentional.
- **2 — Developing:** Some good selections, but one or more marks are redundant or explanations are incomplete.
- **1 — Needs another look:** Most marks are retained without a structural reason, or key structure is omitted with no explanation.

Do not convert this exercise score into a prediction or trading grade.

### Clear W ChartLab

The model answer should display reference bands and give boundary rationale. Do not demand identical endpoints. The answer key should explicitly identify which nearby candle movements are not necessary to include and why. The teaching point is that support/resistance summarizes a reaction area; it is not the exact line that must hold.

## 6. Instructor notes and reference integration

- **John J. Murphy, *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets*:** broad technical-analysis foundation; supports teaching support/resistance alongside structure and prior highs/lows. Do not make it sound like a unique drawing formula.
- **Jack D. Schwager and Mark Etzkorn, *A Complete Guide to the Futures Market*, Chapters 7–8:** supports attention to trading-range boundaries, prior major highs/lows and clusters of turns. Use it to justify considering meaningful structure—not to say every past high/low must be marked.
- **Adam Grimes, *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis*, especially the discussion of trading ranges and trend/range transitions:** critical counterweight. Selection should be evidence-led; conventional chart markings are not automatically predictive.

**Teaching caution:** The “three or four areas” instruction is a practice constraint that makes selection visible. It is not a universal rule. The learner should never infer that fewer zones automatically make a better trade or guarantee results.

## 7. Website and learning-room handoff

The Lesson 5 page needs:

- lesson title, brief bridge from Lessons 1–4 and a compact progress indicator;
- interactive classification of candidate marks (Keep / Combine / Leave out / Not sure) or an accessible equivalent;
- progressive before/after reveal with reasons for each edit;
- ChartLab entry point only after the selection practice;
- a clearly labelled ChartLab transition that preserves lesson context;
- after ChartLab, a return to the same lesson position, the two-question check and Chart Reading Notes prompt;
- a visible “complete lesson” action after the learner has reviewed feedback, not on page open.

Future integration requirements include persistence of lesson completion and ChartLab attempts for the signed-in account across devices. Do not claim this functionality exists until implemented and tested.

# LESSON 06 — Set Defensible Zone Boundaries

**Module:** 3 — Draw and Evaluate Zones  
**Estimated duration:** 16–19 minutes  
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 5  
**Format:** Re-anchor → inspect reactions → compare boundary conventions → draw → explain → review  
**Primary assets:** A clear support area, a resistance counterpart and one deliberately ambiguous boundary example  
**Do not use:** ChartLab, the instructor's own trade chart, exact-price “right answer” scoring, entry/exit labels or any claim that a named wick/body convention is universally correct

## 1. Instructional decision

Lesson 5 taught which areas to keep. Lesson 6 asks a different question: **once an area has been selected, how wide should the zone be and what determines its edges?**

Students need a repeatable reasoning process, not a universal line-drawing law. Their method should begin with the structural turn or cluster, examine the relevant candle ranges and closes, test whether the chosen band captures the reactions it is intended to describe, and check whether it has become too broad to be useful.

The lesson should explain that reasonable traders may draw different but defensible zones. That flexibility must not turn into “anything goes”: the student still needs a structural anchor and a boundary rationale.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end, the learner should be able to:

1. Start a zone from a clear structural anchor or related cluster of reactions.
2. Explain which wicks, bodies or closing areas influenced the chosen boundaries.
3. Recognise a band that is too narrow to represent the reactions or so broad that it includes unrelated movement.
4. Explain a reasonable alternative boundary without treating it as proof that one method is always superior.
5. Write a short rationale for a zone's upper and lower boundaries.

**Not an outcome yet:** using a mechanical wick/body rule, evaluating a zone's predictive probability, identifying rejection versus acceptance, or placing a stop loss.

## 3. Student-facing lesson script and reveal timing

### 0:00–0:45 — Connect selection to drawing

**On screen — title:**

> Set boundaries you can explain

**On screen — copy:**

> Last lesson, you chose which areas deserved attention. Now we will work on the drawing itself.
>
> The question is not “What is the one perfect price?” It is “Which boundaries describe the reactions I am trying to capture, without making the zone meaningless?”

**Production cue:** Show the outline of an unlabelled reaction area with no band yet. Do not start with a completed drawing.

### 0:45–2:20 — Observe the variation first

**Show:** A schematic support example in which three lower reactions occur in the same broad region, but their wick extremes, candle bodies and closes are not identical.

**On screen — prompt:**

> Look carefully at these reactions. Do they all turn at one identical price, or do they occupy a range?

**Response choices:**
- The reactions line up exactly.
- The reactions are near one another but vary.
- I cannot tell from this view.

**Reveal:** Highlight the related lows and show light guide marks for the wick extremes and body/close area. Do not draw the final zone yet.

**On-screen feedback:**

> The reactions are related, but they are not identical. A useful zone needs to represent that variation while still preserving a meaningful area.

### 2:20–4:10 — Establish the boundary method

**On-screen — heading:**

> A defensible zone starts with a reason

Reveal the four steps one at a time.

**Step 1 — Anchor the area.**

> Start with the meaningful swing or group of related reactions. Do not start by choosing a width that looks neat and then searching for candles to justify it.

**Step 2 — Inspect the candles involved.**

> Look at the relevant wick extremes and where the bodies and closes sit. Ask which parts of the candles help describe the reaction you are marking.

**Step 3 — Test whether the band captures the evidence.**

> Does the area include the related reactions you are trying to represent? Or does it miss an important part of the cluster?

**Step 4 — Test whether the band stays useful.**

> Is the zone so wide that it covers unrelated price action and stops helping you distinguish where the reaction occurred?

**Instructor note:** Teach these as a working process, not a mathematical formula. Different markets, instruments, volatility regimes and chart contexts may produce different candle geometry. Do not introduce ATR-based band widths or a percentage tolerance here; those would create premature complexity and suggest a tested rule the course has not established.

### 4:10–6:15 — Compare three choices

**Show in sequence, all on the same chart:**

1. A too-narrow line/band that captures only the very tips of one reaction and misses related variation.
2. A defensible zone that captures the relevant cluster.
3. An overly broad band that swallows candles and movement not needed to describe the reaction.

**On screen — question before each reveal:**

> What does this boundary include—and what important information might it miss or blur?

**Reveal 1 — too narrow:**

> This mark is precise on the screen, but it does not represent the full group of reactions we are examining. A thin line is not automatically more accurate simply because it looks exact.

**Reveal 2 — defensible:**

> This zone captures the main reaction area while keeping its boundaries tied to visible structure. Another trader might choose slightly different edges and still describe the same area reasonably.

**Reveal 3 — too broad:**

> This band covers so much unrelated price action that it becomes harder to explain what the zone actually represents. Wider is not automatically safer or better.

**Instructor note:** Ensure the synthetic chart actually demonstrates the claims. If the narrow zone captures all relevant reactions or the wide zone still has a clear structural meaning, redraw the chart rather than forcing the text to fit.

### 6:15–8:00 — A consistent convention matters more than copying a drawing

**Show:** Two side-by-side versions of the same reaction area. One convention is more wick-inclusive; the other gives greater emphasis to the body/close cluster while still acknowledging the excursion.

**On screen — copy:**

> A wick can show how far price briefly travelled. The body and close can show where the candle finished. Both can be useful observations, but neither gives a universal answer on its own.
>
> Choose a boundary convention that makes sense for the chart, explain why it captures the relevant reactions, and apply it consistently. Do not quietly change the rule from candle to candle just to make the old drawing look right.

**Prompt:**

> Which version would you use for this chart, and what evidence would you use to explain your choice?

**Feedback principle:** Accept either version if the written explanation fits the chart and the band remains useful. If a selected version excludes a defining reaction or covers an excessive amount of unrelated movement, ask the student to reconsider that evidence.

### 8:00–9:25 — Apply the same thinking at resistance

**Show:** A separate upper-reaction chart with varied wick highs and body/close placement. Begin unlabelled.

**On screen — prompt:**

> Now apply the same reasoning from the other direction. Which upper reactions belong to the area, and what would make your zone too broad?

**Reveal:** Add a reference band after the student responds.

**On-screen explanation:**

> The direction has changed, but the reasoning has not. Start from the related structure, inspect the candle ranges and closes, and choose boundaries that capture the area without swallowing unrelated movement.

**Instructor note:** Do not introduce reversal prediction. This exercise concerns how to represent an area, not what price will do when it returns.

### 9:25–12:15 — Independent practice: defend your own boundaries

**Show:** A new, unlabelled schematic chart with one coherent lower reaction cluster. It should be somewhat less tidy than the first demonstration but still interpretable.

**On-screen instructions:**

> 1. Mark a support zone around the relevant reactions.
> 2. Before looking at the reference, complete the three short prompts below.

**Prompts:**
- What is the structural anchor for your zone?
- Which candle features or reactions determined your boundaries?
- What nearby movement did you choose not to include, and why?

**Reveal rule:** No reference overlay until the learner submits both the drawing and the written explanation. If the learner says they are unsure, invite them to compare the cluster and the limits of their band rather than invent an answer.

**After submission — model comparison:** Reveal the reference band with two brief notes: “Why this area was captured” and “A reasonable alternative.” The alternative should be real, not token language; if no alternative is defensible on this chart, the note should say why.

### 12:15–14:30 — Knowledge check

**Q1. Which is the best reason to make a zone a little wider than one thin line?**

A. Wider zones are always safer.  
B. Related reactions occupy a range, and the chosen band should capture the relevant evidence without becoming vague.  
C. A broad zone makes a future reaction more likely.

**Correct answer:** B.

**Feedback:**

> The width should describe the reaction area. It is not a way to guarantee a future outcome.

**Q2. Two students draw slightly different zones around the same cluster. What should we examine?**

A. Which drawing matches the reference pixels exactly.  
B. Whether each drawing has a structural anchor, captures the relevant reactions and remains usefully bounded.  
C. Which student sounds more confident.

**Correct answer:** B.

**Feedback:**

> A reasonable alternative can still be defensible. The drawing must be supported by the chart, not by confidence or visual imitation.

### 14:30–16:00 — Close and give the learner a usable principle

**On-screen takeaway:**

> **A good boundary has a reason. Precision without evidence is only decoration.**

**On-screen closing copy:**

> You have now moved beyond spotting an area. You have practised explaining where it begins, where it ends and why those limits are useful.
>
> The next lesson is about the other half of clear chart work: knowing what to remove. Even a well-drawn zone may not deserve to stay on every chart.

**Chart Reading Notes prompt:**

> I chose these boundaries because…  
> A reasonable alternative could be…  
> I would reconsider my width if…

## 4. Chart pack and annotation specification

### L06-A — Lower reactions with wick/body variation

- **Purpose:** Expose the boundary decision before revealing a zone.
- **Initial state:** Three related lower reactions in a broad area, varied wick extremes, bodies and closes.
- **Reveals:** Relevant lows identified; wick/body guides; too-narrow option; defensible reference zone; overly broad option.
- **Quality gate:** The reference zone must visibly capture the teaching-relevant reactions; it must not be drawn so loosely that nearly any band appears valid.

### L06-B — Upper reactions

- **Purpose:** Demonstrate that the boundary process generalises to resistance.
- **Initial state:** Several related upper reactions with varied wicks/bodies, no line or label.
- **Reveal:** Reference resistance zone and brief rationale.

### L06-C — Independent support practice

- **Purpose:** Test boundary reasoning on a new chart.
- **Required learner response:** Drawing plus three rationale prompts.
- **Answer-key state:** Reference band; structural anchor; included and excluded reactions; reasonable alternative; what would make the zone too broad.

### Required annotation register fields

Asset ID; support/resistance direction; structural anchor; candle features used; included reactions; intentionally excluded price action; why the upper boundary sits where it does; why the lower boundary sits where it does; acceptable alternative; common error; reveal state; mobile legibility note.

## 5. Model answers and scoring notes

**Strong answer:** Identifies a structural anchor, explains how relevant wicks and/or bodies/closes inform the boundaries, captures the intended reaction cluster, and notes at least one nearby movement that was intentionally excluded. A different boundary from the reference can receive full credit if the explanation fits the chart.

**Developing answer:** The band covers the general area but the explanation does not clearly link its boundaries to the chart, or the width is somewhat too broad/narrow.

**Weak answer:** Draws a thin line because “support must be exact”; surrounds a large section of the chart without specifying which reactions matter; changes boundary logic without explanation; or claims a boundary is correct because price later reacted to it.

**Qualitative review rubric:**
- **3 — Defensible:** structural anchor and boundary logic are clear, coverage is useful, and uncertainty is acknowledged where appropriate.
- **2 — Developing:** area is broadly plausible but one boundary or its explanation needs refinement.
- **1 — Needs another look:** no clear anchor, weak relationship to the reactions, or excessive width that obscures the relevant area.

Use this rubric for feedback, not as a probabilistic strength rating.

## 6. Instructor notes and reference integration

- **Murphy, *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets*:** supports teaching the conventional support/resistance framework. Use it as foundation, not as evidence for one exact wick/body boundary procedure.
- **Schwager and Etzkorn, *A Complete Guide to the Futures Market*, Chapter 8:** gives a textbook treatment of support and resistance. Our four-step boundary process is a TradeThesis teaching framework designed to make the student's reasoning explicit; do not attribute it as a verbatim or universal formula from the book.
- **Grimes, *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis*:** supports the course's critical stance. A drawn area should be questioned for its usefulness; conventional marks are not automatically predictive.

**Do not teach yet:** breakout confirmation, retests, “strong/weak” numerical grades, a required volatility-based width, or trade entries and stops.

## 7. Website and learning-room handoff

Required experience:

- initial unlabelled chart with one question;
- stepwise reveal controls for the boundary convention and three candidate widths;
- accessible selection control for the resistance counterpart;
- independent drawing or mark-selection interaction that works on touch screens and is not drag-only;
- written rationale prompts associated with the drawing;
- reference overlay only after submission;
- feedback that explains reasoning and supports acceptable alternatives;
- a short notes recap at completion.

Do not force a strict pixel-perfect pass condition. The interface may show an overlap/coverage comparison to help visualise the reference, but written rationale must remain the main feedback channel.

## 8. Lesson 6 production checklist

- [ ] L06-A makes wick/body variation visible at desktop and phone width.
- [ ] Three candidate boundary choices are substantively distinct.
- [ ] Reference rationales match the actual plotted candles.
- [ ] Resistance example teaches the same process without extra concepts.
- [ ] Independent practice has a genuinely unseen initial state.
- [ ] Reasonable alternative guidance is specific and not merely “anything can be right.”
- [ ] Feedback avoids exact-price scoring and probability claims.
- [ ] Source notes distinguish established concept from TradeThesis's teaching method.

# LESSON 07 — Keep the Chart Readable

**Module:** 3 — Draw and Evaluate Zones  
**Estimated duration:** 13–16 minutes  
**Prerequisites:** Lessons 1–6  
**Format:** Inspect clutter → edit one mark at a time → explain the edit → simplify independently → compare  
**Primary assets:** One cluttered chart, an incremental edit sequence and a new independent chart  
**Do not use:** Personal trade charts, ChartLab, a universal hard cap on zones, or language that treats an unmarked area as permanently irrelevant

## 1. Instructional decision

Lesson 6 asked the learner to draw defensible boundaries. Lesson 7 establishes that a well-drawn zone still may not belong on the chart for the current question. Students need to learn that analysis includes editing: removing marks that duplicate information, combining areas when appropriate and deliberately leaving out a valid but irrelevant historical area.

The learner should see the chart improve step by step. A before/after image alone does not teach why it improved. Each removal or merge should be accompanied by a short explanation. The reward is not a prettier image; it is a chart whose retained marks have clear jobs.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end, the learner should be able to:

1. Identify redundant, weakly anchored or currently irrelevant zones.
2. Decide when nearby areas may be combined and when they should remain separate.
3. Simplify a chart while retaining the major structure needed for the current question.
4. Explain why leaving an area unmarked is an intentional analytical choice, not evidence of missing a signal.

**Not an outcome yet:** assessing whether a zone has predictive edge, choosing an entry, assuming the cleanest chart always performs better, or applying a fixed maximum number of zones in live trading.

## 3. Student-facing lesson script and reveal timing

### 0:00–0:45 — Set the editing task

**On screen — title:**

> A clear chart is edited, not just marked

**On screen — copy:**

> A zone can be drawn reasonably and still not help the current analysis.
>
> Today you will practise editing a chart. You are not trying to erase every detail; you are trying to keep the details that help explain the structure.

**Production cue:** Reuse the visual vocabulary from Lessons 5–6. Show the busy chart only after the short opening.

### 0:45–2:30 — Ask what the marks actually help explain

**Show:** A chart covered with about six or seven zones, including repeated overlapping marks, a minor local mark, a major structural area, and one meaningful but distant area.

**On-screen prompt:**

> Which of these marks helps you explain the part of the chart we are looking at now? Which marks repeat information or pull your attention away?

**Interaction:** Let the learner select zones as **Keep**, **Combine**, **Leave out for this reading**, or **Uncertain**. If the interface cannot support selections per band, use a short numbered list with a chart legend. Do not require precise drawing yet.

**On-screen response after attempt:**

> Before removing anything, decide what job each mark is doing. A mark should earn its place by helping answer a question about this chart.

### 2:30–5:30 — Demonstrate three reasons to edit

Reveal the reasons one at a time. Each reason should highlight the exact band involved.

**Reason 1 — It repeats information already present.**

> These two nearby bands describe the same broad reaction region for the question we are asking. Combining them makes the structure easier to read without losing a distinction this chart needs.

**Caution:** Combine only when the areas are genuinely redundant for the present analysis. If the two bands represent different structural pivots or different parts of a range, keep that distinction visible.

**Reason 2 — It is weakly anchored.**

> This mark sits near a small fluctuation but does not help explain the larger movement. We can acknowledge that price moved there without promoting every pause to a main zone.

**Caution:** “Minor” does not mean “never relevant.” A small area may matter to a different, more local question. We are choosing the focus for this chart, not declaring universal irrelevance.

**Reason 3 — It is not needed for the current question.**

> This area is part of the broader history, but it is far from the section we are studying. Leaving it out helps us concentrate on the current structure. We can bring it back if the question changes.

**Caution:** If the distant zone is needed to understand the visible chart's overall range, do not remove it just to produce a sparse image. The actual chart must make the decision defensible.

### 5:30–7:15 — Reveal the chart becoming clearer

**Show:** Remove or merge one mark per reveal. Keep the previous state available as a brief toggle or “see previous version” action.

For each edit, show:
- the band changed;
- whether it was removed or combined;
- the specific reason; and
- what information remains represented afterward.

**On-screen copy after the last edit:**

> Notice what changed: we did not discover a secret level. We made the chart's main structure easier to explain by removing marks that were not earning their space.

**Instructor note:** The final chart should remain realistic. Do not make the clean version unnaturally empty or imply that only two or three zones can ever be relevant.

### 7:15–8:00 — Explain the exercise constraint

**On screen — copy:**

> For the next chart, aim to keep no more than four primary areas. This limit is a way to practise prioritising. It is not a universal rule for trading charts.

**Prompt:**

> For each area you keep, you should be able to finish the sentence: “This stays because…”

### 8:00–11:30 — Independent practice: edit another chart

**Show:** A new schematic chart with five or six candidate zones. Include one clear structural area, two overlapping/redundant marks, one minor fluctuation and one area that is potentially meaningful but not central to the question.

**On-screen instructions:**

> Edit the chart for this question: “Which areas best explain the main structure shown here?”
>
> Keep, combine or leave out each candidate area. Then write one short reason for one mark you kept and one you removed or combined.

**Response structure:**
- Area kept because…
- Area removed/combined because…
- One area I am unsure about is…

**Reveal rule:** Keep the cleaned-up version and explanations hidden until the learner submits. Permit uncertainty as an answer, but ask for the evidence behind it.

### 11:30–13:00 — Compare, don't copy

**Reveal:** The reasoned reference version, then highlight each key decision.

**On-screen copy:**

> Compare the jobs of the retained marks. Did you keep the important structure? Did any two of your zones repeat the same information? Did you remove something that you can now explain was useful?
>
> Your chart does not need to look identical to this one. If a different selection preserves the important structure and you can defend it, that is worth keeping.

**Instructor note:** Show a plausible alternative selection in the instructor notes if the synthetic chart allows one. Do not invent an alternative just to claim everything is acceptable.

### 13:00–14:45 — Knowledge check

**Q1. You remove a zone because it does not help answer the question in this chart. What does that mean?**

A. The area can never matter in future analysis.  
B. It is not needed for this particular reading; a different question may make it relevant.  
C. All distant zones should always be deleted.

**Correct answer:** B.

**Feedback:**

> Editing is contextual. You are choosing what helps this reading, not declaring that an area is permanently meaningless.

**Q2. When should two nearby zones be combined?**

A. Whenever they are close together on the screen.  
B. When they describe the same broad region for the current question and combining them does not hide a meaningful distinction.  
C. Always combine all nearby zones to reduce clutter.

**Correct answer:** B.

**Feedback:**

> The decision depends on what the areas represent, not just their visual distance.

### 14:45–15:30 — Close with the feeling of control

**On-screen takeaway:**

> **Every mark should have a job. Leaving something out is a decision too.**

**On-screen closing copy:**

> You can now do more than add zones. You can edit your analysis, explain what stays and deliberately leave out what does not help.
>
> Next, we will look at how to evaluate a zone's usefulness without pretending there is a magic number that tells us how strong it is.

**Chart Reading Notes prompt:**

> I removed or combined… because…  
> The most useful area I kept was… because…  
> If my chart question changed, I might reconsider…

## 4. Chart pack and annotation specification

### L07-A — Over-marked demonstration chart

- **Purpose:** Make the learner experience the cost of excessive, redundant or irrelevant marks.
- **Initial state:** Six or seven bands; no band preselected as erroneous.
- **Reveal states:** First redundant merge; removal of minor fluctuation; omission of distant zone for the current question; final clean view.
- **Required annotation:** Explain what information was removed, what information remains and why the change helps this specific reading.

### L07-B — Independent editing chart

- **Purpose:** Test whether the learner can transfer the editing principles without seeing the demonstration answer.
- **Initial state:** Five or six candidate bands with meaningful and redundant areas mixed together.
- **Reference:** Clear before/after plus a rationale for each edit.
- **Alternative answer note:** If more than one selection is defensible, list the alternative and its rationale. If the chart really calls for a specific structural area, make that requirement explicit.

## 5. Model answers and scoring notes

**Strong answer:** Retains the major structural zones needed for the question, merges two marks that describe the same broad area only where no meaningful distinction is lost, removes or temporarily omits low-relevance marks, and explains at least one edit using evidence.

**Acceptable alternative:** Retains an additional area if the student explains a distinct structural role it plays. A clean chart is not necessarily the one with the fewest marks.

**Weak answer:** Removes everything solely because a chart should be simple; retains all marks without a reason; merges zones only because they are near each other; or says an omitted area has no importance under any circumstances.

**Qualitative rubric:**
- **3 — Defensible edit:** Main structure is preserved; edits reduce redundancy or focus the reading; reasons are contextual.
- **2 — Developing:** Chart is simpler but an important area is missed or an edit is poorly explained.
- **1 — Needs another look:** The editing is arbitrary, removes important structure or retains clutter without justification.

## 6. Instructor notes and reference integration

- **Adam Grimes, *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis*, Chapters 4–6:** central critical reference. It helps the course avoid treating every traditional mark as inherently useful. Use it to prompt scrutiny, not to claim that all support/resistance is useless.
- **Murphy and Schwager/Etzkorn:** conventional support/resistance concepts remain useful background; selecting major turns and range boundaries is not the same as marking every fluctuation.

**Important distinction:** The clean-chart exercise teaches communication and selection. It does not establish that reducing a chart to four zones produces better performance in live trading.

## 7. Website and learning-room handoff

Required experience:
- a clear before image and progressive after states;
- accessible keep/combine/leave-out control for each candidate area;
- explanation revealed when each edit occurs;
- independent practice that hides the reference until submission;
- a brief comparison view and Chart Reading Notes recap.

Do not make the learner drag and delete lots of thin lines on a phone as the only path. A selection list linked to highlighted bands is an acceptable accessible alternative. If a visual before/after slider is used, provide a non-drag option.

## 8. Lesson 7 production checklist

- [ ] Demonstration chart includes genuinely redundant, weakly anchored and contextually irrelevant marks.
- [ ] Every edit has a reason and preserves the intended chart information.
- [ ] Independent chart is different from the demonstration.
- [ ] Four-zone constraint is clearly framed as a practice aid, not a market rule.
- [ ] “Leave out” language is contextual and not permanent.
- [ ] Answer key permits reasoned alternatives where supported.
- [ ] Mobile and keyboard-accessible interactions do not depend on dragging.

# LESSON 08 — Evaluate a Zone Without Inventing a Magic Score

**Module:** 3 — Draw and Evaluate Zones  
**Estimated duration:** 18–22 minutes, including ChartLab Study 2  
**Prerequisites:** Lessons 1–7  
**Format:** Compare evidence → test shortcuts → evaluate context → ChartLab → explain uncertainty → retain  
**Primary asset:** Contrasting candidate zones plus ChartLab Study 2, *Uneven Lows*  
**Do not use:** An automatic zone-strength score, a universal touch-count rule, the instructor's own trade chart, a promise of predictive edge or unsupported claims about who is placing orders

## 1. Instructional decision

This lesson asks students to think carefully about the word **useful**. A zone can be worth noting because it is tied to meaningful structure and contains clear prior reactions. That does not mean the zone has a known probability of holding, and the fact that price has visited it several times is not enough to determine its significance.

The learner should not leave with a five-factor formula or a “strong zone” label. We instead teach a qualitative review that makes the reasoning visible:

- What structural feature anchors the area?
- How clear and coherent are the related reactions?
- What does the movement away from the area show—and what does it not show?
- Where is the area relative to current price and other meaningful areas?
- What remains uncertain or open to another interpretation?

The second ChartLab, **Uneven Lows**, is an intentional step up from Clear W. The lower reactions do not line up neatly, so learners must apply their boundary reasoning without expecting a symmetrical textbook picture. The task is to mark an area that captures the related structure, not to force every low to exactly the same price.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

1. Describe a zone's possible usefulness in qualitative, evidence-based terms.
2. Explain why raw touch count alone does not determine whether a zone is meaningful.
3. Consider the structural anchor, reaction clarity, movement away and current chart context without turning these into a predictive formula.
4. Draw a defensible support zone around imperfectly aligned lows in ChartLab Study 2.
5. Explain what supports their reading and what remains uncertain.

**Not an outcome yet:** calculating the probability a zone will hold, ranking zones with a numerical score, forecasting the next move, using the zone as a trade entry, or claiming the exercise proves a trading edge.

## 3. Student-facing lesson script and reveal timing

### 0:00–0:50 — Reframe the question

**On screen — title:**

> What makes a zone worth paying attention to?

**On screen — copy:**

> You can now choose areas, draw boundaries and remove marks that do not help your reading. The next question is more subtle: why is one area worth paying attention to at all?
>
> We will examine the evidence without turning it into a magic score or pretending a zone can guarantee what price will do.

**Instructor cue:** This lesson is the close of the first three ChartLab-related skills. A small progress note may say: “You have moved from marking to explaining.” Avoid awarding a “mastery” badge; the lesson is formative.

### 0:50–3:20 — Compare two candidate zones

**Show:** Two schematic chart excerpts side by side, both with candidate areas highlighted in a neutral style but no labels saying which is better.

- **Area A:** several crossings/touches, some noisy, overlapping movement and no single clear structural anchor in the shown excerpt.
- **Area B:** fewer visible reactions but a clearer relationship to a turning point or broader range structure, with visible movement away from the area.

Do not make Area B unrealistically perfect, and do not make Area A obviously meaningless. Students should need to reason.

**On-screen prompt:**

> One area has more visible visits. The other has fewer, but its reactions are easier to describe. What would you inspect before deciding which area is more useful for this chart?

**Response options:**
- Count the touches and select the higher number.
- Examine structure, how the reactions behave, and whether the area helps explain the current chart.
- Choose whichever area price touched most recently.
- I would need more context before deciding.

**Feedback for the evidence-based choice:**

> Good. A count is an observation, but it does not explain what those visits mean. We need to look at how the area fits the structure and what the chart actually shows.

**Feedback for “need more context”:**

> That may be the most defensible response. You can describe evidence in the visible excerpt while remaining uncertain about how important the area is in a broader view.

### 3:20–6:20 — Build a qualitative review, one question at a time

**On-screen heading:**

> Five questions—not a score

Reveal each question as a short inspection prompt. Do not number them as points or let the user total them into a percentage.

**Question 1 — What anchors the zone?**

> Does it relate to a meaningful swing, a cluster of related reactions or an edge of a broader range? Be able to point to that structure.

**Question 2 — How clear are the reactions?**

> Are the reactions recognisably related, or are we grouping together price movements that do not really describe the same area?

**Question 3 — What happened after the reactions?**

> Did price visibly move away, pause, overlap or cross repeatedly? Describe the behaviour. Do not assume that a clear departure proves the same thing will happen again.

**Question 4 — Where is the area in the current chart?**

> Is it relevant to the part of the chart we are studying? Are there other areas nearby that change what this one helps us explain?

**Question 5 — What remains uncertain?**

> Could another reasonable reading exist? Is this only a local view? What information is not visible in the chart excerpt?

**Instructor note:** Question 3 is descriptive. Do not tell students that a larger movement away automatically makes a stronger level. Question 4 is contextual, not a rigid timeframe hierarchy. Question 5 should be treated as good analytical practice rather than indecision.

### 6:20–8:00 — Correct the three common shortcuts

Reveal each statement, ask the student to choose **Helpful**, **Not a reliable rule**, or **Needs context**, then show the explanation.

**Shortcut A:** “Three touches makes a level strong.”

**Reveal explanation:**

> Three touches is a count, not a complete explanation. Were the reactions clear? Did they describe the same area? What was price doing around them? The count alone cannot answer that.

**Shortcut B:** “Every additional touch makes the zone stronger.”
