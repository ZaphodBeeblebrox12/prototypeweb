## Shared production rules for this lesson

- Use purpose-built schematic charts with fictional prices. Label each unobtrusively: *“Illustrative chart — not historical market data.”* Do not attach a real instrument or timeframe to fictional data.
- Use controlled examples only. **Do not use the instructor's own trade charts in Lesson 6.** The first personal-chart micro-case remains planned for Lesson 9 or 10, after controlled drawing practice.
- Reference zones and annotations stay hidden until the learner has made and submitted a choice.
- The lesson teaches boundary judgement, not prediction. Do not add entries, stops, targets, signals, trade outcomes, breakout/retest interpretations or claims about participant intent.
- Wicks, bodies and closes provide different views of the same price movement. Do not declare one reference universally correct. Require a reasoned and consistently applied choice.
- Accept a defensible alternative when it captures the relevant reaction area and can be explained. Avoid pixel-perfect grading, arbitrary precision percentages and fabricated confidence scores.
- Treat uncertainty as a legitimate response. One of the examples must deliberately demonstrate how to communicate that its boundaries are less clear.
- Keep student-facing copy separate from production notes. Only text marked **On screen** or **Voiceover** is intended for the learner.
- These are content and UX requirements, not claims that the current website implements any of the described features.

## 1. Instructional decision

Lessons 1–5 established what support/resistance areas describe, how to read structure, how to select useful turns, why zones may have width and why not every candidate area deserves a mark. Lesson 6 focuses on one narrower task: **turn a relevant structural anchor and its related price reactions into a zone with boundaries you can explain.**

The learner should not start by searching for the perfect top and bottom price. They should begin with the structural turn or reaction cluster they are trying to describe. Then they inspect the nearby price action, choose a boundary convention, and test whether the resulting band captures the related behaviour without swallowing unrelated movement.

The lesson shows two legitimate conventions on the same chart:

1. **Wick-aware boundary:** give greater weight to the extremes reached during the relevant candles, while checking whether the band remains useful.
2. **Body/close-aware boundary:** give greater weight to the area occupied by candle bodies or where candles finished, while being explicit about the wick extremes that fall outside it.

These are teaching comparisons, not mutually exclusive systems. A mixed convention can be defensible when its purpose is clear. The learner should be able to state what the chosen area includes, what it excludes, and why that choice fits this chart.

**Design principle:** A defensible zone is not one that can be proven to be uniquely correct. It is one whose structural anchor, boundary logic and width can be explained from the visible price action.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

1. Explain the difference between identifying a swing and selecting the boundaries of a zone around related reactions.
2. Identify the structural anchor that gives a proposed zone its purpose.
3. Compare wick-aware and body/close-aware boundary conventions and describe their trade-offs.
4. Check whether a proposed band captures the reactions it is intended to describe without including excessive unrelated movement.
5. Explain what was deliberately left outside the zone and why.
6. Recognise when the available chart evidence permits more than one reasonable boundary choice.

**Not a learning outcome yet:** using a fixed tick/percentage/ATR width, assigning a zone-strength score, interpreting a breakout, deciding on an entry or stop, or developing a multi-timeframe trading system.

## 3. Student-facing lesson script and reveal timing

The following is proposed learner-facing copy. Production cues, scoring notes and asset requirements are internal guidance and must not appear as a single long block in the student interface.

### 0:00–0:45 — Welcome and set the task

**On screen — title:**

> Set defensible boundaries

**On screen — opening copy:**

> We have already looked at why a zone can be useful and why it does not need to be a perfect line. Now we will decide where to place its upper and lower boundaries.
>
> Start with the price behaviour you want to describe. Then choose boundaries you can explain.

**Production cue:** Move directly to a chart after the short introduction. Do not put all the boundary rules on screen before the learner sees the example.

### 0:45–2:15 — Revisit the difference: swing versus zone

**Show:** L06-A, a clean schematic support example with one clear relative low and several nearby reactions in the same general area. The chart initially has no labels, dots, zone or boundary lines.

**On screen — prompt:**

> What are we trying to mark here?

**Options:**
- The single lowest wick, regardless of the other reactions.
- An area around the related reactions that helps describe the structure.
- A guaranteed price at which the next move will reverse.
- I am not sure yet.

**After response — feedback:**

> A swing helps us identify a turn in the surrounding price sequence. A zone uses the related reactions to describe an area. The turn gives us a place to begin; it does not, by itself, determine every boundary.

**Reveal:** Add a small marker to the structural low, then fade it. Do not show the final zone yet.

**Voiceover:**

> First identify what gives the area its purpose. Here, we are examining the group of reactions around a relative low—not trying to prove that one exact wick price is the only meaningful boundary.

**Instructor note:** This is a concise bridge from Lessons 3–5. Do not repeat the full swing lesson or revisit zone prioritisation at length.

### 2:15–4:30 — Step 1: identify the structural anchor

**Show:** Keep the same chart. Slowly reveal the related lower reactions, one at a time, without any boundary overlay.

**On screen — heading:**

> Step 1 — Identify the structural anchor

**On screen — teaching copy:**

> Ask what this area is meant to describe.
>
> Is it a major turning point? A group of related reactions? An edge of a broad range? Be specific about the price behaviour you are grouping together.

**On screen — prompt:**

> Which evidence best explains why these reactions are being considered together?

**Options:**
- They are related turns around the same broad structural area.
- They all occurred on exactly the same price.
- They guarantee price will turn here next time.
- I am not sure yet.

**Reveal — explanation:**

> These reactions are near one another in the broader structure, even though their exact prices vary. That gives us a reason to consider them as one area.

**Voiceover:**

> A good boundary choice begins with a clear question. If you cannot explain which reactions the zone is meant to represent, adjusting the top and bottom by a few pixels will not solve the underlying problem.

**Production note:** Make the chart geometry show a genuinely related cluster. If the reactions look unrelated once drawn, revise the schematic rather than asking the student to accept the intended grouping.

### 4:30–7:15 — Step 2: compare two legitimate conventions

**Show:** Focus on the same L06-A cluster. The candles, scale and crop stay fixed throughout this section. Display the first candidate boundary only after the learner has seen the underlying candles.

**On screen — heading:**

> Step 2 — Choose what your boundaries represent

**State A — wick-aware boundary**

**Reveal:** Place a subtle band around the related wick extremes and their associated reaction area. The band should include the relevant tails without expanding far into unrelated candles.

**On screen — explanation:**

> **Wick-aware boundary**
>
> This choice gives weight to the extremes price reached during the relevant candles. It can help represent the full span of the reactions, but it may also include short-lived extremes that need explaining.

**Voiceover:**

> A wick records that price travelled to that extreme during the candle. It does not prove why price reached it or guarantee that the same area will matter again.

**State B — body/close-aware boundary**

**Reveal:** Without changing the candles or scale, switch to a narrower alternative focused more on the related bodies and closes. Keep any excluded wick extremes visible so the difference is understandable.

**On screen — explanation:**

> **Body/close-aware boundary**
>
> This choice gives more weight to where the candles opened and finished. It can describe a tighter concentration of price activity, but it leaves some wick extremes outside the band.

**On screen — comparison prompt:**

> Which trade-off can you explain more clearly on this chart?

**Options:**
- The wider band captures more of the wick variation but covers more price space.
- The tighter band focuses on bodies/closes but leaves some wick extremes outside.
- Either convention can be defensible if I explain the choice consistently.
- I am not sure yet.

**Reveal — explanation:**

> Neither convention is the universal answer. The useful question is what each boundary includes and whether that choice describes the reactions you intended to group together.

**Instructor note:** The two conventions are illustrative alternatives. Do not suggest every zone must be strictly wick-only or body/close-only; a reasoned combination can also be acceptable. Do not present “where price spent more time” as directly measurable from a candlestick chart unless the chart actually includes data that supports that statement.

### 7:15–9:30 — Step 3: test the band’s coverage and width

**Show:** Return to L06-A with the two boundary choices available through a toggle. Then introduce a deliberately too-narrow candidate and an overly broad candidate on the exact same candles.

**On screen — heading:**

> Step 3 — Check what the band captures

**State A — too narrow for this example**

**On screen — feedback:**

> This band focuses on one extreme but misses part of the related reaction group. Ask which visible reactions you intended the zone to describe and whether your chosen boundaries leave too much of that evidence outside.

**State B — a defensible reference band**

**On screen — feedback:**

> This band captures the related reactions and still leaves a useful distinction between that cluster and the surrounding movement. Its boundaries can be explained from the chart.

**State C — too broad for this example**

**On screen — feedback:**

> This band includes the reaction cluster, but it also absorbs substantial unrelated movement. It becomes harder to say what the zone represents.

**On screen — prompt:**

> Before keeping a boundary, ask yourself:
>
> - What visible reactions does it include?
> - What evidence does it leave outside?
> - Does its width help describe the area, or blur it with unrelated movement?

**Voiceover:**

> These checks are more useful than searching for a universal number of ticks between the boundaries. Different instruments and chart contexts vary. What matters here is whether the selected band represents the reactions you can point to and whether you can explain the exclusions.

**Instructor note:** Avoid giving an exact-price formula or a mandatory width. Do not label the reference band “the true zone” or suggest that it is objectively the only acceptable answer.

### 9:30–11:15 — Apply the same reasoning to resistance

**Show:** L06-B, a separate schematic chart with related upper reactions and a clear structural anchor. Initial state is unlabelled. Keep the chart scale and candle density comparable to L06-A.

**On screen — prompt:**

> Price has reacted in a similar upper area more than once. Which approach should remain the same?

**Options:**
- Begin with the structural anchor, choose a boundary convention and explain what the area includes.
- Make the zone exactly one wick thick.
- Make the area wide enough to cover every nearby candle.
- I am not sure yet.

**After response — reveal:** Show one defensible resistance band. Then present the alternate wick-aware or body/close-aware version on the same chart.

**On screen — explanation:**

> The direction of the reactions has changed, but the boundary questions remain the same. Identify the relevant cluster, choose a reference for the boundaries, and check that the band remains useful.

**Instructor note:** Do not introduce a reversal pattern name, entry signal, trade direction or claim that upper reactions are more reliable than lower reactions.

### 11:15–12:30 — Model uncertainty with an ambiguous example

**Show:** L06-C, a schematic chart where the apparent reaction cluster is less clean: one wick extends beyond the group, and a nearby candle body partly overlaps the candidate area. Do not reveal the reference zone initially.

**On screen — prompt:**

> This example is less tidy. What is the most careful description of your boundary choice?

**Options:**
- I can choose a provisional area and explain what remains uncertain.
- I must identify one exact boundary even if the evidence is mixed.
- A long wick proves the area must hold.
- I am not sure yet.

**Reveal — explanation:**

> The evidence here is less consistent. One reasonable approach is to mark a provisional area around the clearest related reactions and state which extreme or candle does not fit neatly. Another boundary can also be defensible if its logic is clear.

**Voiceover:**

> A chart does not become clearer because we draw a more confident-looking box. When the reactions do not align neatly, describe that limitation. You can record the area you are examining without pretending that every candle belongs to it.

**Instructor note:** Include this ambiguity on purpose. It is not a trick question and should not be scored as though a single boundary is objectively obvious.

### 12:30–15:30 — Independent practice: draw and defend a zone

**Show:** L06-D, an unseen schematic chart with one reasonably clear cluster of related reactions. It must differ visually from the worked examples. Do not reveal any candidate or reference zone before submission.

**On screen — instructions:**

> Your turn.
>
> 1. Identify the structural anchor.
> 2. Mark a zone around the reactions you want to describe.
> 3. Explain which prices or candle features determine your upper and lower boundaries.
> 4. State what visible evidence you chose not to include and why.

**On screen — written response prompt:**

> **What is the structural anchor? Which prices determine your boundaries? What evidence did you choose not to include?**

**Interaction requirements:**
- Provide a touch-friendly way to select or adjust the upper and lower boundaries. Where drawing is unavailable during content review, let the learner choose among candidate overlays and explain their choice.
- Do not force a precise finger-drag for the entire exercise. Offer keyboard-accessible or simple selection controls where supported.
- Let the learner submit the zone and written explanation before revealing the reference.
- Preserve the learner's attempt so it can be compared after the reveal.
- Include an “I am uncertain about this boundary” option or allow uncertainty to be expressed in the written rationale.

**Reference-answer policy:** The answer key must be finalised only after L06-D's actual candles are drawn. Identify a reference band and explain the anchor, boundary convention, coverage, exclusions and width. Add a reasonable alternative if another band captures the same reaction group coherently.

**Feedback — coherent boundary logic:**

> Your explanation connects the zone to visible reactions and states what its boundaries include. Compare the reference to see where the two choices differ and whether either exclusion changes the structural reading.

**Feedback — unclear structural anchor:**

> Before adjusting the boundaries, state what the zone is meant to describe. Which turn or group of reactions gives the area its purpose?

**Feedback — zone too narrow for the stated purpose:**

> The band may miss some reactions you said it was intended to represent. Recheck the candles just outside your boundaries. Do you want to include them, or explain why they are not part of the same area?

**Feedback — zone too broad for the stated purpose:**

> The band captures the main cluster but also covers a substantial amount of other movement. Consider narrowing it while still representing the reactions you chose to explain.

**Feedback — defensible alternative:**

> Your boundaries differ from the reference, but the choice is explained consistently and captures the relevant reaction area. That is a reasonable alternative. Compare what each version includes and leaves outside.

**Feedback — uncertain chart:**

> It is reasonable to mark a provisional area and identify the evidence that does not fit neatly. Do not hide uncertainty by drawing a more precise-looking box.

**Instructor note:** A written rationale is part of this task. If the interface cannot interpret free-text responses, do not pretend it has automatically assessed reasoning; show the student's explanation beside the reference and let them review the rubric.

### 15:30–17:00 — Knowledge check

Give feedback after each response and allow retries. Do not make the check a high-stakes test.

**Q1. What should come first when setting a zone’s boundaries?**

- Find a fixed number of ticks to use on every chart.
- Identify the structural anchor and the reactions the zone is intended to describe. **Correct.**
- Make the band cover as many candles as possible.

**Feedback:**

> The anchor defines what you are trying to represent. Boundary choices make sense only in relation to that purpose.

**Q2. Which statement about wick-aware and body/close-aware conventions is most accurate?**

- Wick extremes always define the only correct zone.
- Bodies and closes always define the only correct zone.
- Each highlights different aspects of price movement; either may be defensible when its trade-offs are explained. **Correct.**

**Feedback:**

> The chart does not prescribe one boundary convention for every case. Explain what you chose to include and what remains outside.

**Q3. Your zone differs from the reference, but it captures the same reaction group and you can explain its width. What should happen?**

- It should automatically be marked wrong.
- It may be a reasonable alternative and should be compared with the reference. **Correct.**
- The course should assign it a numerical confidence score.

**Feedback:**

> The aim is defensible reasoning, not pixel-perfect imitation. A reference helps explain one choice; it does not make every alternate boundary invalid.

**Completion rule:** Let the learner review the feedback and return to the practice comparison. Do not equate simply opening the lesson with completing the exercise.

### 17:00–18:00 — Close and handoff

**On screen — takeaway:**

> **A defensible zone has a clear anchor, explainable boundaries and useful width.**

**On screen — closing copy:**

> You have now practised explaining not just where a zone sits, but why its boundaries make sense for the reactions you are describing.
>
> Next, we will simplify an over-marked chart while keeping the areas that still contribute useful information.

**Production cue:** The next action goes to Lesson 7. Do not automatically launch ChartLab or request a personal chart at the end of this lesson.

## 4. Chart pack and annotation specification

### L06-A — Support boundary demonstration

**Purpose:** Demonstrate the full boundary-selection process around a relatively clear group of lower reactions.

**Schematic requirements:**
- One identifiable relative low or structural anchor.
- Several related lower reactions in a similar broad area, with modest differences in wick extremes.
- Bodies/closes show a distinguishable but related concentration.
- The cluster is separated enough from unrelated movement that the difference between a defensible band and an overly broad band can be shown.
- No future outcome, breakout, entry, stop, target or indicator.

**Required reveal states:**
1. Plain chart, no labels or zone.
2. Structural anchor highlighted.
3. Related reactions shown one at a time.
4. Wick-aware candidate band.
5. Body/close-aware candidate band.
6. Too-narrow example.
7. Defensible reference band.
8. Too-broad example.
9. Toggle or comparison view with identical candles and scale.

**Annotation register fields:** boundary convention, anchor, included reactions, excluded evidence, width rationale, acceptable alternative(s).

**Alt text:** “Illustrative candlestick chart with several related lower reactions; the reveal compares a band informed by wick extremes with a tighter band informed more by candle bodies and closes.”

### L06-B — Resistance boundary example

**Purpose:** Apply the same reasoning to related upper reactions without making direction the focus.

**Requirements:**
- Several related upper reactions with non-identical wick highs.
- An underlying body/close cluster that allows a meaningful boundary comparison.
- Plain state first; no line or zone visible before the prompt.
- Show one reference band and an alternative convention on the exact same candles.
- No named candlestick pattern, trade direction, outcome or predictive language.

**Alt text:** “Illustrative candlestick chart with several upper reactions in a similar broad area; a shaded band groups the reactions while allowing for different wick highs.”

### L06-C — Deliberately ambiguous boundary example

**Purpose:** Model how to state uncertainty when the reaction group does not align neatly.

**Requirements:**
- Include one wick extreme or candle body that complicates the grouping.
- Ensure at least one provisional zone is plausible, but avoid a single obviously perfect boundary.
- Provide a reference interpretation plus at least one defensible alternative.
- Write a note explicitly describing what is uncertain and which candle/price feature creates that ambiguity.
- Do not label the ambiguity as student error.

**Alt text:** “Illustrative candlestick chart with a partly overlapping reaction group and one wick extending beyond the cluster, showing why zone boundaries can remain uncertain.”

### L06-D — Independent practice chart

**Purpose:** Assess whether the learner can connect a boundary choice to a visible structural anchor.

**Requirements:**
- Unseen chart, visually distinct from L06-A and L06-B.
- One primary reaction cluster that supports a defensible zone, plus at least one meaningful feature that the learner must decide whether to include.
- Base chart, submission state, hidden reference band, revealed reference rationale and acceptable-alternative note.
- The asset writer must freeze the final candle geometry before setting the reference zone or writing the key.
- Keep chart marks legible at mobile width and provide text guidance for students who cannot use a drawing interaction.

**Alt text:** “Illustrative candlestick chart with a clear reaction cluster and nearby price movement; the learner selects a boundary area and explains which evidence it includes or excludes.”

## 5. Model answers and scoring notes

### Short rubric

Review the learner's answer against four dimensions. Use descriptive feedback, not a single numeric score.

1. **Structural anchor:** Has the learner explained which turn or reaction group the zone describes?
2. **Boundary logic:** Has the learner stated which wick, body or close evidence informed the upper and lower edges?
3. **Useful width:** Does the band capture the intended reactions without including a large amount of unrelated movement?
4. **Consistency and exclusions:** Can the learner explain what was left outside and apply the boundary convention coherently?

### Full-credit response

The learner identifies the intended reaction group, explains the boundary convention, describes relevant included/excluded evidence and acknowledges uncertainty where the chart is mixed. A drawing that differs slightly from the reference may still receive full credit if the rationale is clear and the zone remains useful.

### Partial response

The learner identifies a reasonable area, but gives a weak explanation such as “it looks right” or cannot say which reactions the boundaries are meant to represent. Feedback should ask for the anchor or excluded evidence rather than simply instructing the learner to widen/narrow the band.

### Needs review

The learner treats one wick or close as a mandatory boundary in every case, cannot connect the zone to a structural anchor, draws a band so narrow it omits the stated reaction group, or draws a band so broad that the intended area loses meaning.

### Acceptable alternatives

A wick-aware, body/close-aware or mixed convention may be defensible if it is appropriate for the chart, applied consistently and explained. The answer key should say why the reference is preferred for this example and when an alternate boundary remains reasonable.

### Do not score

- Pixel-perfect agreement with the reference.
- Whether price later reacts in the area.
- A prediction about future price.
- A universal numerical boundary width.
- An invented confidence or zone-strength score.
