# TradeThesis Support & Resistance
## Lesson 01 Production Script — What Support and Resistance Actually Tell You

**Document role:** Production-ready content brief for the first student-facing lesson  
**Curriculum authority:** `tradethesis_support_resistance_curriculum_blueprint.md`  
**Status:** Draft for curriculum review; no website or repository changes authorised  
**Planned duration:** 12–15 minutes  
**Learner level:** Beginner; knows how to read a basic candlestick chart but has not studied support/resistance  
**Lesson format:** Observe → name → reveal → test a counterexample → practise → retain

---

## 1. Instructional decision

**Lesson 1 uses controlled, purpose-built illustrative charts only. Do not use the instructor's own trade charts and do not launch ChartLab here.**

The student is learning what the concept means, not yet learning how to select boundaries, rank zones or act on price behaviour. A personal trade brings too many distractions at once: instrument, timeframe, trade direction, entry, indicators, risk, outcome and the instructor's own style. ChartLab also asks the student to perform a drawing skill before the underlying concept has been sufficiently established.

Use two simple charts that expose one idea at a time. The student observes first. Explanations and annotations appear only after a response. ChartLab Study 1, **Clear W**, remains in Lesson 5, after structure, swing points, zones and prioritisation have been introduced.

**Design principle:** Teach the learner to describe what is visible before asking them to predict what comes next.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

1. Explain support and resistance in plain language as areas associated with previous price behaviour.
2. Point to visible evidence for why an area might be worth watching (for example, price previously paused or turned near it).
3. Explain why a support/resistance area is not a guarantee, a precise law or an automatic trade signal.
4. Separate an observation (“price turned near this area twice”) from an inference (“buyers must be accumulating here”).

**Not an outcome yet:** drawing precise zone boundaries, judging a zone's relative quality, applying multiple timeframes, identifying an entry, setting a stop, or deciding whether to trade. Those skills belong in later lessons.

## 3. Student-facing lesson script and reveal timing

The text below is the proposed wording shown to the learner. Instructor cues and production instructions are marked separately so they are not accidentally displayed as lesson copy.

### 0:00–0:45 — Welcome and set the task

**On screen — title:**

> What support and resistance actually tell you

**On screen — opening copy:**

> Before we draw lines on a chart, let's learn what we are looking for.
>
> You do not need to know the answer yet. First, observe where price has been moving and what happened when it returned to certain areas.

**Instructor/production cue:** Do not show a definition, a highlighted zone, a ticker symbol, an entry arrow or an outcome at this point. The learner should not have to read a long introduction before seeing the chart.

### 0:45–2:30 — Chart A, unlabelled observation

**Show:** Chart A in its plain state. It should contain two or three visible occasions where declining price slows, pauses or turns upward in roughly the same broad area. No zone, line, arrows or explanatory labels appear yet.

**On screen — prompt:**

> Look at the chart. Without trying to predict the next move, what stands out about the places where price pauses, turns or moves away?

**Response options:**
- Price reacted around a similar area more than once.
- Price is moving mostly in one direction.
- I see no clear pattern yet.
- I'm not sure.

**Reveal rule:** Capture the selection, but do not mark the answer incorrect. This is an observation prompt, not a graded test. If a free-text option is supported, accept a short explanation too.

**After response — on-screen feedback:**

> Good chart reading starts with describing what is visible. More than one interpretation may be reasonable at this stage. We are looking for evidence, not trying to guess the future.

**Instructor note:** Do not say a repeated reaction proves that a zone will work again. The only point being established is that related reactions can make an area worth examining.

### 2:30–4:30 — Name the idea: support

**Reveal:** Add a soft, horizontal band over the broad area containing the prior lower reactions. Avoid a hairline that implies an exact, uniquely correct price. Label the area **“prior reaction area”** first; then reveal the term **“support area.”**

**On screen — teaching copy:**

> **Support** is an area where falling price has previously slowed, paused or turned upward.
>
> We use the word to describe what price did around that area in the history shown on the chart. It does not mean price must stop there the next time.

**Instructor/voiceover notes:**
- Explain the shaded band's extent by pointing to the visible reactions; do not teach wick/body boundary rules yet.
- Say “previously reacted” rather than asserting what unseen participants were doing.
- Avoid “buyers defended the level,” “institutions stepped in,” or “smart money protected the zone.” Those statements go beyond what this chart alone can establish.

**Optional hover/expand note (not required to pass):**

> You will learn how to choose the boundaries of a zone later. For now, notice that the reactions occupy an area, not one perfectly exact price.

This preview is intentionally brief. The full “zones, not perfect lines” treatment remains Lesson 4.

### 4:30–6:15 — Chart B: resistance as the counterpart

**Show:** A second, equally clean illustration. Price rises into a broad area, pauses or turns downward on more than one occasion. Initially, show it without annotations.

**On screen — prompt:**

> Now look at a similar situation from the other direction. What happened when price rose into this area?

**Response options:**
- It paused or turned down around a similar area.
- It moved through without a clear pause.
- I can't tell from this excerpt.

**Reveal:** Add a broad band around the prior upper reactions. Reveal the label **“resistance area.”**

**On screen — teaching copy:**

> **Resistance** is an area where rising price has previously slowed, paused or turned downward.
>
> Support and resistance describe prior price behaviour. They are not a promise about the next move.

**Instructor/production cue:** Keep Chart B similar in size and visual complexity to Chart A. Do not add a trend indicator or pattern name. The student should see the relationship between the two concepts without being asked to learn several new concepts at once.

### 6:15–8:30 — The important counterexample: a zone can be crossed

**Show:** Return to one of the two illustrations (preferably Chart A) and extend the price sequence with later candles moving clearly through the previously highlighted area. Do not show those later candles until the student has seen and interpreted the earlier reactions.

**On screen — prompt before revealing the extension:**

> Based only on the earlier candles, we marked an area where price had reacted. What do you think we should expect the marking to guarantee?

**Options:**
- It guarantees price will turn there again.
- It marks an area worth watching, but the next move is uncertain.
- It guarantees that a trade should be taken there.

After the learner answers, reveal the additional candles crossing the band.

**On screen — feedback:**

> Price has now moved through the area. That does not erase the earlier reactions; it shows that the area did not contain this move.
>
> A support or resistance area is a way to organise what we observe. It is not an unbreakable floor or ceiling, and it does not tell us by itself when to enter a trade.

**Instructor note:** Do not teach breakout confirmation, retests, role reversal or failed breakouts here. The example is only to disprove certainty. Those concepts are taught in Lessons 12–14.

### 8:30–10:15 — Observation versus explanation

**On screen — title:**

> What can the chart tell us — and what can it not tell us?

**On-screen two-column reveal:**

**Visible observation**
- Price turned upward near this area on two earlier occasions.
- Price later crossed the same area.

**Possible interpretation, not proven by the chart alone**
- “Large institutions must be buying here.”
- “The zone will hold next time.”
- “A trade here is certain to work.”

**Student-facing copy:**

> A chart shows us price behaviour. It does not show the full reason for every move or the intentions of every participant.
>
> Good analysis begins with evidence. We can form a scenario, but we must leave room for price to behave differently.

**Instructor note:** This is an important course-wide discipline. It should be reinforced in later lessons and case studies. Do not imply that traders can never reason about order flow; simply teach that the chart excerpt alone does not prove hidden motives.

### 10:15–12:30 — Short practice

**Show:** Two small, previously unseen illustrative chart excerpts labelled **A** and **B**. Chart A includes multiple pauses/turns in a broad area. Chart B includes a single close pass by a price level with no clear repeated reaction.

**On-screen question:**

> Which chart gives you clearer visible evidence of a prior reaction area? Choose one, then explain what you observed.

**Expected answer:** Chart A, because it shows more than one visible reaction around a similar area. The answer must not claim that the area is guaranteed to hold or that the chart proves who caused the reaction.

**Feedback if A selected with a reason:**

> Yes. You referred to what the chart shows: more than one reaction around a similar area. That makes it a reasonable area to pay attention to, not a guaranteed future turning point.

**Feedback if B selected:**

> Look again at how many times price visibly reacted around the area. A single pass may still be relevant in some contexts, but this exercise asks which excerpt shows clearer evidence of repeated prior reactions.

**Feedback if “I'm not sure”:**

> That is acceptable when the evidence is unclear. In chart reading, recognising uncertainty is better than inventing certainty. Compare the visible reactions and decide whether the evidence is strong enough to make a tentative observation.

### 12:30–13:30 — Knowledge check and takeaway

**On-screen takeaway:**

> **A zone is an area of interest, not a prediction.**

**On-screen knowledge check:**

**Q1. Which is the best description of support?**
- A guaranteed floor where price cannot fall further.
- An area where falling price previously slowed, paused or turned upward.
- A signal to buy whenever price touches it.

**Correct:** Second option.

**Q2. Price moves through an area that had previously acted as support. What can we conclude from this chart alone?**
- The original marking was necessarily useless.
- The market was manipulated by institutions.
- The area did not contain this move; the earlier reactions still occurred, and the new evidence matters.

**Correct:** Third option.

**Q3. Which statement is an observation rather than an unsupported explanation?**
- “Price turned upward near this area twice.”
- “Institutions bought here to defend the level.”
- “Price must reverse here again.”

**Correct:** First option.

**Completion behaviour:** Show brief feedback after each answer. Do not use high-stakes scoring or block course navigation for this introductory check. Require the learner to review a missed answer before marking the lesson complete, but allow retry.

## 4. Chart asset specifications

No chart image is being commissioned or approved by this document. These are the exact asset requirements for the later visual-production phase.

### Chart A — Repeated lower reaction area

- **Purpose:** Introduce the observable behaviour associated with support.
- **Format:** Purpose-built schematic candlestick sequence (fictional prices), unless a verified historical chart is deliberately selected later.
- **Visible state 1:** Price approaches a broad lower area, pauses/turns upward, returns later and reacts again. The chart must have enough candles to understand the sequence, but only one lesson point should stand out.
- **Visible state 2:** Same chart with a subtle band labelled “prior reaction area.”
- **Visible state 3:** Same chart with label “support area.”
- **Visible state 4:** Extend the sequence with later candles crossing decisively through the band.
- **Do not include:** entry/exit arrows, target/stop, volume, indicators, instrument logo, watermarks, future annotations visible before the reveal, or language suggesting a trade signal.
- **Required asset variants:** unlabelled PNG/SVG; band-only; band + label; later-candles extension; mobile crop with readable candles.

### Chart B — Repeated upper reaction area

- **Purpose:** Introduce resistance as the counterpart concept.
- **Format:** Schematic sequence with price rising into an area and pausing/turning down on more than one occasion.
- **Reveal states:** unlabelled → band → “resistance area.”
- **Do not include:** neckline, double-top pattern label, entry arrows or extra concepts not taught in this lesson.

### Practice cards A and B

- Two simple, unseen variants that test recognition of prior reactions rather than exact boundary drawing.
- Chart A should present clearer repeated evidence; Chart B should have weaker or more ambiguous evidence, not be intentionally deceptive.
- Both must allow the response “I am not sure” or an uncertainty-based explanation.

### Source and rights metadata

Every final asset must be entered in a register with: asset ID, author/creator, whether it is schematic or historical, instrument, timeframe, date/range if historical, OHLC/data source, intended lesson/use, alt text, mobile crop, annotation layers and rights/permission status. If schematic, label it **“Illustrative chart — not historical market data.”** If historical, do not imply it is a real trade taken by the instructor unless that is true and documented.

## 5. Assessment and answer-key policy

This lesson assesses conceptual understanding, not drawing precision or forecasting skill.

**Full-credit reasoning includes:**
- naming an observable prior reaction or pause;
- understanding that the area is approximate/interpretive, not a perfect law; and
- not claiming future certainty or hidden motive based only on the chart.

**Partial credit:** The learner identifies a relevant area but makes an overconfident statement. Feedback should correct the inference without discarding the observation.

**Not acceptable as a model answer:** “The strongest level has three touches,” “institutions defended the zone,” “the price has to reverse,” or “once broken, the original zone was never valid.” These add unsupported rules or certainty.

**Reasonable uncertainty:** If the excerpt is too short or the reactions do not cluster clearly, “not enough evidence” is a valid chart-reading decision. Avoid forced certainty merely to make the quiz easy to grade.

## 6. Website/learning-room handoff

Lesson 1 should feel like a guided editorial lesson, not an administrative form or trading terminal.

**Required visible components:**
1. Lesson title and a short purpose line.
2. Plain chart area with concise question.
3. A compact response control.
4. “Reveal explanation” state after submission.
5. A second chart / extension used for the counterexample.
6. Short practice question with answer feedback.
7. Takeaway text and a three-question check.
8. Mark-complete action, with the ability to retry the check.

**Progressive reveal states:**
- State 1: unlabelled Chart A; no definition visible.
- State 2: Chart A with prior reaction band.
- State 3: band labelled support.
- State 4: Chart B unlabelled.
- State 5: Chart B with band and resistance label.
- State 6: added candles cross the earlier zone.
- State 7: observation-vs-inference comparison.
- State 8: independent practice and feedback.
- State 9: takeaway + knowledge check.

**UX rules:**
- Keep the active text near the chart it explains. On mobile, stack the chart and question; do not force side-by-side comparisons at narrow widths.
- Preserve the last chart state when the learner advances to a reveal; avoid making the learner mentally compare two unrelated images.
- The learner should be able to revisit the explanation after answering.
- Do not show a “zone strength” score, predictive probability, trade-entry CTA or a “correct drawing” badge in this lesson.
- Account-backed progress and cross-device persistence are platform requirements from the master blueprint, but they are outside this lesson content document and have not been implemented here.

## 8. Reference notes for the instructor — not the main lesson script

Keep references accessible in a modest “Further reading / Where this idea comes from” drawer. Do not front-load citations or research jargon into the beginner's first minute.

### John J. Murphy — *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets*

**Role:** Conventional technical-analysis foundation and terminology. Use this source to explain the established concept of support/resistance in a language familiar to chart readers. It does not remove the need to question whether a particular marked area is useful in a particular chart.

Reference: [Penguin Random House — book page](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/350647/technical-analysis-of-the-financial-markets-by-john-j-murphy/).

### Adam Grimes — *The Art & Science of Technical Analysis*, Chapter 4, “On Trading Ranges”

**Role:** Critical balance. Wiley's chapter summary says price action around support/resistance is complex and questions conventional assumptions; it notes that many levels traders use may be no better than random levels. Use that idea to stop the lesson making a guarantee out of an annotation.

**How to say this to students, if needed:** “A familiar-looking level is not automatically useful. First ask what visible evidence supports your marking; later we will discuss how context changes its relevance.” Avoid a blanket claim that support/resistance never works.

Reference: [Wiley — Chapter 4](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119202837.ch4).

### Jack D. Schwager and Mark Etzkorn — *A Complete Guide to the Futures Market*, Chapter 8, “Support and Resistance”

**Role:** The established account of support/resistance in relation to trading ranges, major highs/lows and concentrations of prior highs/lows. The book also discusses possible role reversal after a range break. Teach the basic vocabulary here; leave role reversal for Lesson 13.

Reference: [Wiley — Chapter 8](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119209713.ch8).

### Carol L. Osler — “Support for Resistance: Technical Analysis and Intraday Exchange Rates” (2000)

**Role:** Evidence literacy only. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York summary reports that levels supplied by six FX firms helped predict intraday trend interruptions, while predictive power varied by exchange rate and firm. This is specific FX research, not proof that every manually drawn zone across assets and timeframes has predictive value.

**Instruction:** Do not lead Lesson 1 with a statistic or say “science proves support/resistance works.” If included, put the research in the reference drawer after the learner has understood the concept and state the study's scope plainly.

Reference: [Federal Reserve Bank of New York — research article](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.html).

### Source discipline

All lesson copy and chart artwork must be original to TradeThesis. Reference and link to the books; do not reproduce their prose, chart images, diagrams or proprietary examples. A citation establishes where an idea was discussed, not that every trading rule commonly associated with it is proven.
