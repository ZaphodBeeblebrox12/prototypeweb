# TradeThesis Support & Resistance
## Lesson 09 Production Script — Read the Approach to a Zone

**Document role:** Student-facing lesson and production handoff  
**Curriculum position:** Module 4 — Read Price Behaviour  
**Estimated duration:** 15–18 minutes  
**Format:** Compare controlled charts → describe the approach → test the idea → first instructor-chart micro-case → retain  
**Asset rule:** This is the first appearance of your own chart, but only as a short, pre-trade micro-case. Do not reveal your entry, stop, target, management or outcome.

---

## 1. Instructional decision: why your chart appears here—and only briefly

Lessons 1–8 taught the learner to recognise structure, identify turns, select zones and make defensible drawings. They have also completed guided ChartLab work. Lesson 9 is therefore the right moment to connect the skill to your actual chart reading—but not yet to explain a whole trade.

The first personal chart must answer one narrow question: **what can we learn from the way price approaches an area?** It should not ask students simultaneously to understand your strategy, choose a trade direction, assess entry timing, interpret indicators, calculate risk and judge a result.

The sequence is deliberate:

1. Controlled comparison charts teach the new concept without your personal style becoming the answer key.
2. Students describe what they observe before the zone is reached.
3. Only after this do they see a cropped section of your chart, with your markings and future price hidden.
4. They make their own observation; then your zone and reasoning are revealed.
5. The example stops before entry or outcome. Full trade construction remains for Lessons 16–17.

**Do not use the outcome as proof that your zone was “right.”** The point is the quality and limits of the information available at that time.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end, the learner should be able to:

- Describe the path price takes toward a zone using observable language (for example, directional movement, overlapping candles, swing sequence or pauses).
- Identify at least one contextual detail that matters and one uncertainty that remains.
- Explain why the way price approaches a zone can inform a scenario but does not determine the outcome.
- Compare their interpretation with your interpretation without assuming identical markings or a single certain forecast.

**Not yet expected:** deciding whether to trade, identifying an entry trigger, forecasting a breakout, applying a universal multiple-timeframe formula, or assessing the complete management of a real trade.

## 3. Student-facing lesson script and reveal sequence

### 0:00–0:45 — Open on a practical question

**On screen — title:**

> Read the approach to a zone

**On screen — copy:**

> A zone gives us an area to pay attention to. But price does not arrive there in the same way every time.
>
> Today, we will look at the journey toward an area—not just the area itself.

**Production note:** A small progress line may show “Lesson 9 of 18.” Do not add a triumphant animation. The learner should feel they are entering a more applied stage, not that they have already mastered the skill.

### 0:45–3:00 — Controlled Chart A: a more directional approach

**Chart A brief:** Use an illustrative, unlabelled candlestick sequence moving toward a pre-identified resistance zone. The approach should show a comparatively directional sequence, relatively limited overlap and a sequence of advancing swing points. Do not show the eventual interaction with the zone in the initial view. A faint, neutral band may be visible as the destination, but do not annotate the route.

**On screen — prompt:**

> Before price reaches the area, describe the path it is taking. What do you notice about the candles and the recent swings?

**Response choices:**
- Price is advancing with relatively little overlap.
- Price is moving back and forth with substantial overlap.
- The excerpt is too short for me to tell.

Allow a short free-text response if the lesson platform supports it.

**After response — feedback:**

> You are describing the approach, not predicting the result. Keep those two jobs separate.

**Reveal sequence:**
1. Highlight the sequence of recent swing points.
2. Highlight the comparatively directional run toward the band.
3. Keep future candles hidden.

**Student-facing explanation:**

> Here, price has moved toward the area in a relatively directional way. That is an observation about the route it has taken so far.
>
> It does **not** tell us that the area must break. Price could continue, pause, turn away or produce mixed evidence. The approach gives us context; it does not give us certainty.

**Instructor note:** Do not use “strong buyers are pushing price” unless clearly framed as shorthand rather than something directly proven by the candles. Prefer “price has advanced with limited overlap.”

### 3:00–5:15 — Controlled Chart B: a hesitant, overlapping approach

**Chart B brief:** Use a second schematic chart approaching a comparably clear area. This time the path includes overlapping candles, short back-and-forth moves and less clean directional progress. Keep scale and visual styling similar to Chart A, so differences in the approach are visible without decoration.

**On screen — prompt:**

> The destination is similar. What differs in the path price takes to get there?

**After the learner responds, reveal:**
- A subtle bracket over the overlapping sequence.
- The recent local swing points.
- The target zone, without showing the eventual reaction.

**Student-facing explanation:**

> This approach has more overlap and less consistent progress in one direction. That tells us something about the route, but not the result.
>
> Avoid turning descriptions into automatic rules. A directional approach does not guarantee a break, and an overlapping approach does not guarantee a rejection. We are building context, not a formula.

**Compare interaction:** Ask the learner to finish both statements:
- “On Chart A, I noticed …”
- “On Chart B, I noticed …”

**Model response:** Chart A advanced more directionally with fewer overlapping candles; Chart B moved back and forth more. Both remained uncertain as to what would happen at the zone.

**Acceptable alternatives:** Students may refer to the swing sequence or the degree of overlap, if these observations match the actual chart. Do not accept predictions presented as facts (“A will break; B will reverse”) as complete answers.

### 5:15–7:30 — Teach four things to observe before the zone

Reveal the following one at a time. Do not put all four labels on the chart at once.

**On screen — heading:**

> Four observations worth making

**1. Direction and swing sequence**

> Is price making progressively higher or lower turns, or has it been moving sideways? Describe the recent structure before you interpret it.

**2. Overlap and pauses**

> Is price moving relatively directly, or spending more time moving back and forth? Overlap is an observation, not a standalone trading signal.

**3. Nearby structure**

> Is there another meaningful area nearby? The room between price and the next opposing area may matter if we later consider a scenario.

**4. Wider context**

> Does the local move sit inside a broader trend or trading range? A wider chart can add context, but it does not automatically overrule the local evidence.

**Instructor notes:**
- Avoid teaching mandatory timeframe alignment, fixed lookback periods, or “three confirmations” as a universal rule.
- Do not introduce indicators here. The learner's job is to describe price structure.
- Keep the idea of “space to the next zone” descriptive. Trade planning and reward/risk belong in Lesson 15.

### 7:30–8:30 — Ask what still remains unknown

**On screen — prompt:**

> Before we reveal what happens next, name one thing the chart tells you and one thing it does not tell you yet.

**Model answer:** It tells me how price has approached the area and what the recent swing sequence looks like. It does not tell me whether price will reject or break the area, or whether a trade should be taken.

**Feedback:**

> That distinction is useful. A good chart reader can describe what is visible and leave the rest open until there is more evidence.

Do not show the subsequent candles yet. The point is to make uncertainty explicit before the reveal.

### 8:30–11:30 — First instructor-chart micro-case

**Asset requirements:** One of your actual historical charts, cropped to show enough context to understand the current structure and the approach to a relevant zone. The initial image must be captured at a point before the zone interaction. Hide your markings, future candles, entry/exit labels, stop, target, P&L and final outcome. Record instrument, timeframe, date/time of the screenshot and what was knowable at that moment in the private asset register.

**Show initial state:** Plain chart only. If the zone itself is not obvious without your mark, do not draw it in advance; students should make a first reading.

**On screen — copy:**

> This time, the chart comes from my own trading. I am going to show you only the information available at this point—not what happened afterward.
>
> You do not need to agree with my eventual analysis. First, make your own observation.

**Prompt:**

> How is price approaching the area you think matters? Name one piece of evidence and one uncertainty.

**Response requirement:** One sentence for the observed evidence; one sentence for the uncertainty.

**Only after the response, reveal your markings.**

**Instructor narration template — personalise it truthfully:**

> “I marked this area because [describe the visible structure or prior reaction]. Before price reached it, I noticed [describe the approach without predicting the outcome]. One thing I did not know yet was [state a genuine uncertainty]. I was not treating the zone as a guarantee.”

Do not fill these blanks with invented rationale. They must be based on the actual trade and chart at that moment.

**After the reveal, show this note:**

> Your drawing may differ from mine and still be defensible. Compare the evidence and the boundaries, not just the shape of the mark.

**Hard stop:** End the micro-case before the entry decision or outcome. Do not click forward to the later candles. Say:

> We will study complete trade decisions later. For now, we are practising one part of that process: reading the approach without pretending we already know the result.

### 11:30–14:30 — Independent practice

**Controlled practice chart:** A third, unseen approach to a zone. Keep the eventual reaction hidden.

**Question 1:** Which description best fits the approach?
- Relatively directional with limited overlap.
- Overlapping and hesitant.
- Too little information to make a useful description.

**Question 2 — free response:** What contextual detail would you check next, and what remains uncertain?

**Model answer:** Any description consistent with the displayed sequence can earn credit. A strong response names a visible feature (for example, recent swing progression or overlap), identifies nearby structure or broader context as a possible next check, and states that the reaction at the zone is not known yet.

**Scoring:**
- 1 point: accurate observation.
- 1 point: identifies relevant context or another area.
- 1 point: states a real uncertainty rather than treating the outcome as guaranteed.

**Feedback if the learner makes a firm prediction:**

> That is one possible scenario, but the candles shown do not establish it as fact. What can you point to on the chart, and what would you still need to observe?

### 14:30–15:30 — Takeaway and close

**On screen — takeaway:**

> **Read the route, not just the destination.** The approach to a zone adds context, but it does not determine what happens next.

**Short reflection:**

> Next time you look at a chart, describe the last few swings and the path toward a zone before drawing a conclusion about the reaction.

**Bridge to Lesson 10:**

> We have looked at the path toward an area. Next, we will watch what happens when price actually reaches it—and learn why one wick or candle is rarely enough to settle the question.

## 4. Assessment and feedback policy

The learner demonstrates the lesson objective when they can describe an approach using chart-visible evidence, name contextual information worth checking, and state at least one uncertainty. Exact agreement with the instructor's zone is not required.

Do not award full marks for a direction prediction alone, even if the hidden next move happens to match it. The assessed skill is evidence-based description, not lucky anticipation.

## 5. Instructor notes and reference rationale

- **Adam Grimes, *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis*, Chapters 4–5:** use the treatment of trading ranges and transitions between trends and ranges to discourage mechanical “approach type means outcome” rules. Reference: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/9781119202837
- **Brian Shannon, *Technical Analysis Using Multiple Timeframes*:** supplementary support for checking broader context. Do not convert multiple-timeframe context into a universal checklist. Reference: https://books.google.com/books/about/Technical_Analysis_Using_Multiple_Timefr.html?id=b-Eo0AEACAAJ
- The instructor's actual chart is a worked example of personal reasoning, not evidence that a zone necessarily has predictive power. Label the distinction between visible observation and your own interpretation.

## 6. Website / learning-room handoff

- Keep the controlled chart and the instructor micro-case as separate lesson states.
- Instructor chart must have a strict pre-reveal state; future candles and instructor marks must not be exposed via the default image, alt preview or page source presented to the learner before answer submission.
- Provide an “Evidence I observed” input and a separate “What I do not know yet” input.
- Instructor annotations reveal only after submission. The learner's response stays visible beside the explanation.
- No ChartLab launch in this lesson; ChartLab practice has already taken place in earlier lessons.
- Do not store or show the instructor trade's outcome on this page. Full case-study interactions are reserved for Lessons 16–17.

## 7. Asset production checklist

- [ ] Controlled Chart A: relatively directional approach; later interaction masked.
- [ ] Controlled Chart B: overlapping approach; later interaction masked.
- [ ] Instructor micro-case at an authentic pre-interaction point, with metadata recorded privately.
- [ ] Instructor version with zone and rationale annotations, but without trade outcome.
- [ ] Unseen controlled practice chart with answer key.
- [ ] Mobile crops checked for readable candles and enough structural context.
- [ ] Confirm all chart labels describe visible facts rather than inferred motives.

# TradeThesis Support & Resistance
## Lesson 10 Production Script — Rejection versus Acceptance

**Document role:** Student-facing lesson and production handoff  
**Curriculum position:** Module 4 — Read Price Behaviour  
**Estimated duration:** 17–19 minutes  
**Format:** Inspect → commit to a provisional read → reveal candles progressively → update → classify practice sequences  
**Asset rule:** Use controlled sequences first. The Lesson 9 instructor micro-case may be revisited briefly, but keep entry and outcome hidden.

---

## 1. Instructional decision

This lesson introduces a vocabulary for describing the interaction between price and a zone. The vocabulary must not become a new set of mechanical signals.

For this course:

- **Rejection evidence** means price tests or enters an area and then moves away from it in the candles shown.
- **Acceptance evidence** means price shows some evidence of holding or establishing activity beyond the area—such as a close beyond it followed by continued action there. The precise evidence must be judged in context; there is no single universally sufficient candle.
- **Inconclusive** means the visible sequence is too short, mixed or conflicting to support a confident classification.

These are descriptive labels for reading chart evidence, not guaranteed predictors and not a claim about the hidden intentions of market participants. They must be taught by revealing price one stage at a time so learners experience how a sensible reading changes as information arrives.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end, learners should be able to:

1. Distinguish a wick that passes through a zone from a confirmed pattern of price holding beyond it.
2. Describe evidence consistent with rejection, possible acceptance or an inconclusive interaction.
3. Update a provisional interpretation after seeing a close and subsequent candles.
4. Explain why one candle alone rarely resolves the interpretation.

**Not expected:** calling every wick a rejection, using a fixed number of closes as a universal rule, predicting continuation, or turning rejection/acceptance labels into entries.

## 3. Student-facing script and reveal sequence

### 0:00–0:50 — Open with the question

**On screen — title:**

> Rejection or acceptance?

**On screen — copy:**

> Price has reached an area we care about. What happens next matters—but one candle rarely tells the whole story.
>
> We will read the evidence as it arrives, rather than looking at the finished chart and pretending the answer was obvious.

### 0:50–2:40 — Sequence A, first candle only

**Chart A brief:** Show prior structure and a clearly defined broad zone. Reveal a candle that trades through part of the zone with a wick, but whose body closes back within or on the near side of the band. Do not reveal subsequent candles.

**On screen — prompt:**

> What can we say from this candle alone?

Options:
- The candle guarantees rejection.
- It shows price traded through part of the area and then closed back away from the extreme.
- It proves price has accepted beyond the zone.
- There is not enough information to know what happens next.

Allow two choices: the factual observation and “not enough information about the next move” are both valid. Avoid grading this as a single-word pattern test.

**Feedback:**

> We can describe the candle's range and close. We cannot yet know whether later price action will reinforce this as rejection, establish activity beyond the zone, or remain mixed.

**Instructor note:** Do not label the wick “stop hunting” or claim it proves a group of traders defended the area.

### 2:40–4:30 — Reveal the close and annotate the candle

**Reveal:** Add small arrows for the high/low excursion and closing location. Do not add a large red/green label saying “rejection.”

**Student-facing explanation:**

> A wick tells us price traded at those prices during the candle. The close tells us where the candle finished. Both are useful observations, but neither tells the entire story by itself.
>
> A move beyond the zone followed by a close back inside can be evidence consistent with rejection. We still need to see what price does afterward.

**Instructor note:** Be careful with candle-close language because data feeds, session times and candle construction can differ. The lesson should describe the chart displayed, not imply a universal law.

### 4:30–6:30 — Reveal follow-through: Sequence A

**Reveal:** Add the next two or three candles. In this sequence, price moves away from the zone and does not immediately reclaim the area.

**Prompt:**

> With these candles added, how would you describe the evidence now?

Options:
- Evidence consistent with rejection has strengthened.
- Price has clearly established itself beyond the zone.
- The evidence remains entirely unchanged.
- I need to inspect the sequence again.

**Model response:** “The close back away from the area followed by movement away strengthens the interpretation that this interaction was a rejection. It still does not guarantee that the zone will hold on another visit.”

**Feedback:**

> Your interpretation can become better supported without becoming certain. We are describing the sequence we can see—not promising the next one.

### 6:30–8:40 — Sequence B: close beyond the band, then continued action

**Chart B brief:** Similar visual layout but a different outcome. Price crosses the band and closes beyond it. Reveal only that candle first, then ask the learner to respond. After the initial response, reveal two additional candles that continue to spend time or close beyond the zone.

**Prompt after first candle:**

> Price has closed beyond the zone. Is that enough on its own to say the area has been accepted?

**Model response:** The close is relevant evidence that price finished beyond the zone, but one candle does not settle the classification.

**Reveal follow-through. Then prompt:**

> What changed after the next candles appeared?

**Model response:** Continued action beyond the zone makes acceptance a more reasonable current description than it was after the first candle alone. This remains a contextual judgement; price could still return through the zone.

**Student-facing copy:**

> **Acceptance is not a magic candle.** We are looking for evidence that price is spending time or continuing to act beyond an area, rather than just passing through it briefly.

**Instructor note:** Use “evidence consistent with acceptance” where possible. Do not set a rigid universal rule such as “two closes equals acceptance” unless a later method is clearly identified as your personal rule rather than a general law.

### 8:40–10:20 — Sequence C: conflicting evidence

**Chart C brief:** Price crosses the area, closes beyond it, then returns to the band and moves back and forth. The excerpt should remain genuinely ambiguous rather than resolve cleanly in either direction.

**Prompt:**

> Does this sequence give you a clear answer, or is the evidence mixed?

Expected answer: Mixed/inconclusive. The price has moved beyond the area but has not shown clean, sustained behaviour on one side. More context or additional candles may be needed.

**Student-facing explanation:**

> “Inconclusive” is a valid chart-reading conclusion. It means the evidence shown does not justify a stronger claim yet. Good analysis is not a contest to produce a confident label for every chart.

**Instructor note:** This language is important. Do not design the assessment to force a binary answer when the sequence is intentionally ambiguous.

### 10:20–11:20 — Three descriptions to keep separate

Reveal three definitions in turn:

**Rejection evidence:**
> Price tested or entered the area and then moved away in the visible sequence.

**Acceptance evidence:**
> Price showed continued or established activity beyond the area in the visible sequence.

**Inconclusive:**
> The available candles are mixed, too few or insufficient to distinguish the two descriptions.

**Copy beneath the definitions:**

> These terms describe the evidence so far. They do not guarantee what price will do next, and they are not trade signals by themselves.

### 11:20–14:40 — Practice: four short sequences

Show four short chart sequences in order. For every sequence, require a classification **and one sentence of evidence**. Let the learner revise after viewing the entire allotted sequence, but never show the final outcome before the classification step.

**Sequence 1:** Wick pierces the upper edge; candle closes back below; next candles move away.  
**Key:** Evidence consistent with rejection. Explain the close back away and subsequent movement. No guarantee of future resistance.

**Sequence 2:** Price closes beyond the zone; the next two candles also act beyond it.  
**Key:** Evidence consistent with acceptance beyond the zone. The relevant evidence is continued action, not just the first close.

**Sequence 3:** Price crosses, comes back, crosses again; sequence ends inside the band.  
**Key:** Inconclusive/mixed. A strong answer explicitly says why neither interpretation is settled.

**Sequence 4:** One large candle closes beyond the zone, but no later candles are shown.  
**Key:** A break and close have occurred in the excerpt; sustained acceptance is not established because follow-through is unavailable. “Too early to classify” is an excellent answer.

**Scoring per sequence (0–2):**
- 1 point for a defensible evidence category (inconclusive earns full credit where appropriate).
- 1 point for citing the right visible evidence and avoiding a guarantee.

**Feedback on overconfident answers:**

> Look at the candles you have, not the candles you wish you had. What is visible, and what information is still missing?

### 14:40–16:00 — Optional link to Lesson 9's micro-case

If the Lesson 9 personal micro-case is suitable, return to its pre-entry crop and reveal only the zone interaction up to the point relevant to rejection/acceptance. Do not reveal entry or outcome. Ask learners to describe the evidence at that moment and compare it with their earlier reading.

If the actual chart would make the answer ambiguous for reasons not taught yet, omit this repeat. The controlled exercises take precedence over forcing a personal chart into the lesson.

### 16:00–17:00 — Takeaway and bridge

**Takeaway:**

> **One candle is evidence, not a verdict.** Read the excursion, the close and what follows; stay willing to say “inconclusive.”

**Bridge:**

> Next, we will follow a zone across several visits. Instead of labelling it once and defending that label forever, we will practise updating our view as the evidence changes.

## 4. Instructor notes and source rationale

- **Adam Grimes, *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis*, Chapters 4–5:** useful counterweight to rigid chart-pattern interpretations and overconfidence at transitions between trends and ranges. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/9781119202837
- **John J. Murphy, *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets*:** broad reference for conventional chart interpretation; use as foundation, not proof that a candle configuration is predictive in every market. Publisher: https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/350647/technical-analysis-of-the-financial-markets-by-john-j-murphy/
- Avoid treating “acceptance/rejection” as universally standardised terms with one mechanical definition. This course uses them as disciplined descriptive vocabulary and must state that clearly.

## 5. Website / learning-room handoff

- Build a progressive chart sequence where the learner can commit to a provisional read before each reveal.
- Preserve prior answers and show how the reading changed as new candles arrive.
- Use at least three categories: **rejection evidence**, **acceptance evidence**, **inconclusive/mixed**. Do not force a binary choice.
- Provide a sentence field for the evidence behind the classification.
- Feedback should cite the candles already shown at that stage; it must not mention later candles before their reveal.
- This lesson does not launch ChartLab and does not expose trade outcome data.

## 6. Asset production checklist

- [ ] Sequence A: wick excursion, close away, subsequent movement away.
- [ ] Sequence B: close beyond zone, followed by continued action beyond.
- [ ] Sequence C: conflicting crosses and no clean resolution.
- [ ] Four practice sequences at the exact reveal cut-points.
- [ ] Optional masked extension of the Lesson 9 personal micro-case.
- [ ] All reference states annotated without words like “guaranteed,” “must hold” or unsupported claims of hidden intent.

# TradeThesis Support & Resistance
## Lesson 11 Production Script — How a Zone's Significance Changes

**Document role:** Student-facing lesson and production handoff  
**Curriculum position:** Module 4 — Read Price Behaviour  
**Estimated duration:** 17–19 minutes  
**Format:** Timeline reveal → keep an evidence log → revise a provisional view → compare initial and later reasoning  
**Asset rule:** Use a controlled timeline. A short instructor-chart example may be used only if it adds clarity; do not reveal its final result early.

---

## 1. Instructional decision

This lesson tackles a common trap: a trader marks a zone once, gives it a label such as “strong support,” and then interprets every future candle as evidence that the label was right. Students need to learn a better habit: keep track of the original evidence, notice what changes, and revise the reading when appropriate.

The core teaching point is not that more touches make a zone stronger or weaker. Touch count alone cannot settle that question. Students must consider how price interacted with the area, what happened afterward, whether the broader structure changed and how much uncertainty remains.

Use a **progressive timeline**. At each stage, freeze the chart at the information available then. Let learners record the current read before revealing the next interaction. At the end, compare how their reasoning evolved. Do not let the final chart make the earlier decisions look inevitable.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end, learners should be able to:

- State the evidence that originally justified watching a zone.
- Update an interpretation after a revisit, a renewed move away, deeper penetration or break.
- Explain why the number of touches alone is not a sufficient quality measure.
- Distinguish “my earlier interpretation changed” from “my earlier reasoning was automatically wrong.”
- Maintain a simple evidence log that separates observation, interpretation and what remains uncertain.

## 3. Student-facing script and progressive reveal

### 0:00–0:50 — Open with the problem

**On screen — title:**

> A zone is not a permanent verdict

**On screen — copy:**

> It is tempting to mark an area once and keep calling it “strong” or “weak.” But price keeps moving, and new evidence can change what that area means for the chart in front of us.
>
> Today, we will keep the original marking visible while updating the evidence around it.

### 0:50–2:40 — Timeline Stage 1: first clear reaction

**Chart brief:** A controlled chart where price previously reacted from a broad area and moved away clearly. Show only the sequence up through the first reaction and departure.

**Prompt:**

> At this point in the chart, what can you reasonably say about the area? What can you not know yet?

**Model response:** The area is worth watching because price reacted there and moved away in the sequence shown. We cannot know whether it will react again or how future price will behave.

**Learner evidence log — row 1:**
- **Observed:** What actually happened in the candles?
- **Current interpretation:** What does the evidence make reasonable to consider?
- **Still unknown:** What has not happened or been established yet?

**On-screen feedback:**

> Keep a record of the evidence that caused you to mark the area. Later, we can see what has changed rather than rewriting the story from memory.

### 2:40–4:40 — Timeline Stage 2: price revisits and moves away again

**Reveal:** Add a later return to the same area followed by another movement away. Do not yet show further visits.

**Prompt:**

> Has the new visit changed your reading? Choose one and explain why.

Options:
- The evidence for a relevant reaction area has gained another example.
- The zone is now guaranteed to hold.
- The zone must be weaker because it has been tested again.
- I need more information before judging the change.

**Correct reasoning:** The second reaction supplies another example of price interacting with the area, but a second reaction does not guarantee the next. It is not sufficient on its own to declare the zone stronger or weaker in a predictive sense.

**Student-facing explanation:**

> A second reaction adds information about how price has behaved around this area. But the count is not a magic score. The nature of each interaction, the move away, and the wider context still matter.

**Evidence log — row 2:** Ask what changed and what remains uncertain. Keep row 1 visible.

### 4:40–6:50 — Timeline Stage 3: a deeper test or longer pause

**Reveal:** Add a third interaction in which price enters further into the zone or spends longer around it, but does not yet break cleanly away. The sequence should be genuinely debatable rather than designed to prove a touch-count rule.

**Prompt:**

> What is different about this interaction compared with the earlier ones?

**Model response:** Price penetrated further into the marked area or spent more time within it; this changes the description of the interaction. It does not by itself prove that the zone has failed or that it will hold.

**On-screen explanation:**

> The later interaction is not identical to the earlier ones. Note what changed rather than forcing every visit into the same label.

**Instructor note:** The answer key must be grounded in the actual chart geometry. Do not infer buyer exhaustion, order absorption or institutional behaviour from the candle sequence alone.

### 6:50–9:00 — Timeline Stage 4: decisive move through, or evidence remains mixed

Use a sequence where price moves decisively through the band or remains beyond it for several candles. This is still about updating significance, not the full breakout/role-reversal lesson.

**Before reveal prompt:**

> If price now moves through the area, what should happen to your original reading?

**Model answer:** The earlier reactions remain historical facts, but the area no longer contains the current move as it did before. The original interpretation must be revised; we should not keep calling the zone guaranteed support/resistance. What happens next still needs observation.

**Reveal the relevant candles. Then display:**

> New evidence does not erase the past. It changes what we can reasonably say now.

**Instructor note:** If price does not decisively establish itself beyond the area in the actual asset, make this stage deliberately “mixed.” Do not manufacture a conclusive break just to fit the teaching concept. The asset can instead demonstrate that the evidence remains ambiguous and that the correct action is to keep the interpretation provisional.

### 9:00–11:00 — Teach the evidence review framework

**On screen — heading:**

> Four questions for updating a zone

Reveal one at a time:

1. **What originally justified marking the area?** Name the structure and prior reaction.
2. **What new evidence has arrived?** Describe price behaviour without inserting a motive.
3. **Does that evidence strengthen, weaken or leave the interpretation unclear?** Explain why; this is a judgement about the current reading, not a numerical score.
4. **What needs to change in my chart or scenario?** The zone might remain relevant, need revised boundaries, become less useful for the current question, or be left as inconclusive.

**Student-facing copy:**

> You do not need to defend your first interpretation forever. You need to explain why you held it at the time and what made you revise it.

### 11:00–14:30 — Independent timeline exercise

**Asset:** A fresh three-stage chart sequence with one zone and later interactions. Learner must submit an entry at each stage before the next stage unlocks.

**Evidence log prompts at each stage:**
- What is visible in this stage?
- What is your current interpretation?
- What remains uncertain?
- Has your reading changed since the previous stage? Why?

**Answer key — Stage A:** Area is worth watching because of a clear prior reaction; the next visit is unknown.

**Answer key — Stage B:** A new reaction changes the evidence by adding another observed response; no guarantee follows from the count.

**Answer key — Stage C:** Deeper penetration or sustained action through the band challenges the earlier view; students should acknowledge the change instead of preserving the original label. If the final candles are ambiguous, “the interpretation is less clear; I need more evidence” can earn full credit.

**Scoring (0–3):**
- 1 point for accurate observation of what changed.
- 1 point for a conditional current interpretation.
- 1 point for an explicit revision or uncertainty where appropriate.

Do not score the student by whether their final directional guess matches the hidden end of the chart. Score the evidence log.

### 14:30–16:00 — Compare first and final reads

Keep the learner's first interpretation visible beside their final one.

**On screen — copy:**

> Your first answer was based on the candles available then. Your final answer has more information. The goal is not to make the first answer look foolish; it is to make the update understandable.

**Reflection prompt:**

> What new evidence mattered most to your change in interpretation?

Accept any answer that names the specific changed candle sequence or price behaviour and explains its effect.

### 16:00–17:00 — Takeaway and bridge

**Takeaway:**

> **A good chart reader updates the read when the evidence changes.** Touch count alone is not a score, and an old reaction does not guarantee the next one.

**Bridge:**

> Next, we will examine a particular kind of change: price moving beyond a zone. We will separate the fact that price crossed a boundary from the stronger claim that a breakout is holding.

# TradeThesis Support & Resistance
## Lesson 12 Production Script — Breakout or Temporary Penetration?

**Document role:** Student-facing lesson and production handoff  
**Curriculum position:** Module 5 — Breakouts and Failed Attempts  
**Estimated duration:** 17–19 minutes  
**Format:** Observe the crossing → distinguish observation from thesis → progressive sequences → conditional response practice  
**Asset rule:** Use controlled sequences. Do not launch ChartLab Study 3 here; *Wider Neckline* remains in Lesson 14 after students have studied retests and failed breakouts.

---

## 1. Instructional decision

This lesson marks the transition from reading interactions at a zone to reading movement through it. Students should learn a simple but powerful distinction:

- **Observation:** price traded or closed beyond a marked area.
- **Interpretation:** evidence may suggest a breakout is developing or holding.
- **Conclusion:** requires more context and remains uncertain.

Do not teach “one close equals a breakout” as a universal law, and do not imply that a brief crossing must reverse. The lesson compares different sequences, including one that moves through and reverses, one that holds beyond the zone, and one that remains unclear. The learner's task is to state the current evidence conditionally and name what would make them reconsider.

The word “breakout” is used in ordinary chart-reading language here, but we should distinguish the fact of crossing a boundary from the judgement that a breakout is meaningful or continuing.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end, learners should be able to:

1. Identify the observable fact that price has crossed a zone.
2. Explain why a crossing alone does not establish sustained acceptance or continuation.
3. Compare quick penetration/reversal, follow-through beyond the area and mixed evidence.
4. State a current interpretation using conditional language and identify evidence that would make them reconsider.
5. Explain that an apparent breakout can fail without treating the failure as proof that every break should be faded.

**Not expected:** trading a breakout, selecting an entry, using fixed candle-count confirmation, predicting a retest, or applying a mechanical volume rule. Those topics are either context-dependent or belong in later lessons.

## 3. Student-facing script and reveal sequence

### 0:00–0:50 — Introduce the distinction

**On screen — title:**

> A break is an event. A breakout thesis is an interpretation.

**On screen — copy:**

> When price moves through a zone, we can describe that crossing immediately. What we cannot know from the crossing alone is whether price will continue, return, or remain uncertain.
>
> Today, we will look at the evidence one step at a time.

### 0:50–3:00 — Sequence A: first movement through the band

**Chart A brief:** A clean prior resistance band and price rising into and through it. Reveal the initial candle that crosses beyond the upper edge. Stop there.

**Prompt:**

> What can you say for certain from the candles now shown?

Options:
- Price has traded beyond the marked area in this sequence.
- The breakout is guaranteed to continue.
- The old resistance is guaranteed to become support.
- The price action is too complicated to describe anything.

**Correct answer:** Price has traded beyond the marked area. This is an observable description; the rest are predictions or later concepts.

**Student-facing copy:**

> The boundary has been crossed. That is the event. Whether this becomes a sustained breakout is a separate question.

**Instructor note:** Use an honest chart with a clear boundary; don't introduce a drawing dispute from earlier modules. Focus on the price sequence after a defensible zone has already been established.

### 3:00–5:00 — Sequence A develops into a temporary penetration/reversal

**Reveal:** Add the next two or three candles. In this case, price returns into the zone and continues back toward the prior range rather than holding beyond it.

**Prompt:**

> How should this new information affect your interpretation?

**Model answer:** The initial crossing did occur, but the return into the prior area and subsequent action weaken the idea that price was establishing itself beyond the zone. The sequence is consistent with a failed or temporary break. The learner should avoid saying every similar cross will fail.

**On-screen explanation:**

> A price crossing can be real even when the attempt to hold beyond the zone does not last. We can describe the later return as evidence against the initial continuation idea.

**Instructor note:** Avoid saying “false breakout” at the initial crossing before the subsequent candles exist. The term is retrospective unless the method gives a clearly defined real-time condition for applying it.

### 5:00–7:20 — Sequence B: crossing with follow-through

**Chart B brief:** Similar starting point and clearly marked zone, but price closes beyond it and subsequent candles continue to trade/close beyond the area. Do not show all candles at once.

**First prompt, after the first close beyond:**

> Has the area been crossed? Yes. Do we already know that the move will continue? No. What evidence would you watch next?

Accept reasonable replies that reference subsequent action, whether price quickly returns through the band, and the surrounding structure.

**Reveal next candles. Then show:**

> The additional candles show that price has continued to act beyond the zone. That is stronger evidence of a move holding than the initial crossing alone. It is still not a guarantee of further continuation.

**Prompt:**

> Finish this sentence: “The current evidence is consistent with ___, but I would reconsider if ___.”

**Model answer:** “The current evidence is consistent with price holding beyond resistance, but I would reconsider if price returns through the zone and begins to act inside the previous range again.”

Accept other specific, plausible invalidating evidence. Do not insist all students use identical wording.

### 7:20–9:30 — Sequence C: mixed or inconclusive evidence

**Chart C brief:** Price moves beyond the zone, returns near it, then crosses back and forth. The extract ends without a decisive sequence.

**Prompt:**

> What is your current read?

Options:
- A sustained break is clearly established.
- The crossing failed with certainty.
- The evidence is mixed; I would not make a stronger claim yet.

**Correct answer:** The evidence is mixed/inconclusive. Students must mention the back-and-forth movement or lack of sustained action outside the band.

**Student-facing explanation:**

> Some charts do not resolve cleanly in the excerpt we have. You are allowed to leave the question open. “Inconclusive” is more useful than pretending uncertainty does not exist.

### 9:30–11:30 — What to consider after a crossing

Reveal four prompts, one at a time:

**1. Where did price close relative to the zone?**

> Record the close as an observation; do not treat it as a guaranteed rule.

**2. What happened after the crossing?**

> Did price continue beyond the area, return quickly, or move back and forth?

**3. How does the crossing fit the broader structure?**

> Is it happening at the edge of a range, within a broader trend or in a congested area? Context can alter the interpretation.

**4. What would make you reconsider?**

> Name evidence that would weaken your current reading, rather than defending it regardless of what price does.

**Instructor note:** Volume can be mentioned as an additional market-specific data point only if the example uses it and the lesson explains its limitations. Do not introduce a universal “breakout needs X times average volume” rule.

### 11:30–14:40 — Independent practice: three staged sequences

The learner completes each sequence with the future candles hidden until their current response is submitted.

**Practice 1 — quick return:** A candle trades above resistance, closes near/inside the band, and the next candles return to the prior range.
- **Model read:** The crossing occurred, but follow-through was absent and price returned to the range. Evidence against sustained acceptance in this sequence; do not infer every such cross will fail.

**Practice 2 — follow-through:** Price closes beyond the zone and subsequent candles continue to hold outside it.
- **Model read:** Evidence is consistent with the move holding beyond the area. The continuation is not guaranteed; a future return could change the interpretation.

**Practice 3 — mixed:** Price crosses beyond, returns to the band and oscillates with no clear separation.
- **Model read:** Inconclusive. State what additional price behaviour would strengthen either interpretation.

**Required written response for each:**
> “The current evidence suggests ___. I am not yet sure about ___. I would reconsider if ___.”

**Scoring per sequence (0–3):**
- 1 point: accurate observation of where price is relative to the zone.
- 1 point: interpretation matches the sequence and uses cautious language.
- 1 point: meaningful condition for reconsideration.

**Feedback for binary overconfidence:**

> You have named a possible direction, but what evidence supports it? What would show that your current read is no longer useful?

### 14:40–16:00 — Carefully bounded research note

**On screen — optional expandable note titled “Why can some breaks accelerate?”**

> Some research in foreign-exchange markets has found that clustered orders around certain widely watched prices can help explain why price sometimes accelerates as a level is crossed. That does not mean every support/resistance line contains those orders or that every breakout will accelerate.

**Instructor notes:** If citing Carol Osler, specify that her work studied particular foreign-exchange settings and order data. Do not suggest that an ordinary candlestick chart directly reveals stop orders, institutional intentions or the cause of a specific breakout. Keep this note optional so it does not distract from the primary skill.

### 16:00–17:00 — Takeaway and bridge

**Takeaway:**

> **Crossing the zone is what happened. Holding beyond it is what we need to assess.** Observe the follow-through, keep alternatives open, and identify what would change your read.

**Bridge:**

> Next, we will explore what may happen when price returns to a broken area. Sometimes the old role appears to reverse; sometimes price re-enters the previous range. Neither outcome should be assumed in advance.

## 4. Assessment and answer-key policy

A good answer makes four moves: states the observable crossing, describes the subsequent candles available so far, gives a conditional interpretation, and names what would cause the interpretation to change. It does not need to predict the final outcome.

Do not reward a student for guessing the eventual path if their explanation ignores evidence. Do reward the correct use of “inconclusive” when the chart sequence is intentionally mixed.
