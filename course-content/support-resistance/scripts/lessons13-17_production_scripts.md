# Lesson 13 — Retests and Role Reversal

## Lesson purpose

Teach students to understand a return to a broken zone as a possible event, not a required step. Explain why former resistance may later behave as support, or former support as resistance, while emphasising that the role can fail.

**Estimated duration:** 25–30 minutes  
**Prerequisites:** Lessons 1–12  
**Chart type:** Controlled historical or purpose-built examples, no indicators needed  
**Personal charts:** Not required in this lesson  
**ChartLab:** No new ChartLab activity

## Learning outcomes

By the end, students should be able to:

1. Identify a zone that price has moved beyond.
2. Describe a retest without assuming that one must occur.
3. Assess whether price is holding, rejecting, or moving back through the area.
4. State at least one alternative outcome and one piece of evidence that would weaken the initial interpretation.

## Lesson flow

1. Recall: what does a breakout change? (1 min)
2. Observe a chart after a break, before the return is revealed (2 min)
3. Reveal the return to the zone (2 min)
4. Explain role reversal as a hypothesis (4 min)
5. Compare a successful-looking retest with a failed one (3 min)
6. Independent practice (3–4 min)
7. Takeaway and knowledge check (2 min)

## Student-facing opening

> In the last few lessons, you learned that a move beyond a zone is not automatically a confirmed breakout. Now we will look at what can happen afterwards.
>
> Sometimes price returns to the area it has just crossed. Traders often call this a **retest**.
>
> The useful question is not “Will price retest?” It is “If price returns, what does its behaviour tell us—and what would show that our interpretation is wrong?”

## Chart sequence and reveal instructions

### Chart A — Break beyond an established zone

**Asset brief:** A clean chart with a clearly marked historical resistance zone and a later sequence that moves above it. No retest candles should be visible initially. Use a simple, liquid instrument and a timeframe where the sequence can be read without magnification. Record instrument, timeframe, date/source and whether the chart is illustrative or historical.

**Initial prompt:**

> Price has moved above the marked resistance area. What has changed on the chart? What has *not* been established yet?

**Do not reveal:** Future candles, entry arrows, labels such as “successful breakout,” or any trade outcome.

**Instructor notes / intended reasoning:**
- Price has traded beyond the zone.
- A move beyond the area alone does not establish that the market will remain above it.
- A retest may happen, but it is not mandatory.
- Avoid claims about who “must” be trapped or what institutions “must” be doing.

### Chart B — Reveal a return to the area

Reveal the next section showing price coming back towards the former resistance zone.

**Narration:**

> Price has returned towards the area it crossed. This is the point where the old label—“resistance”—is not enough on its own.
>
> We now observe how price behaves here. Does it move back above the area after a brief test? Does it close back inside the old range? Does it pass through and continue lower? We do not know the answer from the word *retest*. We have to read the evidence.

**Visual direction:** Keep the original zone visible. Use a subtle annotation for “former resistance,” then a separate marker for “return to area.” Do not rename it “support” before showing the reaction.

### Chart C — Compare two possible outcomes

Present two small, similarly scaled chart excerpts:
- **Sequence 1:** Price returns to the area, then moves away above it.
- **Sequence 2:** Price returns to the area, trades back through it and moves into the prior range.

**Prompt:**

> Both charts show a return to the same kind of area. What is different in the evidence after the return?

**Teaching point:** The location is not the conclusion. What happens around and after the area determines whether the role-reversal interpretation remains plausible.

## Core teaching script

> A role reversal is a way to describe a possible change in how an area behaves.
>
> If price previously struggled to move above resistance, then later breaks above it, that old resistance area may become relevant as support on a subsequent return. The reverse can happen when price breaks below support.
>
> Notice the word **may**. The market is not required to respect the old zone in its new role. Price can hold near it, move through it, or react in a way that remains unclear.
>
> A retest is not a magic entry signal. It is a new piece of evidence to assess in context.
>
> Keep the original area on the chart while you assess the return. Do not move the zone just to make the later candles look like a perfect retest. If the original boundaries no longer explain the behaviour, say so and reassess.
>
> Finally, do not assume that every breakout must return to its origin. Sometimes price continues without a retest. Missing that move is not a reason to chase it.

## Expanded reading and worked historical case

**A retest is not confirmation. It is the next test of a claim.**

The word *retest* describes a return towards an area that price previously crossed. It does not tell us whether the old area is holding in its new role. Separate three events in your notes: the **break**, the **return**, and the **response after the return**.

A return may touch the edge, enter part of the band, travel through it or stop just short. None of these observations alone settles the outcome. If price stays on the new side and later moves away, a role-reversal interpretation becomes more plausible in that sequence. If price crosses back into the old range and cannot reclaim the area, the interpretation weakens. If the candles overlap around the boundary, “inconclusive so far” may be the best description.

Do not redraw the original zone just to make the return look neat after you have seen it. If new evidence leads you to revise a boundary, record when and why. The analyst's job is to make a defensible judgement with the information available at each stage—not to produce a perfect-looking chart after the fact.

Also keep the possibility that no return occurs. A breakout does not owe the analyst a tidy retest. Missing a move because it never returned is not a reason to chase it, and the absence of a retest does not invalidate every break.

### Worked historical case: NIFTY 50 daily bars, August 2014

**Temporary internal teaching reference:** the candidate former-resistance zone is approximately **7,795–7,865 index points**, selected around prior upper-area activity. These are chosen teaching boundaries, not official levels. Replace the associated chart with user-owned or rights-cleared material before publication.

On **18 August 2014**, the daily close was **7,874.25**, above the selected band. Hide subsequent candles and ask whether the break alone proves the area has changed roles. Then reveal **20 August**: the session's low was **7,864.05** and its close was **7,875.30**. The low reached just inside the selected band while the close finished above it. In the following selected window, price stayed above the area and moved higher.

This sequence is consistent with the old area remaining relevant from above during this window. It does not establish that the band caused the rally or must hold on another visit. Another defensible boundary could lead to a slightly different description, so explain the boundary rather than reward pixel-perfect matching.

### The comparison that matters

Compare this hold-like sequence with a return that crosses through the original zone and closes inside the prior range. Ask what changed in the **response after the return**. Both sequences can be called returns or retests; later evidence distinguishes a holding interpretation from a weakening one.

### Field note: preserve the revision, not just the conclusion

Require a four-stage timeline:
1. **Before the break:** why the area was selected.
2. **After the break:** what changed and what remained unproven.
3. **At the return:** whether price touched, entered or crossed the area, including the close.
4. **After later candles:** what strengthened or weakened the interpretation.

Then ask: “Which sentence in your first note would you keep, and which would you revise?” This turns notes into a record of reasoning rather than a retrospective explanation.

## Guided practice

**Task:** On a new chart, students must:
1. Mark the original zone.
2. Identify the point where price moves beyond it.
3. If a return occurs, label the return without deciding the outcome prematurely.
4. Choose one description: *holding beyond the area*, *moving back through the area*, or *inconclusive so far*.
5. Write one alternative scenario.

**Acceptable response example:**

> Price has moved above the former resistance zone and has returned to it. The return is still being assessed. If price holds above the area and moves away, the role-reversal interpretation becomes more plausible. If price moves back through the zone and remains in the prior range, that interpretation weakens.

Accept different wording when the student distinguishes observation from inference and identifies an alternative. Do not grade based on whether their drawing exactly matches the reference.

## Knowledge check

**Q1. Does every breakout need a retest?**  
Correct: No. Price can continue without returning to the zone.

**Q2. Has former resistance definitely become support once price returns to it from above?**  
Correct: No. The return is an opportunity to observe behaviour, not proof that the role has changed.

**Q3. Which evidence would weaken a bullish role-reversal interpretation?**  
Correct: Price moving back through the former resistance area and failing to hold above it. Accept other contextually defensible examples.

## Misconceptions to address

- “Every breakout retests.” Incorrect.
- “A retest is automatically an entry.” Incorrect; the course has not defined a universal entry trigger.
- “If price crosses the zone, redraw it around the new candles.” Not without a reason; avoid hindsight-fitting.
- “Former resistance must become support.” It may, but the role can fail.

## Lesson close — student-facing

> You can now separate three things that are often confused: the original zone, the move beyond it, and the behaviour after a return.
>
> That distinction matters. You are no longer treating “breakout” or “retest” as an answer by itself; you are asking what the chart has actually shown.
>
> **Keep this:** A retest is a situation to evaluate, not an event the market owes you.

## Instructor notes

- Keep the language conditional. Avoid “the market comes back to test the level” as if that were a rule.
- Do not add indicators to the chart unless a later lesson explicitly uses them.
- The purpose is interpretation, not teaching a complete breakout trading strategy.
- Link back to Lesson 10’s rejection/acceptance distinction and Lesson 12’s breakout/temporary-penetration distinction.

## Website / learning-room handoff

- Use a single chart with a reveal control or sequential panels. On mobile, keep the chart readable before placing questions below it.
- Do not show the answer annotation before the learner submits their interpretation.
- Support an “I’m not sure yet” answer. The feedback should explain what remains unknown, rather than mark uncertainty as failure.
- Save the learner’s written reasoning if account-backed lesson notes are available; if not, clearly label the exercise as not yet saved.
- Completion should mean the learner has attempted the exercise and knowledge check, not that they predicted the later candles.

## References and use

- **Schwager & Etzkorn, *A Complete Guide to the Futures Market*, support/resistance chapter:** use as a conventional reference for role reversal after breaks. Do not turn role reversal into a guarantee.
- **Murphy, *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets*:** use for the traditional chart-analysis framing of support and resistance.
- **Grimes, *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis*:** use as the critical counterbalance—context and uncertainty matter, and a pattern label is not a guarantee.
- These are instructional references. Do not reproduce book diagrams or passages; make original charts and explanations.

# Lesson 14 — Failed Breakouts and Alternate Outcomes

## Lesson purpose

Teach students to recognise when price moves beyond a zone but fails to sustain that move, and to revise their interpretation without pretending the failure was obvious beforehand.

**Estimated duration:** 30–38 minutes  
**Prerequisites:** Lessons 9–13  
**ChartLab:** Third and final planned study, **Wider Neckline**  
**Personal charts:** Not yet a full case study; a brief instructor chart may be used only if the controlled exercise is complete and the historical sequence is clearly documented.

## Learning outcomes

Students should be able to:
1. Distinguish the initial move beyond a zone from the later evidence that challenges it.
2. Identify a return through the zone without calling every pullback a failure.
3. Describe at least two plausible scenarios before the outcome is known.
4. Explain what new evidence changes their interpretation.
5. Complete ChartLab’s Wider Neckline study and compare their reasoning with the reference.

## Lesson flow

1. Recall the difference between a break and confirmation (1 min)
2. Show the setup before the outcome (2 min)
3. Reveal the initial move beyond the zone (2 min)
4. Pause and ask for alternate scenarios (2 min)
5. Reveal the move back through the zone (3 min)
6. Teach the difference between a challenged thesis and a confirmed failure (3 min)
7. ChartLab: Wider Neckline (6–8 min)
8. Debrief and takeaway (2 min)

## Student-facing opening

> A breakout can look convincing and still fail. That does not mean every breakout is a trap, and it does not mean we should assume failure whenever price hesitates.
>
> Our job is to describe what is visible, decide what the evidence currently supports, and change our view when the evidence changes.
>
> In this lesson, you will practise holding more than one possible outcome in mind—and letting the chart, not hindsight, determine when an interpretation needs to change.

## Chart sequence

### Chart A — The area before the attempted break

**Asset brief:** Use a clean price sequence approaching a well-defined zone. Hide all later candles. The zone should be defensible based on the preceding structure, not drawn around the eventual failure.

**Prompt:**

> Price is approaching this area. What are two plausible things that could happen next? What would you need to see to distinguish them?

**Model responses:**
- Price may move through and hold beyond the zone.
- Price may fail to sustain the move and return through the area.
- Price may hesitate or remain inconclusive.

### Chart B — The first move beyond the zone

Reveal only enough candles to show the initial move beyond the area.

**Prompt:**

> Has price moved beyond the zone? Yes. Does that alone prove the move will continue? No. What would you watch next?

Do not use “fakeout” or “failed breakout” as a label yet. Those labels reveal the outcome and teach hindsight.

### Chart C — Evidence that challenges the initial interpretation

Reveal the later candles showing price returning through the zone. Keep the original zone unchanged.

**Narration:**

> We now have new evidence. Price moved beyond the area, but it has returned through it. This weakens the idea that price is establishing itself beyond the zone.
>
> We should not rewrite the earlier chart and pretend we knew the outcome. The earlier observation was that price moved beyond the area. The later observation is that it did not sustain that move.
>
> A failed-breakout interpretation becomes more plausible as the evidence develops, but the exact next move is still uncertain.

### Chart D — Contrast with a pullback that holds

Use a second sequence in which price pulls back towards the zone but remains beyond it and subsequently moves away. It must be visually comparable without implying all charts resolve neatly.

**Prompt:**

> What distinguishes this sequence from the previous one? Identify the actual difference in price behaviour, not just the final direction.

**Instructor point:** A pullback is not automatically a failure. The original zone, the location of closes, the follow-through and the broader structure all matter. Avoid rigid one-candle definitions.

## Core teaching script

> A failed breakout is a description of a sequence, not a prediction you can make with certainty at the first crossing.
>
> First, price moves beyond a zone. At that moment, we record what happened. Then new candles arrive. If price returns through the zone and cannot sustain itself beyond it, the continuation interpretation weakens.
>
> We need to be careful with the word *failure*. A brief move back inside the zone may be inconclusive. A sustained return through the area is stronger evidence that the original breakout thesis is not working as expected.
>
> This is why we planned alternatives before the outcome. We do not need to know which scenario will happen to define what evidence would support or weaken each one.
>
> The lesson is not “fade every breakout.” That would replace one rigid rule with another. The lesson is to observe, update, and avoid defending a thesis after its supporting evidence has disappeared.

## Expanded reading: a failed move is evidence, not an instruction

**A failed breakout can tell you more than the breakout attempt—but it is not an automatic reverse signal.**

A failed-breakout interpretation depends on later evidence. At the first crossing, price has moved beyond the area; do not label the attempt a failure before contradictory evidence exists. If price later returns through the original boundary and cannot sustain the move outside it, the continuation thesis has weakened. That still does not establish that price must reverse strongly in the opposite direction.

Use a four-part review:
1. **Prior boundary:** what area was defensible before the attempted break? Preserve the original markings for the first comparison.
2. **Break attempt:** what was visible then—a wick, close, distance beyond the band, or follow-through?
3. **Contradiction:** what later behaviour challenged the continuation idea? Distinguish a return into the band from a close back inside the prior range.
4. **After the contradiction:** did price continue into the range, recover, or remain mixed? The response affects how strongly the failure should be described.

A brief return inside a zone can be inconclusive. Sustained movement back into the old range is stronger evidence against the original continuation thesis. A pullback that approaches the zone and remains beyond it is not the same sequence as a move that crosses back through and cannot reclaim the area.

The opposite direction becomes an alternative worth assessing, not an automatic trade. A chart does not reveal who was trapped, whether an institution engineered the move, or why it happened. Keep claims tied to observable price behaviour.

### Worked historical case: NIFTY 50 daily bars, January–February 2018

**Temporary internal teaching reference:** the candidate former-resistance area is approximately **10,900–10,920 index points**, framed around the prior swing high near **10,906.85 on 19 January 2018**. It is a selected instructional boundary, not an official market level. Replace the associated chart with user-owned or rights-cleared material before publication.

Reveal the sequence through **1 February** but hide **2 February**. The daily close moved above the selected area on **22 January** at **10,966.20** and remained above it through the following sessions; the close on **1 February** was **11,016.90**. Ask: “What is the strongest claim this evidence supports? What remains uncertain? What would make the continuation interpretation weaker?”

Require a provisional response before continuing. Then reveal **2 February**: the session closed at **10,760.60**, back below the original band after trading as high as **11,117.35** the day before. This is material contradictory evidence for the idea that price was sustaining itself above the old boundary in this window.

The lesson is not that the failure was obvious in advance, nor that a short trade was automatically justified. At the time of the earlier closes, the later move back below the band was not yet visible. Preserve the earlier reasoning, then revise it in response to new information.

### Field note: the failure record

Before showing the worked interpretation, record:
- **Original thesis:** what did I think the move beyond the boundary meant?
- **Evidence available then:** what had price done up to that point? Exclude future candles.
- **Contradiction:** which later observation weakened the thesis?
- **Revised reading:** what is now more plausible, and what remains uncertain?
- **Process check:** was the initial interpretation reasonable given the evidence then available, even though the move later failed?

This separates decision process from outcome. A reasonable thesis can fail; a profitable outcome can follow weak reasoning. Review the evidence and consistency of process rather than simply rewarding the final direction.

## ChartLab study 3 — Wider Neckline

**Purpose:** Integrate zone drawing with uncertainty and breakout interpretation, after students have completed the earlier controlled studies.

### Before the activity

Student-facing copy:

> This final ChartLab study brings several skills together. You will mark the relevant areas, submit each drawing before seeing the reference, then compare your reasoning with the worked explanation.
>
> You are not being asked to guess the future. You are being asked to mark what the chart supports and explain what remains uncertain.

### Required activity sequence

1. Display the unlabelled chart with the price structure visible.
2. Ask the learner to mark the support area first.
3. Lock the support submission so it remains visible.
4. Ask the learner to mark the resistance/neckline area.
5. Lock the second submission.
6. Show the learner’s two zones alongside the instructor reference.
7. Explain the reference and acceptable alternatives.
8. Ask two short questions about the area and the evidence around it.
9. Mark the activity complete only after both zones and the knowledge check have been attempted.

**Do not reveal:** Reference zones before submission, the eventual outcome as a title, or a “correct” result badge before feedback.

### Feedback rules

- **Strong reasoning:** The learner identified the meaningful structure and drew a zone that reasonably covers the relevant reactions.
- **Reasonable alternative:** The learner chose slightly different boundaries but can explain how they relate to the visible reactions.
- **Needs reconsideration:** The drawing misses the relevant structure, is so broad that it becomes uninformative, or is placed mainly to fit the later outcome.
- **Uncertain:** If the chart genuinely allows multiple interpretations, say so. Do not force a single exact answer merely to simplify scoring.

### Knowledge check (ChartLab)

**Q1. Price moves beyond the neckline and later returns through it. What should you conclude?**  
Model answer: The initial breakout interpretation has weakened; assess whether the move is being sustained. Do not automatically predict a reversal.

**Q2. Why keep your original zone visible during the comparison?**  
Model answer: To judge whether the original boundaries were defensible and avoid shifting them after seeing the outcome.

## End-of-lesson debrief

> You have now worked through three different chart studies. The point was not to make every drawing look identical. It was to practise choosing areas, explaining boundaries, and noticing when the evidence is clear or uncertain.
>
> A useful analyst can say, “My first interpretation has weakened.” That is not a failure of chart reading. It is part of chart reading.

## Instructor notes

- This is the final planned ChartLab study. Do not add a fourth unless the curriculum is deliberately revised.
- Keep the exercise about reading the chart, not about recommending a trade.
- Use “failed breakout” only after the later evidence has been revealed.
- Avoid attributing the move to “stop hunts,” “smart money” or hidden motives unless there is independent evidence; the chart alone does not establish motive.
- Keep the distinction between an unsuccessful market move and a bad decision. A thesis can be reasonable and still fail.

## Website / learning-room handoff

- ChartLab opens from Lesson 14 with the learner returning to the same lesson after completion.
- Preserve submissions for both zones throughout the comparison stage.
- Show reference markings and rationale only after submission.
- Make the feedback responsive and readable on a phone; chart comparison must not shrink both charts beyond usability.
- Provide a clear exit back to Lesson 14, plus a completion state that can later be tied to account progress.
- This is an integration requirement, not a claim that account persistence currently exists.

## References and use

- **Grimes, *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis*:** informs the emphasis on uncertainty, context and avoiding simplistic pattern claims.
- **Murphy, *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets*:** traditional chart-analysis context.
- **Osler, “Support for Resistance: Technical Analysis and Intraday Exchange Rates”:** an empirical example that some supplied levels showed predictive value in a specific FX setting; not evidence that every apparent breakout or level is predictable.
- Use the references to frame the concepts; do not reproduce proprietary charts or passages.

# Lesson 15 — From Zones to Conditional Scenarios

## Lesson purpose

Teach students to use zones as part of a conditional analysis, not as standalone buy/sell instructions. They should be able to describe the current context, what they are watching, what would strengthen or weaken an idea, where the next opposing zone sits, and when there may be insufficient clarity or space to justify action.

**Estimated duration:** 18–22 minutes  
**Prerequisites:** Lessons 1–14  
**Personal charts:** Optional brief chart excerpt only if it demonstrates a single planning decision; complete trade story remains for Lessons 16–17  
**ChartLab:** No new ChartLab study

## Learning outcomes

Students should be able to write a compact scenario plan containing:
- Current structure and relevant zones.
- A primary conditional scenario.
- At least one alternative.
- Observable evidence that would support or weaken each scenario.
- The next opposing area and available space.
- A statement of what would invalidate the analysis.
- A reason to stand aside when the evidence is poor or the available space is limited.

## Lesson flow

1. Show a chart with zones already marked and ask what can—and cannot—be concluded (2 min)
2. Explain observation versus scenario versus trigger versus invalidation (4 min)
3. Demonstrate a two-scenario plan (4 min)
4. Introduce the next opposing zone and available space (3 min)
5. Practice on an unfamiliar chart (5 min)
6. Review answer key and takeaway (3 min)

## Student-facing opening

> You can now identify and draw useful zones, and you have practised interpreting price behaviour around them. The next step is to organise that reading into a plan.
>
> A plan is not a prediction dressed up as certainty. It is a clear description of what you are observing, what you may do if specific evidence appears, and what would make you reconsider.
>
> Sometimes the best conclusion is that the chart is not clear enough yet. We will treat that as a valid analytical outcome—not as an empty answer.

## The four layers of a scenario

Use a simple visual with four separate labels:

1. **Observation:** What can I point to on the chart right now?
2. **Scenario:** What are plausible next paths?
3. **Condition to watch:** What observable development would make one scenario more relevant?
4. **Invalidation / reassessment:** What evidence would show that the scenario no longer fits?

Add **available space** as a fifth check: how far is the next opposing zone, and is there room for the idea to make sense after costs and risk are considered? Do not teach a universal minimum reward-to-risk ratio in this lesson; that belongs to a defined trading plan and depends on execution assumptions.

## Worked example — two scenarios, no forecast

**Chart brief:** Use a clean range with a lower support area, an upper resistance area and price currently approaching one boundary. Do not show subsequent candles at first.

**Prompt:**

> What can we say from this chart without predicting the next candle?

**Model observation:**

> Price is within a trading range and is approaching the lower marked area. The chart shows previous reactions there, but the area is not guaranteed to hold.

**Scenario A:**

> If price reacts at the lower area and subsequent candles support that interpretation, a move back into the range is one possible scenario.

**Scenario B:**

> If price moves through the lower area and begins sustaining itself below it, the idea that the range floor is holding becomes weaker. The next area below may become relevant.

**Reassessment condition:**

> If price moves through the zone and follow-through contradicts the expected reaction, I will not continue to treat the zone as a guaranteed floor.

**Space check:**

> The upper range boundary is the next opposing area for a move back into the range. If the distance is small relative to the risk required by a proposed trade plan, there may not be enough room to justify the idea.

**Instructor note:** Do not imply that the scenario automatically supplies an entry. A separate entry trigger, risk plan and execution method would be needed before making a trade decision.

## Student-facing explanation

> Notice what this plan does not say: “Support will hold.”
>
> It says where price is, what a reaction might look like, what would weaken that idea, and where the next opposing area lies.
>
> This is more useful because the plan can respond to new evidence. If price behaves differently from the scenario, you have already decided what you need to reconsider.
>
> You do not need to create a trade from every chart. If zones overlap, if the next opposing area is very close, or if price behaviour is inconclusive, your analysis can end with “wait” or “stand aside.”

## Practice task

Provide a new chart with:
- One clearly marked support area.
- One resistance area.
- Current price approaching support.
- No future candles shown.

Students complete:

**1. What do you observe?**  
**2. What are two plausible scenarios?**  
**3. What would strengthen the first scenario?**  
**4. What would weaken or invalidate it?**  
**5. Where is the next opposing area?**  
**6. Is there enough clarity to plan a trade now, or is waiting more reasonable? Explain.**

### Model answer

> Price is approaching a previously observed support area inside a range. One scenario is that price reacts and moves back into the range. Another is that price moves through the area and the range breaks down.
>
> Follow-through away from support would strengthen the reaction interpretation; sustained movement below the zone would weaken it. The upper range boundary is the next opposing area for a move upward. I would not call the area guaranteed support. Whether there is a trade depends on the eventual evidence, a defined entry method, risk and available space. Waiting is reasonable while the reaction remains unclear.

Accept other reasoned answers. The chart may allow a learner to argue that the structure is ambiguous; reward that if they identify why.

## Knowledge check

**Q1. What is the difference between an observation and a scenario?**  
Observation describes visible chart evidence; a scenario describes a possible path conditional on future evidence.

**Q2. Does identifying support provide an entry signal by itself?**  
No. It identifies an area to assess, not a complete trade decision.

**Q3. Why inspect the next opposing zone?**  
It helps assess available space and whether a potential move has room relative to the risk and execution plan.

**Q4. Is “stand aside” a valid conclusion?**  
Yes, when evidence is unclear, the chart is congested, available space is poor, or the conditions required by the student's plan are absent.

## Chart Reading Notes — reusable worksheet

Students record:
- Instrument / timeframe / date.
- Current structure.
- Most relevant support and resistance zones.
- What is observed (facts only).
- Scenario A and its supporting condition.
- Scenario B and its supporting condition.
- Invalidation or reassessment evidence.
- Next opposing area / available space.
- Decision status: *observe*, *wait for evidence*, *plan under conditions*, or *stand aside*.
- What remains uncertain.

## Lesson close — student-facing

> You have moved from drawing zones to explaining what you would watch around them.
>
> That is an important step. Your analysis does not need to predict every move; it needs to make your assumptions visible and tell you when those assumptions should be reconsidered.
>
> **Keep this:** A useful plan includes the conditions under which you would change your mind.

## Instructor notes

- Avoid implying that a scenario is a recommendation or signal.
- Do not add exact entry, stop or target rules unless they are explicitly part of the course's stated method and supported by the instructor's real process.
- Teach available space qualitatively here. A quantitative risk/reward method needs its own assumptions and examples.
- Reinforce that no trade is an acceptable result.
- Keep “invalidation” tied to the thesis and observable chart evidence; do not imply every wick beyond a zone requires an immediate exit.

## Website / learning-room handoff

- Provide a structured worksheet that works on mobile, not a wide table requiring horizontal scrolling.
- Allow saving and revisiting the learner's notes if account-backed progress is available; otherwise disclose that the notes may not persist.
- Include a “compare with model reasoning” step only after submission.
- Do not show a green “correct trade” state. Feedback should assess the quality and completeness of reasoning.
- The worksheet may later be surfaced in the member dashboard as a completed learning activity, but do not promise this before progress storage is implemented.

## References and use

- **Grimes, *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis*:** practical trading templates and the importance of context; use to reinforce conditional thinking.
- **Shannon, *Technical Analysis Using Multiple Timeframes*:** informs context across timeframes; do not force multi-timeframe analysis into every beginner example.
- **Murphy, *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets*:** broad foundation for chart context.
- References inform the teaching framework; all worksheet examples should be original.

# Lesson 16 — Case Study: A Trade Based on a Reaction

## Lesson purpose

Show how you actually applied support/resistance in a real trade, from the information available before entry through execution, management and review. The student should learn how the skill fits into a decision process—not copy a signal or assume the outcome was inevitable.

**Estimated duration:** 20–25 minutes  
**Chart assets:** One authentic trade with the best available before-entry, entry, management and exit evidence  
**Personal charts:** Core of this lesson  
**ChartLab:** None; students are now applying skills to a real trading narrative

## Important selection requirement

Choose a real trade where a support/resistance reaction materially informed your thesis. Ideally, select one with:
- A clear pre-trade chart or screenshot.
- The zone as it was understood before entry.
- A documented entry and invalidation/risk decision.
- At least one point where the thesis could have been questioned.
- A clear exit or review.
- Enough information to separate what was known at the time from what became clear later.

Do not choose solely because the trade was profitable or the chart looks attractive. A profitable trade can teach poor process if the decision was unsupported; a losing trade can teach good process if the reasoning and risk management were defensible. If only retrospective screenshots exist, state that the reconstruction is retrospective.

## Learning outcomes

Students should be able to:
1. Identify the chart context before seeing the outcome.
2. Explain why the instructor selected the zone.
3. Separate the zone from the entry trigger.
4. Identify the thesis, invalidation and risk decision.
5. Describe how new evidence affected management.
6. Review the outcome without assuming that result alone proves decision quality.

## Production preparation — instructor-only

Before writing the final narrative, complete a case-study record:

- Instrument / market / contract if relevant.
- Date and session.
- Timeframe(s) used.
- Chart source and screenshot timestamps.
- What was visible before entry.
- Zone boundaries used at the time.
- Why that zone mattered.
- Other nearby zones considered or rejected.
- Trade direction and thesis.
- Actual trigger used.
- Entry, stop/invalidation, position sizing or risk method, and exit (include only what you can verify).
- What happened during the trade.
- What would have invalidated the thesis.
- Actual result and any costs/slippage if available.
- What you learned afterwards.
- What remains uncertain or could be improved.
- Permission/rights to use the chart and any sensitive information to omit.

Never invent the reason for a trade if the original notes do not establish it. Ask the instructor to supply missing rationale before finalising the script.

## Lesson flow

1. Explain the rules of the case study (1 min)
2. Reveal the pre-trade chart with no markings (2 min)
3. Student marks or describes the important zone (3 min)
4. Reveal the instructor's original zone and rationale (3 min)
5. Reveal the thesis and trigger (3 min)
6. Reveal risk and invalidation (3 min)
7. Reveal management decisions in chronological stages (4 min)
8. Reveal exit and result (2 min)
9. Review process versus outcome (3 min)

## Student-facing opening

> In this lesson, we will examine a real trade from the information available at the time.
>
> I will not show you the finished chart first. You will see the context, make your own observations, and then compare your reasoning with mine.
>
> The aim is not to copy my entry. It is to understand how I used the zone, what else I needed before acting, what risk I accepted, and what would have made me reconsider.
>
> We will reveal the outcome last. That helps us judge the decision using the evidence that was available, rather than allowing the result to rewrite the story.

## Stage 1 — The chart before entry

**Asset:** The earliest available chart immediately before the trade decision. Hide all future candles, trade arrows, zone markings added later, entry/exit labels and profit/loss information. Keep essential axes and time information readable.

**Prompt:**

> Take a moment to read the chart.
>
> What is the broader structure? Which areas, if any, have evidence of previous reactions? What would you want to understand before considering a trade?

**Student response options:** short written note or structured fields for structure, relevant area and uncertainty.

**Instructor note:** Do not imply there is only one possible zone. The exercise is a comparison of reasoning, not a test of whether the student can guess the instructor's historical drawing.

## Stage 2 — Reveal your original zone

Show the zone exactly as it was understood before entry if that record exists. If reconstructed later, label it as a reconstruction.

**Narration template to customise with the instructor's actual explanation:**

> I focused on this area because [insert the actual visible evidence and the reason it mattered at the time].
>
> I did not treat the zone as a guarantee. My working idea was [insert the actual thesis]. The evidence I was watching for was [insert actual condition].
>
> I also considered [insert nearby alternative or limitation]. That mattered because [explain why it affected the decision].

Do not fill the bracketed fields with generic assumptions. The instructor must supply the real rationale.

**Prompt:**

> Compare your interpretation with mine. Did you identify the same structure? If your zone differs, can you explain why your boundaries are still defensible?

**Feedback principle:** Reasoned differences are acceptable. A learner does not need to replicate the instructor's exact zone to demonstrate understanding.

## Stage 3 — Reveal the thesis and entry trigger

Show the actual entry context only after the zone discussion.

**Narration template:**

> The zone was one part of the decision; it was not, by itself, the entry signal.
>
> I entered when [insert the actual trigger]. At that moment, the evidence I had was [insert observable facts]. I was not able to know [insert uncertainty that remained].
>
> This distinction matters: identifying an area of interest and deciding to act are separate decisions.

If the instructor used no separate trigger and the zone itself played a direct role, explain that honestly. Do not retrofit a sophisticated trigger after the fact.

## Stage 4 — Reveal risk and invalidation

Show the actual risk/invalidation logic. If position sizing or exact risk details are unavailable, omit them rather than infer.

**Narration template:**

> Before considering the outcome, we need to know what would make this thesis no longer valid.
>
> My invalidation condition was [insert actual condition]. My risk decision was [insert actual stop/size/risk process, if documented].
>
> This was not a promise that price would behave as expected. It was a way to define what I was prepared to risk and what evidence would challenge the idea.

**Student prompt:**

> Based only on what you know so far, what evidence would make you reconsider this thesis? Why?

## Stage 5 — Reveal management in chronological order

Use two or three snapshots at most, each representing a real decision point. Do not jump from entry directly to the finished chart.

For every snapshot, show:
- What had changed on the chart.
- What the instructor could see at that time.
- The actual management decision.
- Why that decision was made, if documented.
- What remained uncertain.

**Prompt at each stage:**

> What has changed since the previous chart? Does it strengthen the thesis, weaken it, or leave the situation inconclusive? What would you want to watch next?

The instructor's actual decision is revealed after the student responds. Do not present an alternative decision as obviously foolish if it was reasonable under the available evidence.

## Stage 6 — Reveal exit and outcome

Show the exit and outcome only after the preceding decisions.

**Narration template:**

> The trade ended when [insert actual exit reason]. The outcome was [insert verified result].
>
> Now we can review both the result and the decision process. They are related, but they are not the same thing.
>
> We should ask whether the zone was defensible, whether the thesis matched the evidence, whether risk was defined, and whether management followed the plan. We should not conclude that every decision was good merely because the trade made money—or that every decision was bad because it lost.

## Structured review

Ask the student to rate or describe:

1. Was the zone supported by visible structure?
2. Was the entry trigger separate from the zone, or did the instructor explain how the zone itself formed part of the method?
3. Was the invalidation condition clear?
4. Did the management decisions respond to new evidence?
5. What could the instructor reasonably know before entry?
6. What only became clear afterwards?
7. What is one process lesson that transfers to another chart?

Do not score this as a “copy the trade” exercise. Score the quality of the student's explanation.

## Case-study debrief template

Complete this with the actual trade:
- **What I noticed:** [verified observation]
- **Why this zone mattered:** [instructor's actual rationale]
- **What I expected:** [actual thesis]
- **What I needed to see:** [actual trigger or condition]
- **What would challenge my idea:** [actual invalidation]
- **How I managed the risk:** [verified process]
- **What happened:** [verified outcome]
- **What I would retain or change:** [instructor's honest review]

## Lesson close — student-facing

> You have now seen how a zone fits into a real decision rather than existing as a drawing on its own.
>
> Notice how many separate judgements were involved: reading context, selecting an area, deciding what evidence mattered, defining risk and responding as the trade developed.
>
> Your goal is not to reproduce my trade. It is to build a process you can explain, question and review.
>
> **Keep this:** A real trade is most useful when you can see what was knowable at the time—not just how the chart looked afterwards.

## Instructor notes

- The final script cannot be completed honestly until the instructor supplies the real trade details and chart files.
- Keep a clear distinction between contemporaneous evidence and retrospective explanation.
- Remove account numbers, private broker details and any information not needed for teaching.
- Do not publish the trade as a recommendation or imply that the same setup should be copied.
- If the trade used options, futures or leveraged instruments, explain only the product mechanics needed to understand the case; keep the focus on the support/resistance decision.
- Include losses or imperfections honestly where relevant. Do not cherry-pick only visually perfect winners.

## Website / learning-room handoff

- Build this as a sequence of locked/revealed stages, not one long page displaying the completed chart.
- Keep the student's earlier notes visible as the instructor's chart is revealed.
- Provide a “compare reasoning” step after the instructor's rationale.
- Do not show profit/loss in the lesson title, thumbnail or opening screen.
- The final review should allow the learner to save a process takeaway.
- If trade screenshots are used, include clear alt text and a zoomable version that preserves axis labels.
- Case-study completion should record the review activity, not imply the student has mastered live execution.

## References and use

- **Grimes, *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis*:** critical thinking, trading context and templates.
- **Shannon, *Technical Analysis Using Multiple Timeframes*:** context across timeframes where it was actually used in the trade.
- **Murphy, *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets*:** broad chart-analysis foundation.
- The primary source for this lesson is the instructor's own verified trade record. Books inform the framework; they do not substitute for the missing trade facts.

# Lesson 17 — Case Study: When the Thesis Fails, or You Stay Out

## Lesson purpose

Demonstrate how to revise or abandon a thesis, review a losing trade without hindsight bias, or explain why standing aside was the more defensible choice. The lesson should make uncertainty feel like part of disciplined analysis rather than something to hide.

**Estimated duration:** 18–24 minutes  
**Chart assets:** A second authentic case, preferably contrasting with Lesson 16  
**Personal charts:** Core of the lesson  
**ChartLab:** None

## Case selection

Choose one of these, in order of teaching usefulness:
1. A real trade where the initial thesis was challenged and the student can see how the instructor responded.
2. A failed breakout or zone that did not behave as expected.
3. A losing trade with a well-documented pre-trade rationale and risk process.
4. A missed trade or deliberate stand-aside decision where the chart was unclear or available space was poor.

Prefer a case that contrasts with Lesson 16. If Lesson 16 was a clean reaction trade, Lesson 17 should ideally demonstrate a thesis that weakened or a decision not to act. Do not manufacture a loss or present a hypothetical trade as personal history.

## Learning outcomes

Students should be able to:
- Identify evidence that weakens a thesis.
- Distinguish a thesis failure from a single ambiguous candle.
- Describe a reasonable response to new evidence without claiming there is only one possible action.
- Separate process quality from profit/loss.
- Explain when standing aside can be a considered decision.

## Lesson flow

1. Set the case-study ground rules (1 min)
2. Reveal the chart before the decision (2 min)
3. Ask the student for an initial reading (2 min)
4. Reveal the instructor's thesis and zone (3 min)
5. Reveal the point where evidence changes (3 min)
6. Ask what the student would reassess (2 min)
7. Reveal actual action and outcome (3 min)
8. Review process, uncertainty and alternative choices (4 min)
9. Takeaway (1 min)

## Student-facing opening

> The previous case showed how a zone can form part of a trade decision. This case asks a different question: what do we do when the evidence no longer supports the idea—or when the chart never gives us enough clarity to act?
>
> A disciplined process is not one that is always right. It is one that can explain why it acted, what would change its view, and how it responds when the situation changes.
>
> We will again reveal the chart in stages. The goal is not to judge the trade by its outcome alone. It is to examine the decisions with the information that was available at the time.

## Stage 1 — Initial chart and zone

Show the pre-decision chart with no outcome labels.

**Prompt:**

> What is the structure? Which area seems relevant? What would make you cautious about treating that area as reliable?

The answer key should mention uncertainty and possible competing interpretations where present. Do not make the setup look more obvious than it was.

## Stage 2 — Instructor thesis

Reveal the original markings and the instructor's actual reasoning.

**Narration template:**

> At the time, I interpreted the chart as [insert actual thesis]. I focused on [insert zone and evidence].
>
> The limitation I could identify was [insert real uncertainty]. The condition that would make me reconsider was [insert actual condition, if documented].
>
> Looking back, it is important to separate what I planned beforehand from what I learned later.

If the instructor did not document a preplanned invalidation, say that plainly and use it as a process lesson. Do not invent a rule to make the trade look more disciplined.

## Stage 3 — Reveal changing evidence

Reveal the next meaningful segment, where the original idea becomes less convincing. The exact candle sequence depends on the selected real case.

**Prompt:**

> Which part of the original thesis is now being challenged? Is the evidence enough to change your view, or is it still ambiguous? Explain what you are using to decide.

**Instructor explanation should identify:**
- The observable change (for example, price returning through the zone or failing to sustain movement beyond it).
- The assumption that is being weakened.
- What is still uncertain.
- Why one candle alone may not settle the question.
- The instructor's actual response, if known.

Do not label it a “failed trade” before the evidence is revealed. If the case is a stand-aside decision, reveal why the setup never met the instructor's conditions instead of inventing a failed entry.

## Stage 4 — Reveal the decision

Show what the instructor actually did: exit, reduce risk, stop pursuing the thesis, wait, or stay out. Do not frame one response as universal.

**Narration template:**

> My response was [insert actual action]. The reason was [insert actual reasoning].
>
> Another trader using a different, clearly defined method might make a different decision. What matters in this case is whether the action was consistent with the stated thesis, risk plan and evidence—not whether we can find a story that makes the outcome seem inevitable afterwards.

## Stage 5 — Reveal outcome and review

Reveal the actual result, including loss, scratch, missed move or no-trade outcome.

**Narration:**

> Now we know what happened next. That information is useful for reviewing the case, but it was not all available at the earlier decision points.
>
> A losing result does not automatically prove that the original analysis was unreasonable. Equally, a profitable result would not prove that the analysis was sound.
>
> Our review asks: Was the zone defensible? Did the evidence change? Did the response match the plan? What would we improve next time?

## Student review worksheet

Students answer:
1. What was the original thesis?
2. Which observations supported it?
3. Which new observation challenged it?
4. Was the change decisive or still ambiguous? Explain.
5. What did the instructor actually do?
6. What alternative response could be reasonable under a different plan?
7. What did the outcome reveal, and what could not have been known earlier?
8. What is the transferable lesson?

### Model response framework

> The original thesis was [scenario], based on [visible evidence]. It became weaker when [new observation]. At that moment, [specific uncertainty] remained. The instructor responded by [actual action] because [actual rationale]. The result was [verified outcome], but the result alone does not tell us whether the earlier decision was good. The main process lesson is [specific lesson].

This is a framework, not a prefilled answer. Populate it only after selecting the real case.

## Alternative case: the decision to stand aside

If the best example is a no-trade decision, structure the lesson as follows:

1. Show the chart and ask students to identify potential zones.
2. Reveal the competing areas or unclear structure.
3. Ask whether the next opposing zone leaves useful space.
4. Reveal the instructor's reason for not trading.
5. Show what happened later only after the reasoning is recorded.
6. Discuss why the later move does not prove that standing aside was wrong.

Student-facing line:

> A market can move without offering a setup that fits your process. Seeing a move afterwards does not mean you should have taken it beforehand. The decision has to be judged using the information and conditions available at the time.

## Knowledge check

**Q1. Does a losing outcome automatically mean the zone was drawn incorrectly?**  
No. Review the evidence, thesis, risk and management separately from the result.

**Q2. Should you ignore evidence that contradicts your thesis because the zone was originally important?**  
No. A zone is not a promise. New evidence may require reassessment.

**Q3. Is standing aside a valid outcome?**  
Yes, when the chart does not meet the student's defined conditions or the uncertainty/risk is not acceptable.

**Q4. Why reveal the trade outcome last?**  
To reduce hindsight bias and let the learner assess decisions using the information available at each point.

## Lesson close — student-facing

> You have now seen that good chart reading is not about defending your first idea. It is about being clear about why you formed it, noticing when the evidence changes, and responding in a way you can explain.
>
> Sometimes that leads to a trade. Sometimes it leads to a change of plan. Sometimes it means doing nothing.
>
> **Keep this:** The purpose of a process is not to remove uncertainty. It is to help you respond to uncertainty deliberately.

## Instructor notes

- Select a real case and verify its timeline before writing the final script.
- Never manufacture an invalidation rule or claim it was preplanned if it was not.
- Be honest if the trade exposed a weakness in the process.
- Do not turn the lesson into motivational messaging about losses. Show the actual evidence and decision.
- Avoid shaming the learner for choosing a different reasonable response.
- Keep the distinction between a trade that failed and an analysis process that failed.

## Website / learning-room handoff

- Reuse the staged reveal system from Lesson 16 so learners learn a consistent case-study interaction.
- Let learners save their pre-outcome reasoning before the outcome is shown.
- After revealing the result, provide a separate review field so the learner can compare initial and retrospective thoughts.
- The completion state should record that the learner completed the review, not that they chose the instructor's exact action.
- Provide a discreet reminder that these are educational case studies, not trade recommendations.

## References and use

- **Grimes, *The Art and Science of Technical Analysis*:** critical evaluation of chart behaviour and trading templates.
- **Osler's support/resistance research:** a reminder that empirical performance is context-specific and should not be generalised into certainty.
- **Murphy and Schwager/Etzkorn:** conventional support/resistance and breakout context.
- The case itself must be based on the instructor's actual record. Do not use a book's example as if it were the instructor's trade.
