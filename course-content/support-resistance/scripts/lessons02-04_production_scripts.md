# Shared production rules for Lessons 2–4

These decisions apply across all three lessons. They are important because the website, chart pack and lesson writing are being planned together.

## The learning sequence is intentional

- **Lessons 2–4 use controlled charts only.** These are purpose-built, fictional illustrative charts made to isolate one concept. They are not meant to imitate a live signal, show a profitable setup or advertise the instructor's trading results.
- **Do not show the instructor's own trade charts in these lessons.** The first short personal-chart example is scheduled for Lesson 9, after students have learned to identify structure, select swings and understand zones. Keep personal charts out of Lessons 2–4 so the student does not have to process the instrument, timeframe, instructor's decisions, trade direction and outcome while learning foundational visual skills.
- **Do not launch ChartLab in Lessons 2–4.** ChartLab Study 1, *Clear W*, remains in Lesson 5. The learner must first understand structure, swings and zone boundaries before being asked to perform a full drawing task.
- **Do not show later candles before the learner has responded.** When a chart is extended to reveal a transition or a later movement, the future portion must be hidden in the initial state.
- **One main question at a time.** No indicators, volume, entry/exit arrows, stop/target, pattern-name labels or extra chart annotations unless explicitly specified.
- **Use uncertainty honestly.** “I am not sure yet” must be a legitimate option. Some structures or boundaries allow more than one reasonable reading.
- **No pixel-perfect scoring.** Evaluate the student's reasoning and whether the interpretation captures the intended structure. The instructor's annotation is a reference example—not a claim that every other drawing is wrong.
- **Separate student-facing copy from production notes.** Only text labelled “On screen” or “Voiceover” is intended for the learner. Bracketed cues, asset IDs, source notes and answer rationales are internal production material.

## Chart-asset convention

Unless a later production decision explicitly selects and verifies historical market data, create the required assets as **schematic charts with fictional prices**. Each schematic must be labelled unobtrusively: *“Illustrative chart — not historical market data.”* Do not use a real instrument name or timeframe for fictional data. Keep candles legible at phone width.

Every delivered chart asset must have:

- asset ID and lesson number;
- author/creator and whether it is schematic or historical;
- teaching purpose and the learner question it supports;
- all reveal states, with annotations separated into layers where practical;
- short alt text describing the teaching-relevant visual information;
- desktop and mobile crops;
- data source and rights/permission notes if historical data is used; and
- confirmation that the unlabelled state contains no accidentally visible answer.

## Website experience required in this phase

The learning room should present a clear lesson title, short progress indication, chart or content area, one prompt, a response control and a reveal/continue action. On mobile, the chart must remain readable above or near its question; instructions must not bury the chart beneath a long wall of text. A learner must be able to return to a previous lesson without losing the fact that it was completed. These are content and UX requirements only; they are not claims that the current website implements them.

# LESSON 02 — Read Structure Before Drawing

**Estimated duration:** 15 minutes  
**Module:** 1 — Understand what you are looking at  
**Format:** Observe → classify → reveal structure → compare → practise → retain  
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 1  
**Do not use:** Personal trade charts, ChartLab, indicators, entries, support/resistance zone drawings or breakout terminology.

## 1. Instructional decision

The student must learn to describe the *shape of price movement* before deciding where to draw a support or resistance area. This lesson introduces relative swing highs and lows, then uses them to distinguish rising structure, falling structure, a trading range and an uncertain transition.

Do not turn this into a trend-trading lesson. We are not teaching moving averages, trendlines, a mechanical definition of market structure or a trade entry. We are giving students a visual vocabulary they can use in the next lesson when they decide which turning points matter.

The important nuance: **a swing high or low is relative to the surrounding price sequence; there is no single candle shape that defines every swing.** A turn is easier to identify after price has moved away from it. Do not imply that a trader always knows a turning point is final in real time.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end, the learner should be able to:

1. Identify a relative swing high and swing low on a simple chart.
2. Describe a chart as generally rising, falling, range-bound or transitional/unclear, using visible price structure.
3. Recognise that trends and ranges may become less clear as price changes behaviour.
4. Explain why structure is examined before support and resistance zones are drawn.

**Not a learning outcome yet:** ranking major versus minor swings, drawing support/resistance zones, predicting breakouts, planning entries or applying a multi-timeframe routine.

## 3. Lesson narrative and reveal timing

### 0:00–0:45 — Set the task

**On screen — title:**

> Read structure before drawing

**On screen — opening copy:**

> Before we mark support and resistance, let's first describe the path price has taken.
>
> Is it generally advancing, declining, moving sideways, or changing between those behaviours? You do not need to predict the next move. Start with what is already visible.

**Production cue:** Use no chart overlay or definition panel yet. Show only the title and short instruction, then present the first chart.

### 0:45–2:20 — Chart A: rising structure

**Show:** A clean, schematic sequence with an overall upward path. Several pullbacks form relative lows, and the price later rises to higher turning points. Include small fluctuations so the exercise does not look artificially perfect, but keep the broad structure legible.

**On screen — prompt:**

> Which description best fits the overall path?

**Options:**
- Price is generally advancing.
- Price is generally declining.
- Price is moving sideways in a range.
- The structure is changing or unclear.
- I am not sure yet.

**Reveal rule:** Record the response without showing the annotations first.

**After response — on-screen feedback:**

> Look at the larger turns, not each individual candle. The path is generally advancing: the later turning points are higher than the earlier ones. Small pauses and pullbacks do not automatically change that broader reading.

**Reveal:** Add three or four major turning-point markers one by one. Mark the sequence of relative lows and highs; do not draw a trendline or support zone.

**Voiceover / student-facing explanation:**

> A chart can move up without every candle rising. What matters here is the overall sequence of turns. In this example, the pullbacks find their way to higher areas, and price then advances to higher turning points. That supports a description of rising structure.

**Instructor note:** “Generally advancing” is a description of the displayed sequence, not a promise that the next move must be higher. Avoid calling it a buy signal.

### 2:20–4:10 — Name the swing points

**Show:** Freeze the rising chart after the structure reveal. Point to one clear local peak and one clear local trough.

**On screen — teaching copy:**

> A **swing high** is a relative turning point where price stopped rising and then moved lower in the surrounding sequence.
>
> A **swing low** is a relative turning point where price stopped falling and then moved higher in the surrounding sequence.
>
> They are identified from the relationship with nearby price action—not from one special candle shape.

**Reveal:** First highlight the swing high, then the swing low. Do not label every tiny fluctuation.

**On screen — check:**

> Which point is the swing low?

Show three candidate markers, only one of which is the clear relative low used in the teaching example. The other two should be minor candle-to-candle fluctuations.

**Correct feedback:**

> Correct. This point stands out because price moved lower into it and then advanced away. We will learn to judge which turning points matter most in the next lesson.

**Incorrect feedback:**

> Look at the nearby sequence. A swing point is a relative turn in the surrounding movement, not simply any candle that is lower than the candle beside it.

**Instructor note:** The phrase “stopped rising” is an educational simplification of the visible local turn, not a claim that a trader could know the final high in real time. The next candles help us recognise the turn retrospectively.

### 4:10–5:50 — Chart B: falling structure

**Show:** A similarly styled schematic chart with an overall downward path. The highs and lows generally move lower, though some upward retracements occur.

**On screen — prompt:**

> This chart contains some upward candles. What best describes its overall structure?

**Options:**
- Generally advancing.
- Generally declining.
- Moving sideways in a range.
- Changing or unclear.
- I am not sure yet.

**Reveal:** Mark a sequence of relative highs and lows, beginning with one high, then a lower high, then a lower low. Continue only as many marks as needed to show the structure.

**On screen — explanation:**

> A declining structure can contain rallies. In this example, the rallies do not undo the broader sequence: later turning points are generally lower than earlier ones.
>
> Describe the sequence you can see. Do not assume that a declining structure must continue indefinitely.

**Instructor note:** Avoid the oversimplification that “red candles mean downtrend.” The point is the relative path of turns, not candle colour.

### 5:50–7:50 — Chart C: a trading range

**Show:** A chart where price moves up and down within a broad, reasonably recognisable upper and lower area. Do not draw the boundaries in the initial state. The turns should not line up perfectly, because the course will later teach that chart areas are approximate.

**On screen — prompt:**

> This chart has both rising and falling sequences. Which description fits the overall behaviour best?

**Options:**
- A clear rising trend.
- A clear falling trend.
- Price is moving back and forth within a broad range.
- The structure is too unclear to describe.

**Reveal:** Add several swing markers, then show a subtle top and bottom bracket spanning the broad trading area. Label only after the learner has answered: **“Trading range.”**

**On screen — explanation:**

> A **trading range** is a period in which price moves back and forth within a broad area rather than making clear, sustained progress in one direction.
>
> The upper and lower boundaries need not be perfectly horizontal, and every high or low need not match the last one exactly. We are describing the overall structure, not measuring a perfect rectangle.

**Instructor note:** Do not call the range boundaries “resistance” and “support” in this lesson. Those labels return when the course is ready to connect structure to zones. The learner only needs to recognise the range shape now.

### 7:50–10:10 — Chart D: a trend-to-range transition

**Show — state 1:** A schematic chart beginning with a clear rising sequence, then several overlapping moves that no longer advance as decisively. Do not show any candles beyond the beginning of the transition.

**On screen — prompt:**

> At what point does the earlier rising behaviour become less clear? Select the first area where you would start questioning the original description.

Allow the learner to tap a candle or choose “I am not sure yet.”

**Reveal — state 2:** Extend the chart to show more overlapping movement within a developing range. Then mark a vertical divider labelled **“Earlier: rising structure”** and **“Later: less directional / possible range.”**

**On screen — explanation:**

> Charts do not always switch from one neat category to another in a single candle.
>
> A move that was clearly advancing can start to lose direction. At first, the new structure may be uncertain. As more price action appears, a range may become easier to recognise.
>
> It is acceptable to say, “The structure is becoming less clear.” You do not need to force an immediate label.

**Instructor note:** Do not use the later range to claim that the transition should have been obvious at the first pause. This is a no-hindsight exercise. The student should recognise that classification can change as evidence arrives.

### 10:10–12:40 — Independent practice: label the structure

**Show:** An unseen mixed chart in a neutral state. It should contain a clear sequence and 3–5 major turns, plus several minor candle fluctuations. This is not a zone-drawing task.

**On screen — instructions:**

> Your turn.
>
> 1. Mark three to five turning points that help explain the overall path.
> 2. Choose the best description of the chart.
> 3. Write one sentence explaining the evidence you used.

**Classification options:**
- Generally rising.
- Generally falling.
- Broad trading range.
- Transition / unclear at this point.

**Reference-answer policy:** The reference overlay should identify a plausible set of major turns, not every possible minor turn. If the chart permits more than one reasonable reading, include a note explaining what evidence favours the reference description and what remains ambiguous.

**Expected reasoning:** The learner points to a sequence of relative highs/lows, repeated boundaries or loss of directional progress. “It looks bullish” or “there are many green candles” is not sufficient on its own.

**Feedback if sound:**

> Your description is tied to the path of price, which is the point of this exercise. Different traders may mark a nearby turn slightly differently; what matters here is whether your reading is supported by the visible sequence.

**Feedback if too many points are marked:**

> You have identified several local turns. Now step back and select the ones that best explain the larger path. We will work on deciding which turns matter most in Lesson 3.

**Feedback if the learner forces a trend label on an ambiguous chart:**

> Recheck the later part of the chart. Is price still making clear progress in one direction, or has the sequence become mixed? “Transition / unclear” is a valid answer when the evidence is mixed.

### 12:40–14:15 — Knowledge check

**Q1. What is a swing low?**
- Any candle with a lower low than the one before it.
- A relative turning point where price fell into an area and then moved higher in the surrounding sequence. **Correct.**
- A guaranteed support zone.

**Feedback:** A swing is a relative turn, not automatically a support zone and not every tiny fluctuation.

**Q2. Which description best fits a trading range?**
- Price moves back and forth within a broad area rather than making clear sustained progress in one direction. **Correct.**
- Every candle has to be the same size.
- Price cannot break out of its boundaries.

**Feedback:** A range describes the displayed behaviour. It is not a permanent container.

**Q3. What should you do when the structure becomes mixed?**
- Force a rising or falling label.
- Add support/resistance zones immediately.
- Describe the uncertainty and wait for more evidence if needed. **Correct.**

**Feedback:** Recognising uncertainty is part of good chart reading.

**Completion rule:** Show corrective feedback and allow retries. Do not require a perfect first attempt. The key objective is accurate structure vocabulary and observable reasoning.

### 14:15–15:00 — Close and handoff

**On screen — takeaway:**

> **Read the path of price before deciding where to draw.**

**On screen — closing copy:**

> You have learned to recognise relative turning points and describe the broader structure. Next, we will decide which of those turning points deserve attention—and which can be left alone.

**Do not preview zones in detail yet.** A short transition sentence is enough.

## 5. Model answers and scoring notes

**A strong response:**
- uses relative turns rather than candle colour alone;
- describes the overall path as rising, falling, ranging or uncertain;
- identifies only as many points as needed to support that description; and
- acknowledges transition when the path becomes mixed.

**Partial response:** The chosen category is plausible, but the explanation cites only one candle or one isolated turn. Feedback should point the learner back to the sequence.

**Weak response:** Every small wiggle is marked as equally important, or the learner states that a turn guarantees the next move.

**Acceptable alternatives:** A chart can contain mixed structure. The answer key should name a primary interpretation and identify where an alternate reading is reasonable. Do not use hard categorical grading for a deliberately transitional example.

# LESSON 03 — Identify Meaningful Swing Points

**Estimated duration:** 15 minutes  
**Module:** 2 — Find the important areas  
**Format:** Revisit → choose → explain → compare → simplify → retain  
**Prerequisite:** Lessons 1–2  
**Do not use:** Personal trade charts, ChartLab, a numeric zone-strength score, support/resistance boundary drawing, or hindsight-based marking.

## 1. Instructional decision

Lesson 2 taught the learner to *recognise* turns. Lesson 3 teaches them to decide which turns deserve attention. That distinction matters: a chart can contain many local peaks and troughs, but treating every one as equally important produces clutter and encourages students to justify almost any later move.

The learner should use three practical filters:

1. **Relative prominence:** Does the turn stand out from nearby movement, or is it only a small fluctuation?
2. **Structural role:** Does it help explain the current sequence, a major change in direction or the edges of a broad range?
3. **Relevance to the current reading:** Does it help answer the question we are examining on this chart, or does it add little beyond what is already shown?

These are reasoning prompts, not a numeric formula. Do not award one point per filter and call the result a predictive “strength score.” A swing can be structurally interesting but not relevant to the specific question currently being studied.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end, the learner should be able to:

1. Select a small set of structurally useful turns from a chart containing many possible local turns.
2. Explain why a selected turn matters in relation to neighbouring price movement.
3. Explain why minor fluctuations need not all be marked.
4. Recognise that relevance is contextual and that two analysts may reasonably select slightly different swings.

**Not a learning outcome yet:** evaluating the quality of an S/R zone, selecting exact zone boundaries, using touch count as a rule, or deciding whether a trade should be taken.

## 3. Lesson narrative and reveal timing

### 0:00–0:45 — Introduce the selection problem

**On screen — title:**

> Identify meaningful swing points

**On screen — opening copy:**

> A chart may contain dozens of small turns. If we mark every one, the larger story becomes harder to see.
>
> This lesson is about choosing the turns that best explain the structure—not about finding a perfect answer that every trader must copy.

**Production cue:** Reuse the visual style from Lesson 2 so the learner does not feel they have entered a different course. No definition or marked chart yet.

### 0:45–2:30 — The over-marked chart

**Show:** A controlled schematic chart containing 8–10 candidate turning points, including several meaningful turns and several small fluctuations. Initially do not label them or put dots on each one.

**On screen — prompt:**

> Imagine you have 30 seconds to explain this chart to another trader. Which three or four turning points would you keep?
>
> You do not need to draw them yet. Choose the points that help you explain the larger path.

**Interaction:** Let the student tap up to four points. Also provide “I would keep more than four because…” as a short written justification, and “I am not sure yet.” The four-point constraint is for this learning exercise, not a universal trading rule.

**After response — feedback:**

> You have started to separate the larger structure from the smaller fluctuations. Now let's make the reasoning explicit.

Do not reveal the reference points yet.

### 2:30–5:15 — Teach the three filters

**Show:** The same chart. Introduce one filter at a time; do not reveal all recommended points in one step.

**On screen — filter 1: Relative prominence**

> **Does the turn stand out?**
>
> A small pause within a larger move may be less useful than a turn that marks a clearer change in the path. Look at the movement before and after the point, not just the candle itself.

**Reveal:** Highlight one major peak/trough and one minor fluctuation. Keep the markers neutral; do not display “good/bad” badges.

**Instructor voiceover:**

> Prominence is relative. We are not looking for the biggest candle or the sharpest wick by itself. We are asking whether the turn helps explain the sequence around it.

**On screen — filter 2: Structural role**

> **Does the turn help explain the structure?**
>
> A point may define a major reversal, help describe a rising or falling sequence, or sit near an edge of a broad trading range. A small turn that does not change our reading may be less important for this task.

**Reveal:** Highlight a point that helps describe the broader path and a minor inside fluctuation that changes little about the overall reading.

**On screen — filter 3: Relevance**

> **Does it help answer the question in front of you?**
>
> A point may be visible but not particularly helpful for the current analysis. Good chart reading includes deciding what not to mark.

**Instructor note:** Do not make “near current price” a mandatory filter in every chart. In this lesson, relevance means contribution to the reading question; a later lesson will more directly consider zone selection and current-price context.

### 5:15–6:45 — Correct two tempting shortcuts

**On screen — heading:**

> Two shortcuts that can mislead you

**Reveal one at a time:**

**Shortcut A:** “Keep the biggest candle.”

**Feedback copy:**

> A large candle can be part of an important move, but candle size alone does not tell you which turning point best describes the structure. Look at the surrounding sequence.

**Shortcut B:** “Keep the most recent turn.”

**Feedback copy:**

> A recent turn may matter, but recency alone does not make it structurally important. Compare it with the broader path and the question you are trying to answer.

**Instructor note:** We are not teaching that size and recency never matter. We are teaching that neither is sufficient as a stand-alone rule.

### 6:45–8:45 — Reveal a reasoned selection

**Show:** Return to the over-marked chart. Reveal the intended reference points sequentially. After each point is highlighted, show a one-sentence rationale.

**Rationale templates for the asset writer:**
- “This turn helps describe the larger move because price moved clearly away and the subsequent sequence develops from it.”
- “This point helps define the broad range in this example.”
- “This smaller fluctuation is left unmarked because it does not materially change our description of the surrounding structure.”

**Important production constraint:** These are rationale templates, not pre-approved answers to assign blindly. The asset designer must write the final rationale to fit the final chart geometry. Every highlighted or omitted point must make visual sense once the chart is actually drawn.

**On screen — explanation:**

> The reference version keeps the points that best explain this chart's structure. Other readings may be reasonable if they are supported by the visible movement.
>
> The goal is not to collect marks. It is to make the chart easier to understand.

### 8:45–11:45 — Independent practice: select and explain

**Show:** A separate unseen controlled chart with 8–10 possible local turns. Do not reuse the same pattern or place the important turns in exactly the same positions as the worked example.

**On screen — instructions:**

> Choose up to four turns that best explain the overall structure.
>
> For each point, finish this sentence:
>
> “I kept this turn because it helps show __________.”
>
> Leave the other turns unmarked. You are allowed to say that one of the points is ambiguous.

**Interaction:** Tap-to-select candidate points, then select a rationale for each or enter a short sentence. Keep the interface light: the student should be reasoning about the chart, not completing a long form.

**Reference answer:** Three or four turns that show the chart's major path. The answer key should explain why each selected turn is useful and why at least two tempting candidates were omitted.

**Feedback if the learner selects many minor points:**

> Your selections include several local fluctuations. Step back and ask which turns change how you describe the larger path. Leave a point out when it adds little to that explanation.

**Feedback if the learner selects only one point:**

> One turn may be noticeable, but a single point often cannot explain the structure by itself. Look for a sequence that shows how price moved before and after it.

**Feedback if the learner's points differ but reasoning is defensible:**

> Your selections are not identical to the reference, but your explanation connects them to the visible structure. That is a defensible way to read a chart. Compare the reference to see what it prioritises differently.

**Feedback if points are selected only because price later moved:**

> Try not to use the later outcome as the only reason a point mattered. Explain what was structurally visible around the turn, without assuming the future move was obvious beforehand.

### 11:45–13:45 — Knowledge check

**Q1. Which is the most useful reason to retain a swing point?**
- It is the most recent point on the chart.
- It has the largest candle.
- It helps explain the broader price structure in the context of the question. **Correct.**

**Feedback:** Size and recency can be observations, but they are not sufficient on their own.

**Q2. If a chart has many visible local turns, what should you do?**
- Mark all of them equally.
- Select the points that best explain the structure and leave less useful turns unmarked. **Correct.**
- Keep only the turns followed by profitable trades.

**Feedback:** This is a structural reading exercise, not a hindsight contest.

**Q3. Can another trader mark a slightly different set of meaningful turns?**
- No, only one exact set is legitimate.
- Yes, if the alternative is supported by the chart and explained consistently. **Correct.**
- Yes, any random selection is equally useful.

**Feedback:** Reasonable interpretation is not the same as arbitrary interpretation. Evidence and explanation matter.

### 13:45–15:00 — Close and handoff

**On screen — takeaway:**

> **Choose the turns that explain the chart; leave the noise out.**

**On screen — closing copy:**

> You can now recognise structure and choose a smaller set of meaningful turns. Next, we will look at why the reactions associated with support and resistance are often better represented as areas than as perfect lines.

**Do not start teaching zone boundaries in the closing paragraph.** The next lesson should introduce that idea with its own chart and demonstration.

## 4. Chart pack and annotation specification

### L03-A — Over-marked teaching chart
- **Purpose:** Show many possible turns and teach the selection filters.
- **Geometry:** 8–10 visible candidate turning points; a clear overall path; at least two minor inside fluctuations; at least three candidates that can be reasonably prioritised by structural role.
- **Initial state:** Plain chart, no dots or labels.
- **State 2:** Candidate points can be tapped. Candidate IDs may appear after the initial observation to support interaction; do not put the intended answer in the IDs.
- **State 3:** Three/four intended reference points revealed sequentially with rationale.
- **State 4:** Over-marked view fades minor points and retains the reference set. Use this only as a visual comparison, not as a claim that every omitted point is objectively meaningless.

### L03-B — Independent practice chart
- Different visual shape from L03-A, with 8–10 plausible turns.
- Include a deliberately borderline candidate for which the key discusses ambiguity.
- Provide a reference set, but also an “acceptable alternative” note for any close case.
- The asset writer must freeze the final chart first, then assign candidate IDs and write the answer key against the finished geometry. Do not write a fixed key before the chart exists.

### Required annotation register entries
For every reference point, record:
- candidate ID;
- whether it is selected in the reference set;
- its structural role in the actual chart;
- the visible movement that supports this explanation;
- if omitted, why omission improves the current reading; and
- whether an alternate selection would also be acceptable.

**Alt text example:** “Illustrative candlestick chart with several candidate swing points; the reveal highlights a smaller set of turns that best explains the broad price path.”

## 5. Model answers and scoring notes

**Full-credit rationale:** A selected swing is explained through surrounding movement or its role in the broad structure, and the learner recognises that not every fluctuation needs a marker.

**Partial credit:** The learner picks a plausible turn but gives a weak reason such as “it looks important” without referring to the price sequence.

**Needs review:** The learner marks every local candle as equally important, gives a numeric strength score with no basis, or chooses points solely because of what happened later.

**Do not assess:** Exact selection of the same three or four points as the reference when another set is defensible. The purpose is to develop judgement, not enforce one visual answer.

Lesson 3 goes beyond memorising a definition by asking students to select among many visible turns. [Penguin Random House](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/350647/technical-analysis-of-the-financial-markets-by-john-j-murphy/)

Here, refer to them as structural points only; do not leap to zone-strength ranking or breakout rules. [Wiley — Chapter 7](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119209713.ch7) · [Wiley — Chapter 8](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119209713.ch8)

# LESSON 04 — Zones, Not Perfect Lines

**Estimated duration:** 15 minutes  
**Module:** 2 — Find the important areas  
**Format:** Notice variation → compare representations → explain boundaries → practise → retain  
**Prerequisite:** Lessons 1–3  
**Do not use:** Personal trade charts, ChartLab, exact-price “correctness,” fixed tick-distance rules, or entries/stops/targets.

## 1. Instructional decision

Students already understand that prior reactions can make an area worth examining and that structure contains meaningful turns. Now they must understand why related reactions may occur near one another without matching at exactly the same price.

Teach a **zone as a practical representation of a group of related price reactions**, not a precisely measurable physical barrier. Wicks show how far price travelled during a candle; candle bodies and closes show other parts of the same price action. None is universally the only correct reference. The analyst must choose a boundary convention that captures the relevant behaviour while keeping the zone useful.

This lesson is a **boundary-judgement lesson, not a full drawing lab**. Students first compare examples and defend a choice. ChartLab does not begin until Lesson 5.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end, the learner should be able to:

1. Explain why nearby reactions may not align to one exact price.
2. Describe how wicks, bodies and closes can inform boundary choices.
3. Recognise the drawbacks of a zone that is too narrow or too broad.
4. Select and explain a defensible zone around related reactions while acknowledging that other boundaries may also be reasonable.

**Not a learning outcome yet:** a complete support/resistance drawing workflow, zone-strength scoring, selecting every important zone on a complex chart, entries/stops, or ChartLab submission.

## 3. Lesson narrative and reveal timing

### 0:00–0:45 — Introduce the boundary problem

**On screen — title:**

> Zones, not perfect lines

**On screen — opening copy:**

> Price often reacts around a similar area without turning at exactly the same price each time.
>
> If we insist on one perfect line, we may ignore part of what the chart shows. If we draw an area too wide, it may stop telling us anything useful. Let's examine that trade-off.

**Production cue:** Keep this to two short paragraphs. Move directly to the chart.

### 0:45–2:30 — Unlabelled chart: notice the variation

**Show:** Chart L04-A, a schematic price sequence with three related lower reactions. Their wick lows differ; bodies and closes cluster somewhat higher. Each reaction must be visually related to the same general area, but not identical. Price moves away from each area sufficiently for the learner to recognise the reactions.

**On screen — prompt:**

> Look closely at the three reactions. Do they turn at exactly the same price?

**Options:**
- Yes, the low is identical each time.
- No, the lows vary, but the reactions are in a similar area.
- The chart does not show enough to compare.
- I am not sure yet.

**After response — feedback:**

> Notice the small differences between the reactions. The chart can show a related area without giving us one perfectly identical turning price.

**Instructor note:** Do not reveal an overlay during this question. Let the student observe the price variation.

### 2:30–4:15 — Name the practical representation

**Reveal:** Add a broad neutral bracket around the related reactions. Do not call it the final answer yet.

**On screen — teaching copy:**

> A **zone** is an area used to describe related price reactions. Its boundaries help us organise the evidence; they are not a claim that every reaction must stop at the same tick.
>
> We want the area to include the behaviour we are trying to describe, without expanding so far that unrelated price movement becomes part of the same explanation.

**Voiceover:**

> There is judgement in this choice. The aim is not to make price fit a perfect line. The aim is to draw an area we can explain consistently from the chart.

**Instructor note:** The broad bracket is an introduction, not the defended final boundary. The next stages explain why it has the width it does.

### 4:15–6:30 — Wicks, bodies and closes

**Show:** Zoom to a single reaction candle, with wick and body visibly distinguishable. Then return to the cluster of reactions.

**On screen — reveal 1: Wicks**

> **Wicks** show the extremes price reached during the candle. They may help us see how far a reaction travelled, even if the candle did not finish near that extreme.

**On screen — reveal 2: Bodies and closes**

> **Bodies and closes** show where the candle opened and finished. They may help us judge where price spent more of the candle's visible range or where it ended the period.

**On screen — reveal 3: choosing a reference**

> Wicks, bodies and closes give us different information. Neither a wick-only nor a close-only boundary is the universal answer for every chart.
>
> Choose the reference that best explains the group of reactions you are examining, then apply that reasoning consistently.

**Instructor note / guardrail:** Do not say that a long wick proves buyers or sellers “defended” a price, nor that bodies always matter more than wicks. A candle records price movement; the chart alone does not prove the intentions of participants.

### 6:30–8:45 — Compare three boundary choices

**Show:** The same L04-A chart in three sequential states. The underlying candles remain identical; only the proposed overlay changes.

**State A — too narrow:** A hairline or very thin band captures one wick extreme but excludes the other clearly related reactions.

**On screen — feedback:**

> This boundary is so narrow that it leaves out much of the variation we are trying to describe. It may be precise on the screen, but it does not represent the group of reactions very well.

**State B — defensible reference zone:** A band spans the relevant cluster of lower reactions and related body/close area, while remaining clearly separated from unrelated movement.

**On screen — feedback:**

> This is a defensible zone for this example. It captures the related reactions and leaves enough distinction for us to explain where the area sits.
>
> Another analyst may choose a slightly different boundary and still have a reasonable explanation.

**State C — too broad:** A large band captures the reaction cluster plus substantial unrelated movement above and below.

**On screen — feedback:**

> This band is so wide that it covers much more than the reactions we are trying to describe. It becomes harder to explain what the zone actually represents.

**Production requirement:** Do not call State B “the objectively correct zone” or score it as a single exact numerical answer. Its purpose is to demonstrate a reasoned middle ground.

### 8:45–10:00 — Show that the same reasoning can apply at resistance

**Show:** A second short schematic chart containing several related upper reactions. Initially unlabelled; wick highs differ and the bodies/closes occur somewhat lower.

**On screen — prompt:**

> The reactions are now near the upper part of the chart. What should remain consistent about the way you choose the area?

**Options:**
- The zone should capture the related reactions without becoming unnecessarily broad.
- The zone must touch every wick exactly and have no width.
- The zone should be as wide as possible so price cannot leave it.
- I am not sure yet.

**Reveal:** Show a defensible upper reaction band.

**On screen — explanation:**

> The direction of the reactions changes; the reasoning does not. Look at the related price behaviour, choose boundaries you can explain, and avoid pretending the edge is perfectly exact.

**Instructor note:** Do not yet teach how to select the most important resistance zone on a complicated chart. Lesson 5 teaches prioritisation and clutter.

### 10:00–12:30 — Independent practice: defend a zone

**Show:** An unseen chart with a cluster of related reactions. Do not show any proposed zone first.

**On screen — instructions:**

> Choose a zone around the related reactions.
>
> Then complete this sentence:
>
> **“I chose these boundaries because…”**
>
> You may use wicks, bodies/closes, or a combination. Your explanation matters more than matching the reference drawing exactly.

**Interaction:** Prefer a simple zone-width selection or chart tap/control that can be used on mobile. If an actual drawing interaction is not available during content review, use three candidate overlays of different widths and ask the student to select and explain. Do not force a fine drag gesture on a phone for this concept lesson.

**Reference key:** Identify a defendable zone, and explain what the upper and lower boundaries include. Add notes explaining why the narrow alternative misses relevant reactions and why the broad alternative absorbs unrelated movement.

**Feedback if the zone is too narrow:**

> Your zone captures one extreme, but consider whether it represents the other related reactions too. What evidence would be left outside your boundary?

**Feedback if the zone is too broad:**

> Your zone includes the reaction cluster, but also a large amount of unrelated movement. Try reducing its width while still accounting for the reactions you chose to describe.

**Feedback if the zone differs from the reference but is defensible:**

> Your boundaries differ slightly from the reference, but your explanation uses the visible candles consistently. That is a reasonable interpretation. Compare what each boundary includes and excludes.

**Feedback if the reason is “because that is where price will turn”:**

> We cannot know that a zone will hold next time. Explain the boundary from the reactions visible in the chart, rather than from a prediction about future price.

### 12:30–14:15 — Knowledge check

**Q1. Why might a zone be more useful than one thin line?**
- Related reactions may occur across a range of prices rather than at an identical point. **Correct.**
- A zone guarantees price cannot pass through it.
- A line is never useful in any circumstance.

**Feedback:** Some traders may use lines in particular contexts. This lesson teaches why a zone is a practical representation when related reactions vary.

**Q2. Which statement about wicks and bodies is most accurate?**
- Wicks always determine the only correct boundary.
- Candle bodies and closes must always determine the only correct boundary.
- Wicks, bodies and closes show different aspects of price movement; the chosen reference should fit the reactions being explained. **Correct.**

**Feedback:** Explain the information used and keep the method consistent.

**Q3. What is the main problem with a zone that is extremely wide?**
- It may include so much unrelated movement that it becomes less informative. **Correct.**
- It guarantees a loss.
- It ensures the zone will always hold.

**Feedback:** The band should explain a meaningful area; maximum width is not the goal.

### 14:15–15:00 — Close and handoff

**On screen — takeaway:**

> **A useful zone captures the relevant reactions without pretending the boundary is exact.**

**On screen — closing copy:**

> You have seen why related reactions may spread across an area. Next, we will bring the skills together: choosing the areas that matter, reducing chart clutter, and then practising your first complete support/resistance drawing in ChartLab.

**This is the first mention that ChartLab is coming next. Do not launch it early from this lesson.**

## 4. Chart pack and annotation specification

### L04-A — Lower reactions with wick/body variation

**Purpose:** Main teaching chart for the difference between a line and a zone.

**Schematic geometry:**
- Three related lower reactions occur in the same broad region.
- Wick extremes differ modestly rather than matching exactly.
- Bodies/closes sit somewhat above the lowest wick points and cluster in a related area.
- Each reaction has enough subsequent movement away to be visually recognisable.
- No later breakout or trade outcome is required; the focus is the boundary, not prediction.

**Illustrative coordinates for the asset designer only:** One possible fictional design might put wick lows near 98.2, 98.4 and 98.7, with relevant body/close references around 98.9–99.3. These numbers are only a drawing specification for a schematic illustration; they are **not a universal zone-width rule** and must not be shown as a trading formula. Final values may change to make the visual geometry coherent.

**Required reveal states:**
1. Plain chart.
2. Broad neutral bracket around the related reactions.
3. Too-narrow line/band.
4. Defensible reference zone.
5. Too-broad band.
6. Side-by-side or toggle comparison of all three, with the same candles fixed underneath.

### L04-B — Upper reactions
- A separate short schematic chart with several upper reactions that vary slightly in wick highs and body/close locations.
- Initial state unlabelled.
- Reveal one defensible resistance-area band after the learner answers.
- Avoid pattern labels such as “double top,” entry arrows, or any implication that the next move is certain.

### L04-C — Independent practice chart
- An unseen group of related reactions that differ slightly in price.
- A reasonable zone should be possible, but the boundaries should not have a unique exact-price solution.
- Provide a reference boundary and a written “acceptable alternative” note.
- Include a chart version with the reference overlay hidden and an annotated answer version.

**Alt text example:** “Illustrative candlestick chart showing three lower reactions with slightly different wick lows; a broad shaded area groups the related reactions.”

## 5. Model answers and scoring notes

**Full-credit reasoning:**
- describes the zone as an area around related visible reactions;
- names the reference used (wicks, bodies/closes or combination);
- explains what the boundaries include and exclude; and
- avoids saying the zone guarantees a future reaction.

**Partial credit:** The learner chooses a broadly plausible band but cannot explain why it is wide or narrow.

**Needs review:** The learner treats the highest/lowest wick as a mandatory boundary in every situation, says the zone must hold, or draws an area so broad that it no longer isolates the reactions being described.

**Acceptable alternatives:** Two boundaries can both be defensible if each captures the relevant reaction group, the convention is consistent and the width remains useful. The answer key should state why the instructor prefers one, not mark every other boundary wrong.

**No numeric scoring:** Do not assign a universal tick width, percentage width or volatility multiple in this introductory lesson. Those would introduce rules outside the agreed curriculum and would vary across instruments/timeframes.

## Why the sequencing matters

Lesson 2 teaches **what the path looks like**. Lesson 3 teaches **which turns help explain that path**. Lesson 4 teaches **why related reactions may occupy an area instead of a perfect price line**. Only after those three capabilities—and Lesson 5's prioritisation explanation—does the learner perform the first complete ChartLab drawing activity.

This order prevents a common course-design failure: asking a beginner to drag a zone on a chart before they understand what they are looking for, then grading them against an instructor's unexplained drawing. It also leaves your own trading charts available for the later moment when they become most useful: showing how these foundations inform a real decision without overwhelming the learner.
