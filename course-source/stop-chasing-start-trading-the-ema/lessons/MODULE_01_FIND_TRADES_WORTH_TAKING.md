# Module 1 — Find Trades Worth Taking

**Module purpose:** Move from “the EMAs look bullish” to a complete, conditional trade idea. We will use three setups: trend pullback, reclaim/rejection and breakout-retest continuation. None is an automatic signal.

The EMA is not the strategy. The market condition, price structure, entry trigger, invalidation and execution plan are the strategy.

---

## Lesson 1.1 — Qualify the Market Before Looking for an Entry

### Start with the market, not the line

A chart can show a neat bullish EMA stack and still be a poor place to buy. Perhaps price has stopped making higher highs. Perhaps the market has spent two weeks crossing the same averages. Perhaps the next resistance is so close that even a correct entry has little room to run.

Before looking for an entry, answer a simpler question: **is this a market in which a directional EMA setup has a reasonable chance of getting follow-through?**

Use the following sequence. It is deliberately short.

1. **Structure:** Is price making higher highs and higher lows for a long idea, or lower highs and lower lows for a short idea? A neat average stack without progress in price is not enough.
2. **Average behaviour:** Are the averages moving in the direction of the idea, or are they flat and tangled? Treat this as context, not a green light.
3. **Progress:** Is price actually travelling in one direction, or repeatedly returning to the same area? Look at the distance between meaningful swing points, not just the distance between the lines.
4. **Location:** Is there room before the next obvious swing, range edge, supply/demand area or higher-timeframe level?
5. **Execution:** Is the spread reasonable, is liquidity sufficient, and is an event or gap risk likely to distort the planned stop?

A market qualifies for further study when several pieces of evidence agree. You do not need every indicator to agree. You do need to know what would prove your reading wrong.

### The fast market filter

| Observation | Supports a directional setup | Warns you to wait |
|---|---|---|
| Swing structure | Clear sequence of higher lows / lower highs | Repeated breaks that immediately reverse |
| 20/50 EMA relationship | Ordered and visibly moving | Flat, intertwined or frequently crossing |
| Price progress | Swings are extending in the trend direction | Price keeps returning to the same range |
| Pullbacks | Controlled; prior swing structure holds | Deep, fast and increasingly damaging |
| Room | Clear space to the next obstacle | Nearby resistance/support leaves little payoff |
| Execution | Normal spread and liquidity | Wide spread, thin book, event gap or poor fill |

No single row decides the trade. The point is to stop treating the EMA alignment as a complete market assessment.

In a range, averages may continue to point in the old direction for some time. Price can cross them repeatedly while the market itself makes no meaningful progress. If you buy every return to the 20 EMA, the range can take you in and out several times before a real move appears.

### Your decision

Look at a chart of a liquid instrument on your usual timeframe. Hide the most recent 5–10 candles. Write:

- the latest two meaningful swing highs and lows;
- whether the average stack and slope support the same direction;
- whether price has made progress in that direction;
- the nearest obstacle;
- what would make you abandon the directional idea.

Then choose: **qualifies for a setup, wait for more evidence, or no-trade market**.

**Keep this:** an EMA stack tells you how recent prices are arranged. The market's structure and progress tell you whether that arrangement is being supported by actual movement.

## Lesson 1.2 — The Trend-Pullback Setup

### The idea

A pullback setup tries to join an existing trend after price has moved back toward a short- or intermediate-term average. The aim is not to buy the lowest candle of the pullback. It is to wait for evidence that the pullback is losing control and the prevailing direction may be resuming.

The 20 EMA is a common reference on daily charts; faster traders may use a different period and slower traders may use a longer one. The period is a test parameter, not a universal setting. This lesson uses 20 EMA and 200-day SMA/EMA as an example rule set because it is easy to state and compare with published tests.

### Long setup: conditions before the trigger

**Market condition**

- Price is above a rising 200-period trend reference on the same daily chart, or the learner has another clearly documented higher-timeframe trend filter.
- The 20 EMA is rising and price structure is making higher highs and higher lows.
- The previous impulse has created enough distance to the next meaningful resistance for a trade to be worthwhile.

**Pullback condition**

- Price retraces toward the 20 EMA rather than falling in a series of wide, accelerating candles.
- The pullback does not decisively break the swing low that supports the bullish structure.
- The chart is not simply moving sideways through the average.

**Trigger**

Do not buy merely because the low touches the EMA. Wait for a defined price event: for example, a completed candle that rejects the pullback area and closes back in the direction of the trend, followed by a break of that candle's high. A learner can choose a different trigger, but it must be defined before reviewing the historical outcome.

**Invalidation and stop**

The thesis is invalidated when the structure that justified the long is broken. For many pullback plans that is below the pullback swing low, with a buffer suited to the instrument's tick size and volatility. The EMA itself is not automatically the stop. A close below the average can be a warning; the structural invalidation level must be explicit.

**Target and room**

Mark the next prior swing high or other meaningful obstacle before entering. If the entry-to-obstacle distance is too small relative to the distance to the stop, do not take the trade. Do not move the target simply to make the reward-to-risk ratio look attractive.

### Short setup: mirror the logic, not the assumptions

For a short, look for a falling trend reference, lower highs and lower lows, and a controlled bounce toward the EMA. Wait for sellers to show up and define a trigger below the rejection candle. Invalidation belongs above the bounce high or another structural level that would disprove the bearish thesis. Check the next support before entering; a short directly above major support may have very little room.

### What our own comparison found

TradeThesis compared two ways of entering the same daily, long-side pullback context on NIFTY 50, Reliance, HDFC Bank and Infosys, from 1 January 2016 to 14 January 2026. The touch-only control entered at the next session's open after the first qualifying approach to the 20 EMA. The confirmation method waited for a bullish rejection candle and a break above its high.

| Entry method | Trades | Win rate | Mean result | Profit factor |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Touch-only control | 116 | 35.3% | +0.449R | 1.465 |
| Rejection-and-break confirmation | 22 | 40.9% | -0.023R | 0.962 |

The confirmation method won more often, but its average result and profit factor were worse in this sample. It also produced far fewer trades. **A higher win rate did not mean a better result.** This does not prove that touch entries are generally superior. It tells us only that this particular confirmation rule did not improve this particular test.

The test is exploratory, long-only, pre-cost and limited to four instruments. It does not validate short setups, intraday trading or options execution. Lesson 2.4 examines the result and its limitations.

### What makes this setup poor?

Pass on a pullback when:

- the higher-high/higher-low sequence has already failed;
- the pullback is impulsive and larger than the preceding swing suggests;
- the 20 and 50 EMAs are flat and repeatedly crossed;
- the entry would be far from the pullback low because price has already run;
- the next obstacle is close;
- the spread or likely slippage makes the planned stop unrealistic.

### Practice

Find one bullish and one bearish example. For each, record the market filter, pullback area, signal candle, trigger, invalidation, stop, target and cancellation condition. Then find a third chart that looks similar but should be rejected. Explain which condition fails.

**Keep this:** the touch is only the location. The trigger is the event. The swing structure defines whether the idea is still valid.

## Lesson 1.3 — The EMA Reclaim and Rejection Setup

### A reclaim is a sequence, not a candle

A reclaim occurs when price moves through an average and then gets back on the intended side. In an established uptrend, a temporary move below the 20 EMA followed by a convincing recovery may show that the pullback has failed to develop into a deeper reversal. In a downtrend, a bounce above the average that fails and closes back below it may create a rejection candidate.

The important word is **sequence**. A single candle crossing the line is not enough.

### Bullish reclaim: what must be visible

1. The wider structure still supports an uptrend. The prior swing low that matters has not been broken.
2. Price moves below the chosen EMA. This is a warning, not an automatic buy.
3. Price reclaims the EMA on a completed candle. Ideally the close is not merely one tick above the line; the candle should show a meaningful response relative to recent bars.
4. The next trigger is defined. One simple method is to buy only if price breaks the reclaim candle's high. Another is to wait for a retest that holds above the reclaimed area. Pick one rule and test it.
5. The stop is tied to the low that invalidates the reclaim, not to an arbitrary fixed number of points.

For a bearish rejection, reverse the sequence: established downtrend, bounce through the EMA, failure to hold above it, close back below, then a trigger below the rejection candle or failed retest. The invalidation is above the bounce high.

### A chart example: average, resistance and a failed push

Notice that the average is not acting alone. The chart includes a horizontal resistance area and a volatile response. The bearish idea becomes more interesting because the move into resistance fails and price then loses the average. If the same EMA cross had occurred in the middle of an empty range, the case would be weaker.

### What a reclaim does not prove

It does not prove that the original trend has resumed. It does not prove buyers have control on every timeframe. It does not make the EMA a physical support line. A reclaim can fail immediately, especially when the broader structure is deteriorating or the market is reacting to a news event.

Avoid turning the sequence into a mechanical instruction such as “close above the 20 EMA, buy.” Ask where the price came from, what level it reclaimed, whether the prior swing structure is intact, and how much room remains.

### Entry planning

A reclaim/rejection plan should specify:

- the broader trend condition;
- the key level and the direction of the failed move;
- the reclaim/rejection candle or retest definition;
- the actual entry trigger;
- the structural invalidation level;
- the next obstacle and acceptable room;
- a time or price condition that cancels an unfilled order.

If you cannot point to the event that turns the idea into an order, you have an observation, not a trade.

### Practice: separate a reclaim from a random cross

Review 20 historical examples on your chosen instrument and timeframe. Mark every close that crosses the EMA. Then classify each one:

- trend structure intact and a reclaim sequence followed;
- trend structure intact but no trigger followed;
- structure already broken;
- range / repeated cross;
- event-driven gap or shock.

Do not count only the examples that later moved in your favour. The rejected examples are the part that teaches you whether the rule has a useful boundary.

**Keep this:** a reclaim or rejection becomes a candidate only when the larger structure, the level, the price response and the risk location make sense together.

## Lesson 1.4 — Breakout, Retest and Trend Continuation

### Do not chase the first large candle

A breakout candle can make a chart look obvious after the move. At the moment it appears, however, the entry may already be extended and the stop may be wide. A continuation plan waits for the market to demonstrate that it can hold the breakout area.

This setup is useful when the market has been consolidating inside a defined range while the broader structure remains directional. It is not a reason to trade every range break.

### The long-side sequence

1. **Context:** the higher-timeframe trend supports a long, or the breakout is part of a clearly defined new directional structure.
2. **Base:** price has formed a recognisable range or pause. Mark its boundaries before the breakout.
3. **Break:** a completed candle closes above the range high. A wick through the level that closes back inside is not a confirmed break under this rule.
4. **Retest:** price returns toward the broken boundary and does not decisively fail back into the old range. The retest may not happen; do not chase if it runs away.
5. **Trigger:** use a pre-defined response, such as a completed bullish rejection candle followed by a break of its high.
6. **Invalidation:** a decisive return into the range or a break of the retest low tells you the breakout thesis has failed.
7. **Room:** the next higher-timeframe obstacle must leave enough space for the planned risk.

For a short, reverse the levels and conditions.

### What the EMA contributes

The EMA helps describe whether the pause sits within an established trend and whether the average is still moving in the direction of the intended trade. It is not the breakout level unless the trading plan explicitly defines it as one. The range boundary and the price response carry the decision.

A crossover that occurs after the breakout is not a substitute for the trigger. It may simply confirm a move that has already travelled too far. Treat it as context, not an order.

### Common failure patterns

- **False break:** price trades above the range high but closes back inside.
- **Breakout without room:** price clears the level directly into major resistance.
- **No retest:** price accelerates away; the plan expires rather than becoming a chase.
- **Weak retest:** price returns into the range and cannot reclaim the boundary.
- **Late retest:** the stop must be so wide that the target no longer compensates for the risk.

### Practice: draw the plan before the breakout

Select a chart with a well-defined consolidation. Before revealing the next candles, mark the range, the breakout level, the nearest obstacle, the level that would invalidate the idea and the trigger you would use after a retest. Then reveal what happened. Record whether you followed the plan, not just whether the market eventually rose or fell.

### Module 1 decision lab — prepare, enter or pass

For each chart, choose one of three decisions and explain it in a short trade note.

**Case A — Pullback candidate:** the daily structure remains higher-high / higher-low, the 20 EMA rises above a rising 50 EMA, price has retraced toward the 20 EMA and the prior swing low is intact. The next resistance is close. Your task is not to say “buy.” Decide whether there is enough room, what trigger would be required and whether the setup should be passed.

**Case B — Reclaim candidate:** price dips below the 20 EMA, recovers it on a completed candle and then retests. The wider swing structure remains intact. Define the trigger, stop and the exact price event that cancels the idea.

**Case C — Breakout candidate:** price closes above a consolidation range but the candle is unusually large and the next resistance is nearby. Decide whether you will wait for a retest, reject the trade or use another plan. Do not invent a fill at the breakout close after seeing the full candle.

**Answer standard:** a good answer identifies the evidence, the missing condition, the trigger, the invalidation and the room. A directional opinion without those parts is incomplete.
