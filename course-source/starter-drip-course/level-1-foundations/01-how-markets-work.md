# Day 1 — How Markets Actually Work

**🎬 The story of 45 minutes that broke Wall Street's brain**

On May 6, 2010, at 2:32 PM New York time, the Dow Jones was having a bad but ordinary
day — down about 300 points. Then, in the next 36 minutes, it fell nearly 1,000 points
more. For a few minutes, shares of the consulting firm Accenture traded at **one cent**.
PG&E — a boring, stable utility — traded at **$39,999.99** a share. P&G itself crashed
37% in minutes, then recovered almost everything before the closing bell.

No asteroid hit. No bank collapsed. What happened was simpler and more important:
one large mutual-fund company started selling about **$4.1 billion** worth of
stock-market bets, all at once, into a market where buyers had quietly stepped away. For a few minutes, there were almost no agreements between buyers and
sellers at *any* price. So prices — remember, prices are just the last agreement —
printed absurd numbers until buyers returned. The SEC later called it the Flash Crash.

Sit with that: the most sophisticated market on Earth briefly priced Accenture at 1
cent and a power company at $39,999.99. Not because anyone decided those were fair
prices — but because those were the last agreements between the few buyers and sellers
still standing.

**That is the entire subject of today's lesson.** Once you truly understand what a price
is, 80% of trading confusion evaporates.

## What you'll learn
- What a "price" actually is (the Flash Crash makes it obvious)
- Why prices move up and down
- Who you're trading against

## A market is an auction — nothing more

Forget the news, the indicators, the opinions. At its core, every market is an auction
with one item for sale and millions of bidders.

- A **buyer** says: *"I'll pay ₹24,100 for one Nifty futures contract."*
- A **seller** says: *"I'll only sell at ₹24,120."*
- When a buyer agrees to pay the seller's price (or vice versa), a **trade happens** —
  and the price on your screen updates to that last traded price.

**The price on your screen is simply the price of the most recent agreement between one
buyer and one seller.** Accenture at ₹0.01 wasn't a valuation — it was the last
agreement in a temporarily empty room.

## Why prices move

Prices move for one reason only:

> **When aggressive buyers outnumber aggressive sellers, price rises. When aggressive
> sellers outnumber aggressive buyers, price falls.**

- **Aggressive buyer** = someone who hits "buy now" and accepts the seller's asking price.
- **Aggressive seller** = someone who hits "sell now" and accepts the buyer's price.

If 1,000 people suddenly want to buy and only 200 want to sell at the current price,
the buyers consume the available sellers. The next sellers demand more. Price rises.
The Flash Crash was the extreme version: sellers screamed, buyers vanished, and price
fell off a cliff until new buyers found the prices attractive enough to agree again.

## Who is on the other side of your trade?

You are never trading against "the market." You're trading against *people*:

| Participant | What they want | How it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| Retail traders (like you) | Quick profits | Small, scattered orders |
| Institutions (mutual funds, FIIs/DIIs) | Fill huge positions over days | Sustained one-directional moves |
| Market makers | Earn the spread, stay flat | Constant two-sided quotes |
| Hedgers | Protect existing holdings | Predictable orders at levels |

Beginners imagine they're trading against geniuses. Mostly you're trading against
*patterns of behavior* — and behavior repeats. That's why price patterns exist at all.

## 📌 Case file: March 2020 — the panic you will live through again

Between February 19 and March 23, 2020, the S&P 500 fell **34% in 23 trading days** —
the fastest bear market in history. Nifty did the same in lockstep. News readers were
paralyzed; auction readers saw something simpler: aggressive sellers everywhere, buyers
only at lower and lower prices. Every day, the last agreement printed lower. Then on
March 23, buyers stepped back in aggressively — and the same mechanism ran in reverse:
the S&P rose roughly 50% in five months and made new all-time highs by August.

No prediction was needed. Only the ability to read *who is more aggressive right now* —
the skill this course builds from the ground up.

## 🇮🇳 The Indian story: circuits, panic, and the 7,511 bottom

You lived the Indian version of the Flash Crash. On March 13, 2020, Nifty hit its 10%
lower circuit within minutes of opening — trading was frozen, and thousands of sell
orders sat frozen with it. On March 23, it happened again, and Nifty touched **7,511
intraday** — down ~39% in five weeks. India VIX, the fear gauge, spiked past **83**, the
highest reading anywhere in the world at that time. Same mechanism as May 2010: sellers
screaming, buyers gone, last agreements printing lower by the minute. Then, on March 24,
buyers stepped back in aggressively — and Nifty roughly **doubled within eight months**,
closing 2020 near 13,982. No prediction needed — just reading who was more aggressive,
day by day.

## Key takeaways
- Price = the most recent agreement between a buyer and a seller. (Flash Crash: 1 cent
  to $39,999.99 proves it.)
- Price moves when one side becomes more aggressive than the other.
- You trade against repeating human behavior, not against "the market."
