# Day 13 — Moving Averages

**🎬 The line that saved investors in 2008 — and the crosses that lie on schedule**

From January 2008 to March 2009, the S&P 500 fell roughly 57%. For almost that entire
period — over a full year — the index traded **below its 200-day moving average**, and
every rally back toward the line was sold. An investor with one rule — "I'm long only
above the 200-day" — would have sidestepped the worst crash since the Depression, then
been back in by May 2009 when price reclaimed the line. No prediction. No genius. One
filter.

Now the cautionary tale. Fast forward to March 14, 2022: the S&P printed a "death cross"
(50-day crossing below the 200-day) and financial media ran ominous segments. It sounded
meaningful — and the market indeed fell further. But flip through history: December 2018's
death cross came within days of the *bottom*, and whipsawed bull-market followers. Even
in 2022, by the time the cross printed, half the decline was already over.

Same indicator. One use saves you in 2008; the other use fakes you out in 2018. Today
you learn the difference — and it's the difference between using moving averages and
being used by them.

> 💬 **Honest one:** in 2008, Nifty stayed below its 200-day average for 17 months. Do you think you could have stayed out that long while everyone around you was "buying the dip" every single month? ## What a moving average actually is

A 50-day moving average is the average price of the last 50 days — the average opinion
of the last 50 days' buyers and sellers. Price above it means the average participant is
profitable and calm; below it means the average participant is losing and anxious.

**The MA is a map of the crowd's mood and cost basis.** In 2008, price below the 200-day
meant the average buyer of the last year was underwater — every rally met their
breakeven selling. That's why the line held as resistance for 14 months.

- **SMA** weights all days equally; **EMA** weights recent days more. For today's uses
  they behave almost identically — pick EMA and be done.

## The 20 / 50 / 200 framework

| MA | Timeframe | What it represents |
|---|---|---|
| 20 EMA | ~1 month of trading days | The swing trader's line — momentum pulse |
| 50 EMA | ~1 quarter | The intermediate trend |
| 200 EMA/SMA | ~1 year | The big trend — bull/bear divide |

These numbers work *because everyone uses them* — self-fulfilling tools are the best kind.

## Use 1 — The trend filter (the 2008 lesson)

> **Only take longs when price is above the 200 MA on your trading timeframe.
> Only take shorts when below it.**

This one filter eliminates the majority of beginner losses, because most losses come
from fighting the main trend. You will be amazed how much of "skill" is just refusing to
fight the 200.

## Use 2 — Dynamic support/resistance

In clean trends, price respects the MA instead of horizontal levels:

- Strong uptrends repeatedly bounce **off the 20 EMA**.
- Each touch-and-hold confirms the trend; the day the bounce fails is an early warning —
  in 2008, the failed retests *of the 200-day from below* were the tells.

## Use 3 — Pullback entries

The institutional bread-and-butter:
1. Price above rising 50/200 → uptrend confirmed.
2. Pullback to the 20 EMA stalls.
3. Enter on the first bullish sign at the MA (engulfing, pin bar — yesterday's patterns),
   stop below the pullback low.

You bought the average cost of a month of traders in an established uptrend, with a
level right under your stop. Simple, boring, profitable — the anti-2008.

## Crossovers: understand the lag

A "golden cross" (50 above 200) sounds magical and arrives *months* late — the 2009
golden cross confirmed a recovery that was already +40% off the bottom. Treat crossovers
as **regime confirmations**, not entry triggers: they tell you the tide has turned, not
where to buy today. Entries still come from levels and pullbacks.

> ⚠️ **Common beginner mistakes**
> 1. Five MAs on one chart — each extra line is another excuse to overtrade.
> 2. Shorting every touch of the 20 EMA in an uptrend "because it's overbought."
> 3. Trading MA signals on a timeframe you don't actually trade.

## 🇮🇳 The Indian story: Nifty obeyed the 200-DMA for 17 months

The 200-day filter works in India exactly as it did in America — twice in living memory.
**2008:** Nifty broke below its 200-DMA in January 2008 near 6,200 and stayed below it
for nearly 17 months, through the −60% crash — every single rally back to the line was
sold, all the way to October 2008's lows. Investors with the one filter sidestepped the
worst period in Indian market history. **Contrast 2020:** Nifty crashed below the
200-DMA in March 2020 too — but reclaimed it within months, which is exactly the regime
difference Day 13 warns about. The filter doesn't predict; it tells you which movie
you're in. India has shown both movies.

## Key takeaways
- MA = the crowd's average cost basis and mood (2008: a year of resistance, one filter avoided).
- Three uses: filter (200), dynamic level (20/50), pullback entry.
- Crossovers confirm regimes — they're too slow for entries (2009 proved it).
