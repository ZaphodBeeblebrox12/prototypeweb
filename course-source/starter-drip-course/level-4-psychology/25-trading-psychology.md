# Day 25 — Trading Psychology

**🎬 How one hidden loss became €4.9 billion — the anatomy of a psychological spiral**

In 2005, a trader at a big French bank named Jérôme Kerviel took a position that went against him. It was small — the kind of loss every trader takes weekly. But Kerviel couldn't press the button that made it real. So he hid it, and did the thing his instincts demanded: he took *another*, bigger trade to win the money back. It lost too. He hid that one as well, entering fake covering trades so the bank's computers showed he had no risk.

For three years the spiral compounded — each hidden loss demanding a bigger trade to recover the previous ones, each "one last recovery trade" digging the hole deeper. By January 2008, the bank's risk team finally saw the truth: **€49.9 billion** of hidden positions, and closing them cost the bank **€4.9 billion** — then the
largest trading loss in banking history. Kerviel wasn't a gambler at heart; colleagues
described him as quiet, hardworking, from a modest background, terrified of failure.

His first mistake wasn't financial. It was psychological: a small loss he couldn't
emotionally afford to realize. Everything after was arithmetic. Today you learn the
five patterns that turned a routine loss into a national scandal — because the same
patterns, at your scale, are exactly how ordinary accounts die.

> 💬 **You don't have to write it down:** Kerviel's spiral started with a loss too small to matter. Honest question — what's the smallest loss you've ever quietly turned into a big one, not by math, but by behavior? Averaging? Refusing to sell? "One more trade to fix it"? Just recognize yours silently. Naming it is today's entire lesson.

## What you'll learn
- The five money-destroying mental patterns (with their triggers)
- Process-over-outcome thinking
- The daily operating rules of psychologically stable traders

## Trap 1 — Loss aversion (the root of almost everything)

Losses hurt roughly **twice as much** as equivalent gains feel good (proven repeatedly
in behavioral economics — Kahneman's prospect theory). In trading, this single quirk
produces nearly every other trap:

- You hold losers hoping (avoiding the pain of realizing the loss) while cutting winners
  early (banking the pleasure now). **Disposition effect.** Result: small wins, huge
  losses — the exact reverse of the asymmetry Day 21 proved you need.

**Antidote:** your stop and targets are decided *before* emotion enters (Days 17–18).
The plan is written when you're calm; it's executed when you're not. Kerviel's tragedy
is that he had no plan — only a feeling that had to be made whole.

## Trap 2 — Revenge trading

Loss → anger → "the market owes me" → immediate re-entry, bigger → bigger loss. The
second trade has no setup; it has an emotion. Kerviel's three-year spiral was revenge
trading at institutional scale — each trade existing only to undo the last one.

**Antidote:** the Day 19 circuit breaker is the hard version. The soft version: **after
any loss, 10 minutes away from the screen, mandatory.** Not to calm down — to make the
next decision deliberate. Ten minutes is the cheapest trade filter ever invented. Kerviel
never took ten minutes in three years.

## Trap 3 — FOMO (fear of missing out)

A vertical move you're not in feels like money being stolen from you. You chase it at
the worst price, with no plan, and become someone else's exit liquidity.

**Antidote:** reframing. A missed move costs nothing — P&L is unchanged by trades you
didn't take. A *chased* move has negative expectancy by definition. The market runs
every single day; there is always another bus. FOMO is the illusion that this one was
the last.

## Trap 4 — Overconfidence after wins

Three wins in a row and the brain starts narrating genius. Size creeps up, checklist
boxes get skipped ("I can feel this one"), and the account gives back the month in one
trade. Wins are the *most* dangerous moment in trading — losses make you careful.

**Antidote:** size never changes based on recent results — the formula outputs the size,
period. The checklist applies identically to trade #1 and trade #30 of the streak. If
the sentence "I'm on a heater" appears in your mind — that's the signal to *halve* size,
not double it. (Kerviel's early "wins" while hiding losses likely fed his escalation.)

## Trap 5 — Anchoring to your entry price

"I bought at 500, it's at 480, I just need it to come back to 500 and I'm out even."
The market has never heard your entry price. The only question that matters: *would I
open this position at today's price, with today's information?* If no — exit per plan,
whether you're up or down.

## The professional's operating system

1. **Process over outcome.** Judge each trade by checklist adherence, not profit. A
   checklist-perfect loss is a good trade; a reckless win is a bad trade. Over 100
   trades, process and outcome converge — and only process is controllable.
2. **Fixed routine:** same prep, same checklist, same review time. Routine is willpower
   saved for when it matters.
3. **State check before the first trade:** slept? calm? no personal crisis? Any "no" →
   half size or no trades. Your brain is position #1.
4. **The journal as therapist (Day 27):** feelings logged become patterns you catch next
   week instead of next year. (Kerviel's managers might have caught it in month one.)

> ⚠️ **Common beginner mistakes**
> 1. Believing psychology is a "later" topic — it's the multiplier on everything else.
>    Perfect system × 0 psychology = 0. (Kerviel had world-class tools.)
> 2. Treating a calm mood as a green light. Mood ≠ state; routine > motivation.
> 3. White-knuckling through tilt. Tilt is physiological (cortisol, heart rate). No pep
>    talk fixes it; only distance and time do.

## 🇮🇳 The Indian story: the Tuesday that ate the salary

You know this trader — he's in every Indian office. Friday: two quick wins on expiry
trades, +₹8,000. The group chat calls him a legend. Monday: full position size on a
"confirmed" BankNifty call, -₹6,000. He doesn't feel the loss — he feels the insult. He
re-enters within four minutes, bigger, on the 2:45 move. -₹9,000. Now he's down a month's
SIP contribution and the market "owes him." Final trade of the day: an OTM weekly buy
sized to win it all back by 3:15. It expires worthless. Tuesday morning, he doesn't open
the terminal; he can't. Multiply this Tuesday by a few million and you get SEBI's
₹1.8-lakh-crore number — Kerviel's spiral, retold in ₹6,000 installments. India's brokers
responded with the kill switch: a feature to lock yourself out of your own account.
Think about that. The industry built a product whose entire purpose is defending you
from the person in this lesson.

## Key takeaways
- Loss aversion → disposition effect → inverted asymmetry. Pre-written plans are the
  only known antidote. (€4.9B started as one small, un-realized loss.)
- Revenge: 10-minute rule + circuit breaker. FOMO: missed moves are free. Wins: danger
  zone — size never follows emotion.
- Judge yourself on process adherence. Outcomes follow over a large enough sample.
