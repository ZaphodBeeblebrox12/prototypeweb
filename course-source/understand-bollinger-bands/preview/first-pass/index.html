Keep the methods distinct

Not every setup asks the same question.

The author-defined method supplies the setup logic. A trader still has to decide how to execute it, where the idea fails, and whether the remaining reward justifies the risk.

Two different problems

Expansion, continuation or reversal?

Method I uses a squeeze as preparation for a volatility breakout. Method II combines %B and MFI to confirm strength or weakness. Method III looks for price/indicator disagreement. W-bottoms and M-tops depend on structure and confirmation. Carter's TTM Squeeze defines compression differently by comparing Bollinger Bands with Keltner Channels.

!
Source rules are not a guarantee

A method can define a repeatable setup without proving that every trigger is worth taking. Stop placement, position sizing, costs and room to the next obstacle still matter.

METHOD I

Volatility breakout

Look for very low BandWidth, then wait for price to break beyond the relevant band.

METHOD II

%B + MFI

Use price position and a volume-based money-flow measure to look for aligned strength or weakness.

METHOD III

Reversal clues

Compare a price extreme with the behaviour of a volume indicator, then wait for price confirmation.

METHOD IV

Confirmed breakout

Require two consecutive closes outside the same band; ADX helps interpret whether the market is trending.

Start with the trading question

What do you know—and what are you assuming?

A candle gives you a record of price over a chosen period. Volume gives you a record of activity according to the data source you are using. Neither, by itself, proves why participants acted or what price must do next.

The useful habit is to keep three things separate: the price result, the activity recorded, and the interpretation you are testing. Once those are distinct, you can compare similar-looking bars without treating a single one as a signal.

✓
By the end, you can

Describe a candle's price outcome and associated activity separately, then combine them without treating volume as a stand-alone signal.

OBSERVE

What is visible?

The bar's range, close location, direction and activity relative to a stated baseline.

INTERPRET

What might it mean?

A possible reading in context. Keep at least one plausible alternative open.

TEST

What would change your view?

Identify the next observable evidence that would strengthen or weaken the interpretation.

Read the chart before naming the pattern.

Use the chart as an example, not a prediction. Compare price progress, relative activity and where the move occurs in the sequence before deciding which explanation is better supported.

Chart examples / Lesson 1.1

Read the price result alongside activity.

Use the same questions each time: What did price accomplish? How unusual was recorded activity relative to this chart's baseline? What else in the sequence might change your interpretation?

A careful model reading

“Recorded activity is unusually high compared with nearby bars. The price result and its location in the sequence need to be assessed together. This could reflect a meaningful change in participation, but the bar alone does not establish direction. I would compare the response over the next bars and keep an alternative scenario open.”

Before you reveal the explanation

Record what you observe first. Then compare it with the model reasoning and note what evidence would change your view.
