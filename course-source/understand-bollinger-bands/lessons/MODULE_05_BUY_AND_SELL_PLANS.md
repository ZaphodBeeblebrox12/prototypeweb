# Module 5 — Build Buy and Sell Plans

So far, you have learned what the bands measure, how to read the market context, and how to assess common patterns. This module turns that reading into a trading plan. The plans here are **course-designed frameworks inspired by Bollinger's principles**. They are not presented as a set of exact rules prescribed by Bollinger, and they are not guaranteed to work.

By the end, you should be able to write a long or short plan with context, trigger, confirmation, invalidation, stop logic, exit management, position risk, and conditions that make you skip the trade.

## Lesson 5.1 — From Chart Reading to a Trade Plan

**Estimated time:** 15 minutes — read 7, inspect the figure 3, task 3, review 2.  
**Figure:** Figure 5.1 — compare a complete plan with one that identifies an entry but leaves risk and exit undefined.
![Figure 5.1: compare a complete plan with one that identifies an entry but leaves risk and exit undefined](../assets/student-assets/svg/bb-m05-f01.svg)

### The question

What must be decided before an interesting chart becomes a planned trade?

A chart can look promising and still offer no clear trade. To move from reading to planning, you need to turn the idea into a set of conditions that another trader could understand and test.

Use this sequence:

**Context:** What kind of market is this? Trend, range, possible reversal, or quiet period with a potential breakout? If the context is unclear, that may already be a reason to wait.

**Trigger:** What exact event must happen before you consider entry? Avoid phrases such as “when it looks strong.” Use something observable, such as a completed bar closing above a defined range boundary.

**Confirmation:** What additional evidence would support the idea? Confirmation should add information, not repeat the trigger in a different form. You do not need several indicators to agree before every trade.

**Invalidation:** What would show that the idea no longer fits? This should be based on the structure or condition behind your thesis, not a number selected after the trade begins losing.

**Stop and position size:** Once you know the invalidation area, determine the stop logic and the size that fits your allowed loss. The stop distance affects the position size.

**Exit and management:** Decide before entry how you will take profit, manage the position, or exit if the premise weakens. A plan can include more than one exit rule, but each must be clear.

**No-trade conditions:** State what would make you skip the setup: missing trigger, unclear context, conflicting evidence, too little room to target, poor liquidity, or another condition that makes the risk unacceptable.

### What is the difference between a trigger and confirmation?

The trigger is the event that activates your plan. Confirmation is supporting evidence you have chosen in advance. For example, a close above a range boundary may be the trigger, while the next bar holding above that boundary may be a confirmation rule if your method requires it. Do not keep adding confirmation after the fact until a chart finally looks convincing.

### Task

Fill the missing fields in the incomplete plan shown in Figure 5.1. If the figure does not provide enough information to choose an entry or stop, say what must be defined first.

**Model reasoning:** The student should not invent a stop from the band alone. They should identify the structure the trade depends on, define invalidation relative to that structure, then calculate size from the allowed cash risk and stop distance. ### Try a new case

A squeeze appears, but price remains inside its recent range and your plan has no boundary trigger yet. What do you do?

**Model response:** Keep the idea on a watchlist. The squeeze is context, not the entry. I will wait for the defined trigger or cancel the idea if the setup no longer fits.

### Common mistake

Choosing an entry first and deciding how to control risk only after price moves.

### Keep this

> **A setup becomes a plan only when you know what must happen, what would prove the idea wrong, and how you will control the risk.**

**Source note:** The structure of the plan is a TradeThesis teaching framework. It draws on Bollinger's distinction between identifying a setup and providing continuous advice, but the template is not a verbatim Bollinger method.

## Lesson 5.2 — Plan a Squeeze Breakout in Either Direction

**Estimated time:** 18 minutes — read 8, inspect the figure 4, task 4, review 2.  
**Figure:** Figure 5.2 — mirrored long and short examples, showing compression, a price boundary, trigger, invalidation and a planned exit area.
![Figure 5.2: mirrored long and short examples, showing compression, a price boundary, trigger, invalidation and a planned exit area](../assets/student-assets/svg/bb-m05-f02.svg)

### The question

How can you prepare for a move without guessing which way price will break?

A squeeze is a quiet-volatility condition. It does not select a direction. To build a trade plan, combine the volatility context with a price level that matters and a rule for what counts as a breakout.

The following is a **course-defined framework**. Its purpose is to turn the idea into something clear enough to test, not to claim that every trader should use these exact conditions.

### Long plan: an upward break

**1. Context.** BandWidth is unusually low compared with the instrument's recent history, and price has formed a clear consolidation area. Identify the upper boundary of that area before the break. A low BandWidth reading without a useful price boundary is not enough.

**2. Trigger.** Require a completed candle to close above the chosen boundary. A wick poking above and closing back inside does not meet this example rule. The boundary should be visible from the chart rather than drawn after the breakout.

**3. Confirmation.** Choose one useful rule before reviewing the outcome. For example, require the next candle to hold above the boundary, or require a clearly defined momentum measure to support the move. Neither confirmation is compulsory for every strategy; what matters is that the chosen method is specific and tested. Avoid adding three related indicators that all measure similar momentum.

**4. Invalidation and stop.** Decide what would show the breakout thesis has failed. One possible course rule is a return into the prior range that violates the intended hold condition. Place the stop where that failed premise is represented by price structure, allowing for the instrument's normal movement. Do not place it at the band simply because the band is visible.

**5. Exit and management.** Choose an exit method before entry. It may use the next meaningful price area, a measured range projection, a trailing structure rule, or another clearly defined method. The course does not claim one target is universally best. If the nearest realistic target leaves too little reward compared with the stop distance, skip the trade.

### Short plan: the mirrored downside break

The short plan mirrors the long:

- **Context:** narrow BandWidth and a clear consolidation with a defined lower boundary.
- **Trigger:** a completed candle closes below that boundary.
- **Confirmation:** apply the preselected hold or independent-confirmation rule in the opposite direction.
- **Invalidation:** define the price behaviour that would put price back into the range and contradict the plan.
- **Stop:** place it around the structure that invalidates the idea, then calculate a position size that fits the allowed loss.
- **Exit:** define a target or management rule and skip if the available room does not justify the risk.

The aim of symmetry is not to force both sides to be traded. It is to avoid making the long rules strict and the short rules vague, or vice versa.

### What if price closes outside the Bollinger Band?

An outside-band close is relevant to Bollinger's method, but it is not automatically an entry. The chosen range-boundary trigger, market context, confirmation, stop and target still matter. Price can continue outside the band, or the move can fail. Do not confuse “initially treat an outside close as continuation rather than automatic reversal” with “trade every outside close.”

### Inspect Figure 5.2

Study the long and short panels. Find the compression area, selected boundary, trigger candle, point where the idea fails, and planned exit area. If one panel lacks a clear boundary or offers little room to the target, it may not qualify as a trade.

### Task

Write the long and short conditions using the same fields. Then identify which case you would reject if price breaks the boundary but immediately returns to the prior range.

**Model reasoning:** Each side needs the same kind of evidence in its own direction. A quick return inside the range weakens or invalidates the continuation case if that condition was defined in advance. It does not automatically create a reverse trade; the new direction needs its own trigger and risk plan.

### Common mistake

Treating every squeeze release or every close outside a band as permission to enter.

### Keep this

> **The squeeze highlights a condition. The price boundary creates a testable trigger, and the plan controls the risk.**

**Source note:** Bollinger discusses BandWidth and the Squeeze, as well as volatility-breakout methods, in *Bollinger on Bollinger Bands*. This lesson's boundary, confirmation, stop and exit rules are course-designed and require testing. [Official rules](https://www.bollingerbands.com/bollinger-band-rules).

## Lesson 5.3 — Plan a Range Trade Without Fighting a Trend

**Estimated time:** 18 minutes — read 8, inspect the figure 4, task 4, review 2.  
**Figure:** Figure 5.3 — a range that continues to hold its boundaries beside a range that begins to break into a trend.
![Figure 5.3: a range that continues to hold its boundaries beside a range that begins to break into a trend](../assets/student-assets/svg/bb-m05-f03.svg)

### The question

When is a move toward an outer band a possible range opportunity rather than a trend signal?

A range trade starts with the range, not with the band. You first need evidence that price has been rotating between reasonably clear boundaries. Only then does it make sense to ask whether the upper or lower edge is producing a potential rejection.

A range can break at any time. If you treat every band touch as a reversal, you may keep fading a boundary after the market has changed character.

### The long framework: buying near a range low

**Context:** Price has repeatedly turned or stalled in a lower area and has returned toward the upper part of the range on earlier attempts. The market is not showing a clear sequence of lower swing highs and lower swing lows that would make a downward trend the more useful description.

**Trigger:** Price tests the lower boundary and then shows the specific rejection or return-inside behaviour defined in your method. For this course example, the learner must wait for a completed candle to close back inside the range after testing below or near the lower boundary. A band touch alone is not enough.

**Confirmation:** Check that the range structure remains intact and that the closing candle has not simply paused before another push lower. You may use a single independent piece of evidence, but do not require multiple indicators that all repeat the same information.

**Invalidation and stop:** If price breaks and sustains beyond the range low, the range-fade idea may be wrong. Define the stop around the structure that invalidates the plan, not at an arbitrary fixed distance from the band.

**Exit:** The middle of the range can be one testable first objective; the opposite boundary is a more ambitious objective. Which is appropriate depends on the stop distance, structure, and your tested method. If the available distance is too small, skip the trade.

### The short framework: selling near a range high

Mirror the logic. Price has repeatedly failed to hold above the range high; it tests that upper area and then closes back inside under your chosen rule. The invalidation condition is a sustained break above the boundary. The target may be the middle of the range or lower boundary if the planned reward justifies the risk.

The symmetry prevents a common mistake: being cautious about longs but allowing vague, late shorts—or vice versa.

### How Bollinger Bands fit

The outer bands help describe relative price position. They may help you notice when price is near an edge of the recent banded range, but they do not define the trading range for you. Use the visible highs and lows of price to establish the range. Then use the bands as supporting context.

### Inspect Figure 5.3

The first panel shows a range where previous boundaries have mattered. The second shows price moving outside a boundary and beginning to make directional progress. Compare the swing structure and what happens after the boundary test.

### Task

Which panel supports a range-fade plan under the lesson's rules? For the other panel, write the condition that would make you stop treating it as a range.

**Model reasoning:** The first panel can qualify if the boundaries and return-inside trigger are clear. The second should not be faded merely because it touches an outer band; continued progress outside the range can invalidate the premise. If the distinction is uncertain, wait for more evidence.

### Try a new case

Price reaches the lower band in a market with falling swing highs and lows and expanding bands. Is the range-long framework appropriate?

**Model response:** Not on the evidence given. The market may be trending down, so the range premise is not established. A lower-band touch alone is not a reason to buy.

### Common mistake

Building a range trade around the bands without first proving that price is ranging.

### Keep this

> **Range strategies need a range. The band can help you read the location, but the price structure must support the premise.**

**Source note:** The context distinction and warning against band-touch signals follow Bollinger's official Rules 6 and 7. Entry, stop and target rules in this lesson are course-designed examples, not a universal Bollinger setup.

## Lesson 5.4 — Plan a Possible W-Bottom or M-Top Reversal

**Estimated time:** 18 minutes — read 8, inspect the figure 4, task 4, review 2.  
**Figure:** Figure 5.4 — a candidate W-bottom and a candidate M-top annotated with trigger, invalidation, stop reference and target area.
![Figure 5.4: a candidate W-bottom and a candidate M-top annotated with trigger, invalidation, stop reference and target area](../assets/student-assets/svg/bb-m05-f04.svg)

### The question

What must happen before a reversal pattern becomes a trade plan?

A W-bottom or M-top gives you a way to study the relationship between two lows or highs and the bands. It does not guarantee that the market has turned. To use the pattern as a trade idea, you need a trigger, a point where the idea is wrong, and a risk plan.

### A possible W-bottom long

Start with the two lows and their relationship to the lower band. Is the second low showing a different relative position from the first? Is the shape complete enough to study, or is another low still developing?

For this course's example, use a break above the intervening swing high as the trigger. This is a **course-defined confirmation rule**, chosen because it can be identified and tested consistently. Do not say that Bollinger prescribed this exact entry for every W-bottom unless you have verified that specific claim from the text.

Set invalidation where the structure behind the long case fails. If price falls and breaks the second-low area under the plan's rule, the long thesis may no longer fit. The stop should reflect that invalidation area, with allowance for the instrument and trading method. Calculate position size from the stop distance and allowed cash risk.

Before entry, decide how to exit. The next resistance area may limit the opportunity. A target based on structure or a tested rule is preferable to assuming price must reach the upper band.

### A possible M-top short

Mirror the process. Study the two highs and their relationship to the upper band. For this course's example, require price to break below the intervening swing low before considering a short. Define what would invalidate the short, place the stop around that structure, and assess the room to the next support area or chosen target.

The symmetry is important, but the market does not owe you equal opportunities in both directions. If only one pattern qualifies, only one plan may be relevant.

### What the bands do not replace

A well-formed candidate may still fail. Price might break the intervening high and then fall back, or break the intervening low and then recover. A trigger makes the rule specific; it does not eliminate risk.

A pattern can also be technically valid but still offer poor reward relative to risk. If the target is close and the invalidation point is far away, you can reject the trade even after the trigger occurs.

### Task

Use Figure 5.4 to write a long plan for the W-bottom and a short plan for the M-top. For each, mark context, trigger, invalidation, stop reference, target area, and one reason to skip the trade.

**Model reasoning:** A valid answer states the conditions in advance and keeps the two directions symmetrical. It should not claim that a band touch or pattern shape is enough. A missing trigger, unclear structure or inadequate room to target is a defensible reason to stand aside.

### Try a new case

A W-bottom meets the example trigger, but price is already close to a major resistance zone. The stop must sit below a distant swing low. What is the decision?

**Model response:** The trigger exists, but the trade may be unattractive because the target is close relative to the risk. I can skip it rather than reduce the stop distance artificially.

### Common mistake

Assuming a recognisable reversal pattern has to be traded.

### Keep this

> **A pattern suggests a possibility. The trigger makes it testable; invalidation and risk determine whether the trade is acceptable.**

**Source note:** John Bollinger's W-type bottom and M-type top discussions are in Chapters 12 and 13 of *Bollinger on Bollinger Bands*. The trigger, stop and exit template used here is a course framework unless an exact author rule is specifically verified.

## Lesson 5.5 — Which Plan Fits This Chart—or None?

**Estimated time:** 14 minutes — read 6, inspect the figure 3, task 3, review 2.  
**Figure:** Figure 5.5 — a trend continuation candidate, a range candidate, a reversal candidate, and an unclear/no-trade case.
![Figure 5.5: a trend continuation candidate, a range candidate, a reversal candidate, and an unclear/no-trade case](../assets/student-assets/svg/bb-m05-f05.svg)

### The question

Does every chart with Bollinger Bands offer a trade?

No. The purpose of the course is not to find a reason to trade every chart. It is to match a plan to the evidence. If none of the studied contexts and triggers is present, no trade is a valid decision.

### Match the plan to the condition

Figure 5.5 contains four different situations.

**Trend continuation candidate:** Price is making progress in one direction and interacting with one band repeatedly. The range-fade framework is not appropriate simply because price is near an outer band. You may consider a trend-related plan only if its own trigger, invalidation and risk rules are met.

**Range candidate:** Price is rotating between established boundaries. A range-fade setup may be worth reviewing near an edge if the defined rejection trigger appears and the range premise remains intact.

**Reversal candidate:** A possible W-bottom or M-top is forming. Do not treat the shape itself as confirmation. Wait for the event required by your chosen pattern plan.

**Unclear case:** The swing structure is mixed, the boundaries are not clear, or the possible target is too close relative to the stop. The best decision may be to do nothing.

### A simple decision sequence

Before choosing a setup, ask:

1. What market condition do I see?
2. Which framework actually matches that condition?
3. Has its trigger occurred?
4. Do I know where the idea is wrong?
5. Is there enough room for the planned trade to make sense?
6. Is there any condition that means I should stand aside?

If you cannot answer these questions, you do not need to invent an answer. You can wait until the evidence improves or move to another chart.

### Task

For each panel in Figure 5.5, choose **long framework**, **short framework**, or **no trade**. Give one visible reason and one condition that is still missing or would cancel the idea.

**Model reasoning:** A sound answer links the chosen framework to the context shown. If a trend is present, do not use a range-fade just because a band is touched. If the pattern lacks a trigger, wait. If the chart is unclear or the risk is unattractive, no trade is reasonable. More than one answer may be defensible if it follows the evidence and states the remaining uncertainty.

### Try a new case

The chart is trending upward, price is well above the middle band, and the bands are widening. The move has already travelled far, and the next price barrier is close. What is the best response?

**Model response:** The trend may be strong, but the entry is late and the available reward may be limited. I should not short only because price is near the upper band, nor chase the move automatically. Waiting for a better location or skipping the trade is reasonable.

### Common mistake

Forcing a chart into the setup that feels most familiar.

### Keep this

> **A good trader does not need a trade on every chart. The framework must fit the context, and the risk must fit the opportunity.**

**Source note:** This lesson combines the previous course frameworks and Bollinger's Official Rule 22: bands help identify setups rather than provide continuous advice. [Official rules](https://www.bollingerbands.com/bollinger-band-rules).
