## Lesson 3.1 — Stop Chasing Price: Finding Potential Setups in an Established Trend

### Learning objective
Describe an established trend, identify a potential continuation area, and explain what price-volume evidence would support or weaken a setup before entry.

### Outline and timing
- **0:00–1:00 — The cost of chasing.**
- **1:00–3:00 — Define trend structure through swings, not indicator labels alone.**
- **3:00–5:00 — Impulse, pause and continuation conditions.**
- **5:00–7:30 — Volume during advances and pullbacks.**
- **7:30–10:30 — ChartLab: mark a setup area without entering early.**
- **10:30–11:00 — Recap.**

### Full narration script
A market begins to rise quickly. You notice a sequence of green candles and increasing volume. The move looks convincing, and the natural impulse is to buy immediately because you do not want to miss it. But by the time the move looks obvious, price may already be far from a sensible point of invalidation. Chasing makes it harder to define risk and easier to enter just as the market pauses.

The alternative is to study the structure that the move leaves behind. An established uptrend is commonly described through a sequence of higher swing highs and higher swing lows; a downtrend shows lower swing highs and lower swing lows. These descriptions are not perfectly mechanical, because swing selection depends on timeframe and method, but they provide a common reference. The purpose is not to label every chart bullish or bearish. It is to understand whether the recent sequence is producing directional progress or remains balanced.

In a trend, price often alternates between an impulse in the dominant direction and a pause or pullback. The impulse shows the market making progress. The pause provides a chance to assess whether the countertrend move is challenging the structure or merely interrupting it. Volume can inform the reading, but it cannot identify the future outcome on its own. A pullback with smaller ranges and lower relative volume may be consistent with reduced countertrend activity. A pullback with expanding downside ranges and strong volume may warrant more caution. Either can continue further than expected.

Let's build a conditional scenario. Suppose price has broken above a well-observed range high and then advanced. Instead of buying the tallest green candle, mark the former range boundary and the latest meaningful swing low. Ask whether price can hold the breakout area on a pullback. During the retest, observe range, close location and relative volume. If selling produces little downside progress and price begins to reclaim the local swing structure, that sequence may support a continuation hypothesis. The exact trigger must be defined by the learner's method before acting; this course is teaching the reasoning, not prescribing one universal entry signal.

Now challenge your initial idea. What would weaken it? A decisive return inside the old range, follow-through lower, or a break below the swing low that supported the trend would all matter. Do not move the definition of invalidation merely because you still want the trade. A hypothesis needs a boundary at which the evidence is no longer consistent with it. If price violates that boundary, the correct response may simply be to abandon the setup and wait for a new structure.

It helps to separate the setup area from the entry trigger. A setup area is a place where your thesis may be worth evaluating—perhaps a prior breakout level, a pullback zone or a former swing. A trigger is the observable behaviour you require before taking action. Without that distinction, traders often enter just because price has reached a level. But a level is not an instruction. Price must still demonstrate the behaviour the plan calls for.

Volume analysis can prevent chasing in another way. After a strong impulse, exceptionally high volume with little further progress may be a sign to slow down and investigate rather than assume the trend is accelerating. It might be continuation-related activity, opposing interest, or a temporary pause. The important point is that the result no longer matches the simple story “more volume means more upside.” You may decide that the trade is too extended or that more evidence is required.

Here is a practical pre-entry checklist. First, is there a recognisable trend structure? Second, where was the last meaningful impulse and where is the current pullback relative to it? Third, is the potential setup near a level that matters, or is it in the middle of nowhere? Fourth, what does volume say about the activity and result of the pullback? Fifth, what observable condition would make the scenario invalid? Sixth, is there enough room to a potential target to justify the risk? If you cannot answer these questions, waiting is a valid outcome.

The skill is not finding a way to enter every trend. It is learning to wait for a location where the thesis and the risk can both be described clearly. You will miss some moves. That is unavoidable. A repeatable process is designed to avoid low-quality decisions, not to participate in every price fluctuation.

### Extended spoken case analysis — additional narration

The most important moment in this lesson is when the student does not enter. On the screen, show the steep impulse and draw the distance from the late breakout candle to the structural invalidation. The stop distance is large because the market has already travelled far. Even if the uptrend remains intact, the trade may have poor geometry. Then rewind to the pullback near a previous breakout zone. Here there is a reason to watch: the price location provides a clear question and a nearby structural reference. Explain that an area of interest is not yet an entry. A trigger might be a recovery through a local swing; a different method might require a retest or bar close. Whatever the method, specify it before the next candle. Compare the two cases using the same risk worksheet. Ask which one gives the student a clearer thesis and where the idea becomes invalid. The answer should be about the quality of the plan, not whether one chart later rose more. The student is learning to wait for conditions that can be described and managed, not to chase whichever chart currently looks strongest.

### Knowledge check
1. What is the difference between a setup area and an entry trigger?
2. Which pullback behaviours might support or weaken a trend-continuation hypothesis?
3. Why can a technically valid trend still offer a poor entry after an extended move?

## Lesson 3.2 — Is the Pullback Running Out of Steam? Reading Countertrend Moves

### Learning objective
Compare a pullback's range, progress, relative volume and structural effect with the preceding impulse to judge whether the continuation case remains plausible.

### Outline and timing
- **0:00–1:00 — Pullbacks are information, not automatic bargains.**
- **1:00–3:00 — Compare the pullback with the impulse.**
- **3:00–5:00 — Progress, range and volume.**
- **5:00–7:30 — Failure cases and structural invalidation.**
- **7:30–10:30 — ChartLab: compare two pullbacks.**
- **10:30–11:00 — Recap.**

### Full narration script
A pullback is a movement against a recent directional move. Traders often want to buy every pullback in an uptrend or sell every rally in a downtrend. That misses the central question: is the countertrend move simply pausing the trend, or is it changing the market's structure? Volume and price analysis can help you compare the counter-move with the impulse that came before it.

Start with the impulse. How far did price travel? How long did it take? Were the bars broad, and did they retain their progress? Was activity elevated relative to comparable bars? Record a few objective features rather than relying on the vague description “strong trend.” Then study the pullback in the same units. Does it retrace a small portion of the impulse over several narrow bars, or does it erase the entire move in a few wide bars? Is volume subdued or expanding? Where are the closes located within the bars?

A pullback with narrow ranges, limited downside progress and relatively low activity can be consistent with a weak countertrend response. In an uptrend, that may be a reason to continue watching for the prior upward direction to resume. But the interpretation is conditional. Quiet activity may reflect an inactive session or simply a temporary lack of participation. Low volume does not guarantee that sellers are finished. The level and the next price response remain essential.

Now consider a different pullback. Price declines in wide bars, closes near the lows, activity expands, and the move breaks through a previous higher low. The counter-move is not merely a pause in the original sequence; it is damaging the structure that supported the uptrend. A continuation thesis should be reassessed. It may still recover, but the evidence is no longer the same. You should not keep calling every lower price a “buy-the-dip opportunity” simply because the larger chart once trended higher.

Location helps. If the pullback returns to a prior breakout area, that level becomes a natural place to examine the response. If price holds near the level and downward pushes lose progress, that can support a possible resumption scenario. If price passes straight through the level and then fails to reclaim it, the previous breakout may have failed. Support and resistance should be treated as zones of interest, not magical lines that must hold.

Volume spread analysis places attention on relationships among a bar's range, close and volume. The value for this course is not memorising a codebook of named bars. It is learning to ask: what amount of activity occurred, what price movement resulted, and was that outcome consistent with the surrounding structure? For example, an increase in volume during a pullback is not automatically bearish; it matters whether the decline makes meaningful progress and whether subsequent bars continue lower. Likewise, low-volume selling is not automatically harmless; if price steadily erodes the level, the structural result matters.

A practical comparison is to study two pullbacks that begin after similar upward impulses. Pullback A declines slowly, stays above the prior swing low, has diminishing range, and then price breaks a nearby lower high. Pullback B begins similarly but accelerates, closes near lows, breaks the swing low and cannot recover. Only after seeing the full sequences do we compare what could have been known earlier and when the evidence changed. Avoid hindsight by freezing the chart at each bar and writing the current hypothesis at that moment.

For your own plan, define which structural feature matters. Some methods use the prior swing low; others use a volatility-adjusted or time-based boundary. There is no single invalidation rule for all traders. What matters is that the boundary is chosen before the outcome and is relevant to the thesis. If the pullback exceeds the boundary, do not reclassify it after the fact just to preserve the trade idea.

The real question is not “How far has price pulled back?” It is “What is this pullback doing to the structure and the evidence behind the prior move?” If the countertrend move is making little progress and failing to damage the structure, a continuation scenario may remain plausible. If it is making sustained progress against the trend, you should not ignore that evidence. Both readings must remain open until price resolves the question.

### Extended spoken case analysis — additional narration

A useful pullback comparison must account for both distance and time. A decline that retraces half the impulse over two bars may have a different character from a shallow decline that drifts for ten bars. Neither is automatically better. Add ranges and volume to the comparison. If the impulse made broad upward progress on elevated activity and the pullback consists of smaller bars with subdued activity, a continuation hypothesis may remain plausible. If the pullback starts printing wider downside bars, closes near their lows, and breaks the prior higher low, the counter-move is changing the structure. Do not allow the phrase “buy the dip” to protect the original thesis from evidence. Make students freeze both charts after three pullback bars and write what they currently know, not what the whole chart will later reveal. When more bars appear, they can update the read. This is a useful way to teach real-time reasoning and reduce hindsight bias. The lesson is not to find the most elegant pullback after the event; it is to identify what would need to happen next before committing to a decision.

### Knowledge check
1. What is a useful way to compare a pullback with the impulse that preceded it?
2. Why isn't lower volume during a pullback enough to guarantee continuation?
3. Which structural event might challenge an uptrend-continuation thesis?

## Lesson 3.3 — Trading Near Support and Resistance: What to Look for Before Entry

### Learning objective
Treat support and resistance as zones where a question can be tested, and develop a volume-informed checklist for evaluating the market's response there.

### Outline and timing
- **0:00–1:00 — Levels are areas of interest, not commands.**
- **1:00–3:00 — Select meaningful zones from prior structure.**
- **3:00–5:00 — Read approach, test and response.**
- **5:00–7:30 — Volume and follow-through around the zone.**
- **7:30–10:30 — ChartLab: test a level.**
- **10:30–11:00 — Recap.**

### Full narration script
Support and resistance are often drawn as lines, but market behaviour rarely respects a line with mathematical precision. A level is better treated as a zone where price previously found acceptance, stalled, reversed, or changed direction. That history makes the area worth watching. It does not guarantee that price will reverse there next time.

Start with a clean chart. Mark a zone that is visible from meaningful swing points or a prior trading range. Avoid drawing so many lines that every candle touches one. A useful level has an explanation: this is where price previously failed to advance, where a breakout occurred, or where a range repeatedly found its boundary. Keep the chart simple enough that another person can understand why the zone matters.

Next, study how price approaches it. Is price moving toward resistance in a sequence of strong, broad advances? Is it grinding up with smaller results? Is price falling toward support in a fast decline, or drifting down with limited progress? The approach matters because it establishes the context for the test. An important area reached after a forceful move presents a different question from the same area reached after a slow rotation.

At the zone, observe the bar range, closing position and relative activity. Price may test the area and reject it, pass through it, or trade back and forth without resolution. A high-volume bar that cannot make progress beyond resistance raises a question about opposing activity or temporary balance. A close above resistance on meaningful range followed by acceptance beyond the zone supports a different hypothesis. Neither reading is complete until you look at subsequent behaviour.

Use the language of testing rather than prophecy. “Price is approaching a support zone; I will watch whether downward progress slows and whether price can reclaim the local structure” is more useful than “support will hold.” It defines what you want to see and leaves room for the market to behave differently. If price breaks through support and then fails to reclaim it, the support-hold idea has lost support. You do not need to turn immediately bearish; you simply need to stop relying on the broken premise.

The same applies to resistance. If price trades above resistance for a moment but closes back below the zone and then makes a lower high, the failed-breakout interpretation becomes more plausible. If price closes beyond the zone, holds it on a retest and then extends higher, a breakout-and-acceptance scenario becomes more plausible. The retest is not mandatory in every trading method, and a price can move away without offering one. Here we study the evidence process, not a universal entry recipe.

Volume's role is to qualify the event. Elevated activity during a decisive move through a zone may show that the event occurred amid meaningful participation on the feed. Elevated activity with little progress may flag conflict. Low activity can mean the move occurred in a quiet environment; it does not automatically make the level stronger or weaker. Compare the activity with the instrument's normal behaviour and note whether the market sustains its position after leaving the zone.

A zone should also be evaluated relative to the intended trade. Where is the next opposing area? How far away is invalidation? Does the potential reward justify the risk? A trader can correctly identify a level and still choose not to trade it because the entry is too close to the next resistance or the invalidation distance is too large. Analysis is not complete at the moment the level is identified; it must lead to a decision about whether a trade is reasonably structured.

For the exercise, mark support and resistance before revealing the next sequence. At each zone, write the test condition, the evidence that would suggest acceptance, the evidence that would suggest rejection, and the invalidation for each scenario. Then reveal the chart's development. A correct process does not require predicting the outcome. It requires defining alternatives clearly enough to respond without improvising.

### Extended spoken case analysis — additional narration

Treat support and resistance as zones that help organise a decision, not as precise prediction lines. When price reaches a zone, the analyst's first job is to examine the approach and response. Did price arrive in a fast impulse or a slow drift? Did the test move through the area and close beyond it, or did price return? What did volume do relative to a comparable baseline? If price rejects the zone, what later evidence would show that the rejection matters? If price accepts beyond it, what would demonstrate that the earlier barrier no longer contains price? These questions keep the learner from entering simply because price touched a line. Another useful point is that a level can be relevant but no longer tradable. If the next opposing zone is very close, a theoretically valid setup may offer little room. If the invalidation point is far away, the risk budget may dictate a much smaller position or no trade. Ask learners to include the next opposing level in every exercise. The quality of a decision depends on the whole nearby structure, not only the level at which a candle happens to react.

### Knowledge check
1. Why is a support/resistance zone not a guaranteed reversal point?
2. What distinguishes rejection from acceptance in the following bars?
3. How can a correct level still lead to a poor trade decision?

## Lesson 3.4 — Finding Opportunities at the Edges of a Trading Range

### Learning objective
Recognise a balanced range, explain why its edges present different questions from its middle, and use price-volume evidence to monitor rejection or breakout scenarios.

### Outline and timing
- **0:00–1:00 — Range trading versus trend chasing.**
- **1:00–3:00 — Define the range and its boundaries.**
- **3:00–5:00 — Why edges matter more than the middle.**
- **5:00–7:30 — Read tests, failed moves and volume.**
- **7:30–10:30 — ChartLab: classify range behaviour.**
- **10:30–11:00 — Recap.**

### Full narration script
A trading range is a period in which price rotates between an upper and lower area without making sustained directional progress beyond them. It may represent a temporary balance before a trend continues, a pause in a larger move, or a more meaningful transition. The appearance of a range alone does not tell you which it is. Your first task is to identify its boundaries and describe the behaviour inside them.

Choose an upper zone where several attempts to advance have stalled and a lower zone where several declines have found a response. The zones need not be perfectly horizontal or identical in width. They need to describe the structure well enough to guide observation. If you redraw the boundaries after every new candle, the analysis becomes impossible to test. Mark them based on the information available at the time and revise them only when there is a clear structural reason.

Why focus on the edges? Near the middle of a range, price is often far from either boundary and the relationship between potential reward and invalidation can be poor. At an edge, the market is testing a place where its prior rotation changed direction. If price reaches the upper edge and stalls, a rejection hypothesis may be worth examining. If it pushes through and holds beyond that edge, the market may be leaving balance. Those are different scenarios with different risk points.

Volume can help distinguish the character of the test, but there are no fixed volume rules that settle every range. Repeated high-volume attempts to move above the upper boundary with little progress may indicate substantial activity without acceptance. If those attempts are followed by a return into the range and later downside progress, the rejection hypothesis gains evidence. Conversely, a wide-range close above the upper edge, followed by holding behaviour, supports a breakout hypothesis. A breakout may occur on average or even muted volume in some conditions; the subsequent price result matters.

Wyckoff's work gives special attention to the behaviour inside trading ranges and to tests, springs and upthrusts around their boundaries. For beginners, the key concept is not to memorise the names before understanding the structure. A spring is broadly a move below a range boundary that returns back inside; an upthrust is a move above the upper boundary that falls back inside. Those are events to inspect, not automatic reversal instructions. The strength of the interpretation depends on how price arrived there, how it closed, and what happened afterward.

A range can also resolve in the direction of the preceding trend. For instance, an uptrend may pause in a range, repeatedly hold higher lows, and then break upward. The same-looking range after a long decline may develop differently. The larger structure changes the prior probabilities you consider, but it does not guarantee the outcome. A useful chart note records the larger trend, the range's width, the repeated tests, and the evidence for either rejection or acceptance.

Plan a range trade in conditional language. If testing the lower edge, what behaviour would suggest the level is holding? What would show that the range is breaking down? If price tests the upper edge, what would support rejection and what would support an upside breakout? Where would the thesis be invalidated? If price is in the centre, is there a compelling location at all? “No trade in the middle” is not an inflexible law, but it can be a useful filter when the setup depends on range boundaries.

Range analysis also requires patience. Price can overshoot boundaries because markets are dynamic and levels are not exact. It can make several attempts before resolving, or it can produce a false breakout and later break out for real. The goal is not to guess which edge will hold. It is to understand that the edge is where new information becomes most relevant, then assess the response.

### Extended spoken case analysis — additional narration

Range edges matter because they are places where prior price behaviour repeatedly changed, but they are not guaranteed turning points. Use a range that has at least two clear tests at each side and show how the market behaves near the middle versus at the edge. At the edge, there is a defined question: will price be rejected back into the range, or will it be accepted beyond the boundary? At the middle, price may have room to move in either direction but often lacks the same clean reference for invalidation. This does not mean every middle-of-range trade is wrong; it means that a setup based on range-edge behaviour may no longer apply there. Show an upper-edge false break that returns into the range, then show price later breaking out for real. The first failure did not guarantee that price would remain inside the range indefinitely. Ask learners to define a no-trade condition: for example, when price is too close to the middle for the chosen range-edge plan, or when the boundaries are not clear. The point is to match the method to the structure actually present.

### Knowledge check
1. Why might the middle of a range offer less attractive conditions than an edge?
2. What is the defining price behaviour of a spring or upthrust?
3. What evidence would help distinguish rejection from acceptance beyond a boundary?

## Lesson 3.5 — Before You Enter: Separating Promising Setups From Weak Ones

### Learning objective
Use a consistent checklist to reject poorly located or poorly defined setups and explain why not trading is a legitimate analytical decision.

### Outline and timing
- **0:00–1:00 — A setup is a hypothesis, not a command.**
- **1:00–3:00 — Context and location.**
- **3:00–5:00 — Evidence, trigger and invalidation.**
- **5:00–7:30 — Reward, risk and conflicting signals.**
- **7:30–10:30 — ChartLab: grade three candidate setups.**
- **10:30–11:00 — Recap.**

### Full narration script
After learning to recognise trends, pullbacks and range edges, it is tempting to start seeing trade setups everywhere. The chart offers endless movement, and many patterns look obvious once the outcome is known. A useful process must therefore do more than find candidates. It must also reject the ones that are poorly located, unclear, contradicted by evidence, or difficult to risk sensibly.

Use five filters. The first is context: can you describe whether the market is trending, ranging or transitioning? You do not need absolute certainty about the regime, but you should know what evidence you are using. A pullback setup in an established trend has a different logic from a fade trade in the middle of a range. If you cannot describe the market's state, the setup's rationale may be weak.

The second filter is location. Is the setup near a meaningful swing, prior range edge, support/resistance zone or volume-profile area relevant to the thesis? Or is it simply a candle that looks attractive after several bars have already moved? A good-looking pattern in a poor location may not offer a clear invalidation point or useful potential reward. Location does not make a trade valid, but it helps define the question.

The third filter is evidence. What is price doing relative to the level? How does the candle range compare with recent bars? Is activity unusually high or low against a sensible baseline? Does the sequence show progress, stalling, rejection or acceptance? A setup should have a reason beyond the fact that an indicator or candle pattern appeared. Write down the observations that support it and the observations that challenge it.

The fourth filter is a trigger and invalidation. A setup area tells you where to watch; a trigger tells you what must happen before acting. Invalidation tells you what would make the thesis no longer defensible. If both are vague, the trader may enter on emotion and move the stop after the market disagrees. The exact trigger can vary by strategy, but it must be observable and defined in advance.

The fifth filter is risk and opportunity. Where is the next opposing level? How far away is the proposed invalidation? Is the potential target large enough to justify that risk under your plan? You do not need every trade to have a large reward-to-risk ratio, and a headline ratio cannot compensate for a weak setup. But the geometry matters. If the trade is being entered directly below a major resistance zone with a distant stop, the idea may be analytically interesting but poorly constructed.

Now consider three hypothetical candidates. Candidate A is a pullback in an established uptrend. It reaches a former breakout zone, selling progress slows, and price reclaims a nearby swing high. The invalidation is visible beneath the zone, and the next opposing area leaves room for the planned target. Candidate B is a green candle in the middle of a broad range, with no clear level, no defined trigger and resistance just overhead. Candidate C is a breakout above a range with elevated volume, but price has already moved a long distance and the logical invalidation lies far below. Candidate A may deserve further work; B may not justify an entry; C requires a careful assessment of whether chasing makes the risk unacceptable. This is not a ranking of guaranteed outcomes, only an example of structured screening.

Use a simple grading rubric: context clear; location justified; price-volume evidence described; alternative scenario considered; trigger and invalidation explicit; risk acceptable under plan. Mark each as yes, no, or unknown. Unknown is not the same as yes. If key evidence is missing, wait. A setup can be incomplete because the market has not yet shown the behaviour you need.

Do not confuse selectivity with perfectionism. No checklist removes uncertainty. The point is to avoid taking trades whose rationale is difficult to explain or whose risk is not controlled. A good process will sometimes lead to a losing trade, and an impulsive process will sometimes make money. Judge the quality of the decision from the information available at the time, not solely from the outcome.

Your most valuable skill may be learning to write: “This is not ready yet.” The market does not owe you a trade. If the level, evidence, trigger or risk is unclear, preserving capital and attention can be the better decision. A potential setup becomes a trade plan only when the conditions are defined well enough to manage uncertainty.

### Extended spoken case analysis — additional narration

A setup-quality checklist should protect the learner from inventing reasons after falling in love with a chart. Have them score each candidate with the same questions and allow an “unknown” answer. Unknown should not be silently counted as yes. For example, the chart might have a clear trend and a pullback zone, but no trigger has appeared. The setup is worth monitoring, not entering. Another chart may have a good-looking breakout but poor room before the next resistance; its pattern may be valid while the trade economics are not. A third may have a strong apparent signal but unreliable volume data; the learner should lower confidence in the volume-specific part of the argument. The exercise needs a debrief on process versus result. A rejected candidate may later make a large move. That does not mean rejecting it was irrational if the predefined conditions were absent. Conversely, an impulsive entry may win by chance. Ask learners to justify decisions using what was visible at the time. Over time, this produces a reviewable process, rather than a collection of anecdotes about charts that would have worked.

### Knowledge check
1. Name the five setup filters and explain why each matters.
2. Why is a valid chart pattern not automatically a good trade?
3. What should you do when a required condition is still unknown?

## Module 3 instructor notes

- Keep “setup area,” “trigger,” “invalidation” and “trade plan” distinct in every lesson.
- Prefer zones over false precision; require learners to explain why a level matters.
- Present trend and range patterns in both successful and failed outcomes.
- Reference themes: Weis on chart-by-chart ambiguity and support/resistance; Wyckoff on trading-range behaviour and tests; Coulling and Williams on placing volume in context.
