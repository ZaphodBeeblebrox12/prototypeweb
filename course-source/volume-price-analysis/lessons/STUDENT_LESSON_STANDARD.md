# Student-First Lesson Standard
## Read Volume. Find Trade Setups.

**Purpose:** Define the format for the six-module, 30-lesson course. Module manuscripts are student-facing teaching material, not presenter scripts. A student should be able to work through each lesson independently without being asked to read narration, production cues or asset instructions.

## The core rule

Write to teach the student, not to simulate a teacher speaking aloud. The teacher's voice is expressed through clear explanations, thoughtful sequencing, practical examples, anticipation of likely confusion and useful feedback. Do not write “what the narrator says,” “now show the chart,” or other video-production directions in the student lesson.

## Standard lesson structure

Use this as a flexible pattern, not a rigid form to fill mechanically:

1. **Lesson title and outcome:** Say what the learner will understand or be able to do.
2. **A question worth answering:** Begin with a concrete chart situation, decision or common mistake.
3. **The idea in plain English:** Explain one main concept at a time. Define specialist terms before relying on them.
4. **Read the evidence:** Use a small chart, data table or described example. Separate visible facts from possible interpretations.
5. **Worked example:** Show the reasoning step by step, including an alternative explanation and what future evidence would change the reading.
6. **Practise with support:** Give a task, useful hints and feedback that explains why.
7. **Try a fresh case:** Reduce the hints. Ask the learner to apply the idea to a new situation and justify the judgement.
8. **Common mistake:** Correct the most likely misunderstanding without repeating the whole lesson.
9. **Takeaway and knowledge check:** Provide a short reusable checklist or decision rule, then test understanding with explained answers.

Combine or omit a section when it does not help that particular lesson. Do not repeat the same explanation just to satisfy a template.

## Requirements for every lesson

- Anchor the teaching in visible price behaviour and the relevant volume data.
- Explain why the concept matters and when it is useful.
- Give a worked example with enough detail to follow the reasoning without an instructor.
- Include at least one meaningful learner task and explanatory feedback. A task specification alone is not a completed exercise.
- Include a counterexample or plausible alternative where interpretations are uncertain.
- Explain what would strengthen or weaken an interpretation; do not present patterns as guaranteed signals.
- Keep the figure visually clean: use its module/lesson figure number, not a repeated synthetic-data banner. State in the surrounding lesson or source/alt-text notes that illustrative examples are synthetic rather than historical data.
- Define the data source, timeframe, session and comparison baseline wherever they change the interpretation.
- Use compact headings, short paragraphs, comparison tables and readable charts where they improve understanding. Do not turn every sentence into a bullet.
- Preserve useful analytical depth. Remove repetition, not nuance.
- Keep time targets as planning estimates only. Actual student learning time must be checked in the rendered lesson; do not treat word count as proof of duration.

## Keep internal production material elsewhere

Do not put these into the student-facing lesson body:
- Full narration scripts or “extended spoken case analysis”.
- Timestamped video outlines or presenter instructions.
- Asset generation prompts, ChartLab build specifications or internal production status.
- Editorial rationale and research provenance notes intended for the production team.

Those belong in the asset brief, editorial guide, research notes and production checklist.

## Voice and epistemic discipline

Use a calm, patient, precise teacher's voice. Explain everyday meaning before specialist terminology. Never assume that an observed candle identifies a participant or proves intent. The preferred reasoning pattern is:

1. **Observation:** What does the chart/data show?
2. **Interpretation:** What might that evidence mean?
3. **Alternative:** What else could explain it?
4. **Discriminator:** What future behaviour would help distinguish the explanations?
5. **Decision:** Is there enough evidence to act, or is waiting/no trade more sensible?

Do not make “bullish”, “bearish”, “absorption”, “rejection”, “acceptance”, “spring” or “upthrust” a substitute for explaining the evidence.

## Practice progression

Use **Notice → Compare → Apply** as an internal design scaffold for increasing learner responsibility. Do not expose those as numbered levels or internal difficulty labels in student-facing headings. Prefer natural task headings such as “Look closely at the evidence”, “Compare the cases” and “Try a new case”.

The underlying progression is: identify observable facts; compare similar-looking cases or distinguish evidence from interpretation; then work through an unfamiliar case, retain uncertainty where appropriate and state what evidence would change the view. Difficulty should increase through reasoning, not jargon or chart clutter.

## Review gate

A rewritten lesson is not finished merely because its prose reads cleanly. Review it for factual clarity, a complete worked example, meaningful practice, feedback quality, readable visual needs, terminology consistency and a sensible connection to adjacent lessons. Timed learner testing, accessibility and final visual QA remain separate release checks.
