# Read Volume. Find Trade Setups.
## Learner Q&A Bank — Second Pass: Value, Application and Feedback

**Purpose:** Help a beginner turn lesson ideas into chart-reading skills. This is a learner resource and teaching/feedback bank—not a list of questions included merely to satisfy a course checklist.

**Who this is for:** Someone who is new to charts or has only recently started using price and volume together.

## The value question

Before retaining any question, ask:

> **If a learner answers this correctly, what can they now do on a chart that they could not do before?**

If the answer is only “remember a definition,” strengthen the question or add a practical follow-through task. Each lesson below contains two questions, model answers, and a short application exercise. A learner should be able to explain the reasoning, point to visible evidence, and identify what they still do not know.

## How to use this bank

- **Try first:** Give yourself a moment to answer before reading the model answer.
- **Use the chart:** Point to the candle, volume bar, range or price area that supports your answer. Do not rely on memory alone.
- **Separate fact from interpretation:** First state what is visible. Then say what it may mean. Finally, say what evidence would strengthen or weaken that reading.
- **Accept well-reasoned alternatives:** An uncertain chart may support more than one possible explanation. The goal is not to guess the future; it is to make a defensible reading and know what to watch next.

**Assessment rule:** Reward clear observation, context, a plausible alternative and an observable next condition. Do not give full credit to an unsupported directional guess merely because it matched the later price move.

# Module 1 — Read the Market Behind the Candles

## Lesson 1.1 — What Candlesticks Don't Tell You: Reading the Volume Behind Price

**What this should help you do:** Explain why similar candles can carry different information depending on where they appear and what activity accompanied them.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Two green candles have almost identical bodies. One appears after a pullback near a previously tested area; the other appears after a long advance near an earlier high. What can you conclude?**

**Model answer:** The candle shape alone does not tell us whether the two situations are alike. The first may be part of a recovery from a pullback; the second may be a test of an area where price previously struggled. Compare the volume bars, where each candle sits in the structure, how each closes, and what price does next. Both readings remain provisional until more evidence appears.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. What should you write down before explaining what a candle means?**

**Model answer:** Record the location, the candle's high-to-low range and close, volume compared with a suitable group of recent bars, and the price structure immediately before it. Then write one possible explanation and one alternative. This order makes it harder to confuse something you can see with something you are guessing.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Find two similar-looking candles in different chart locations. For each one, write one observation, one possible interpretation, and one thing you would watch next.

**A strong response includes:** A clear distinction between candle appearance and chart context; no claim that the candle predicts the next move.

## Lesson 1.2 — Is This Move Significant? Spotting Unusual Volume

**What this should help you do:** Decide whether a volume bar stands out against a relevant comparison, rather than judging by its height alone.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. A volume bar is three times the size of the last few bars, but it occurs during the market's opening period when volume is usually high. Is it exceptional?**

**Model answer:** Not necessarily. It stands out against the bars immediately before it, but that comparison may be misleading if the opening period normally has more activity. Compare it with similar bars from the same time of day or session, and state which baseline you used. The word “unusual” is meaningful only after you explain what you compared it with.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. A volume bar is unusually large. What should you check before you give it a bullish or bearish meaning?**

**Model answer:** Check what price did during the bar, where it appeared, how close price finished to the high or low, and whether the next bars kept or gave back the move. Confirm that the volume feed and session are comparable too. A spike tells you that reported activity was high; it does not, by itself, tell you the direction or outcome.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Choose a possible volume spike. Note your comparison bars, explain why they are a fair baseline, and describe the price result without using “bullish” or “bearish.”

**A strong response includes:** A stated baseline, a description of price progress, and a reminder that the spike alone is not an entry signal.

## Lesson 1.3 — Read the Story Inside a Candle: Wicks, Range and Closing Price

**What this should help you do:** Describe what happened within a candle without treating a wick or wide range as a prediction.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Price trades above a previous high, leaves a long upper wick and closes back below that high. What does the candle show—and what does it not prove?**

**Model answer:** Price traded higher during the period but did not keep those higher prices through the close. At that moment, the attempt to hold above the earlier high has not succeeded. The candle does not prove that price must fall: later bars could recover above the level and stay there. The follow-through is part of the evidence.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. A wide candle forms on high volume but closes near the middle of its range. Should you call it a strong directional candle?**

**Model answer:** Not from range and volume alone. Price travelled a long distance and the feed recorded substantial activity, but the close in the middle means the candle did not retain all its progress in either direction. Describe that mixed result, then inspect its location and subsequent bars before choosing an interpretation.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** On three candles, mark the high, low, open, close and close location. For each, finish the sentence: “The candle shows ___; it does not yet prove ___.”

**A strong response includes:** Accurate labels and a separation between the observed candle result and a forecast.

## Lesson 1.4 — When Price Moves on Low Volume: Opportunity or Warning Sign?

**What this should help you do:** Interpret low activity in context instead of treating it as an automatic continuation signal.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Price pulls back on lower volume after an advance and pauses near a previous breakout area. Is the uptrend safe to trade?**

**Model answer:** The pullback may be a pause with less reported activity, but that is only one possible reading. Check whether the area holds, whether the pullback keeps making downward progress, and whether price later shows the behaviour your entry plan requires. Until the trigger appears, “worth watching” is more accurate than “safe to buy.”

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. What new evidence would challenge the idea that the pullback is only a pause?**

**Model answer:** A clear move through the level that was supposed to hold, followed by continued progress lower or an unsuccessful attempt to reclaim the level, would weaken the continuation idea. Low volume does not cancel that price evidence. The lesson is to define in advance what would make your first reading less credible.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Compare a quiet pullback that holds a level with one that breaks it. Write down the first visible difference that matters and the condition that would make you stop favouring continuation.

**A strong response includes:** The level being tested, the actual price behaviour and an observable condition that would challenge the initial idea.

## Lesson 1.5 — Which Volume Can You Trust? Understanding Data Across Different Markets

**What this should help you do:** Know what the volume number represents before using it as evidence.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Your retail spot-FX chart shows fewer volume units than another platform for the same pair and time. Which reading is correct?**

**Model answer:** The numbers may measure different things or come from different feeds. A retail FX chart often reports the count of price updates from that provider, not all trades across the decentralised global FX market. Check each provider's definition before comparing values; do not assume the higher number represents more total market trading.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. What should you record before comparing volume bars across instruments or platforms?**

**Model answer:** Record the market and instrument, data provider, what its volume field measures, timeframe/session, and the bars or period used as a baseline. For crypto, note the specific venue; for exchange-traded instruments, confirm the contract and session. If you cannot verify what is counted, state that limitation rather than making a stronger claim than the data supports.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Make a small “volume data card” for one instrument: source, definition, timeframe, baseline and one limitation.

**A strong response includes:** The source and definition of the volume measure, and a recognition that different feeds may not be directly comparable.

# Module 2 — Spot Buying and Selling Pressure

## Lesson 2.1 — Who's Pushing Price? Reading Buying and Selling Pressure

**What this should help you do:** Use buying and selling pressure as a description of price progress, not as a claim that you know who placed the orders.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. A series of candles makes higher highs and closes near their highs. How can you describe the evidence without claiming to know who is buying?**

**Model answer:** Price is making upward progress and is retaining much of that progress through the closes. If volume is also elevated relative to a fair baseline, that adds information about recorded activity. This is consistent with buying pressure in the course's descriptive sense, but the chart does not identify the people trading or their motives.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. Why is a sequence usually more informative than one candle?**

**Model answer:** One candle may be a brief event that is reversed immediately. A sequence shows whether progress is continuing, stalling or changing. Looking at several bars also helps distinguish an early warning from a meaningful change in price structure.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Label a five-bar sequence as upward progress, downward progress, limited progress or inconclusive. Point to at least two bars that support your label.

**A strong response includes:** Evidence from the sequence and language that describes the price result without inventing a hidden story.

## Lesson 2.2 — When Volume Supports a Move: Recognising Evidence of Strength

**What this should help you do:** Check whether an initial move beyond a level is being sustained.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Price breaks above a range on elevated volume and closes outside it. What additional evidence would make the breakout more convincing?**

**Model answer:** Price remaining outside the range, holding the boundary on a retest, or continuing to make upward progress would support the continuation reading. Elevated volume adds context to the first move, but cannot substitute for what happens afterward. Decide in advance what “holding” means for your timeframe instead of choosing the rule after seeing the result.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. A breakout happens on ordinary volume and then price stays above the level. Is it automatically weak?**

**Model answer:** No. Activity must be judged against the instrument, session and baseline. A sustained move on ordinary activity may still be meaningful, while a high-volume move can fail. Describe the price behaviour and use volume as supporting context, not as a universal pass/fail threshold.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Review two breakouts. For each, record the boundary, close location, volume comparison and first three to five bars afterward. Decide whether the evidence supports continuation, challenges it or remains unclear.

**A strong response includes:** A clear reference level and post-breakout behaviour, not a conclusion based only on a volume spike.

## Lesson 2.3 — Heavy Volume, Little Progress: What Is Happening Behind the Move?

**What this should help you do:** Recognise the difference between high activity and actual price progress.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Price reaches resistance on very high volume but barely moves higher. What is the most careful first description?**

**Model answer:** The market recorded high activity, but price made little upward progress at that location. Buyers and sellers may be meeting each other, an event may be creating two-sided trade, or a data/session issue may affect the comparison. Absorption is one possible explanation, not something a single candle proves.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. What would help you assess whether the area is rejecting price?**

**Model answer:** Look for price to move back below the area, fail to regain it, and then make further progress lower. If price instead returns above the area and stays there, the rejection idea weakens. The next price response is more useful than trying to name unseen participants.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Find a high-volume candle with little price progress. Write three columns: “what I see,” “possible explanations,” and “what I need to see next.”

**A strong response includes:** The effort-versus-result mismatch, more than one plausible explanation and a condition that could weaken the preferred explanation.

## Lesson 2.4 — Is the Move Losing Steam? Reading Signs of Weakening Pressure

**What this should help you do:** Distinguish a warning that momentum is changing from evidence that the price structure has reversed.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Each new push makes less progress, and closes move farther from the highs. Does that mean a downtrend is beginning?**

**Model answer:** It suggests the advance is struggling to retain the same quality of progress, but it does not establish a downtrend. Price may pause before continuing. Look for a break of a meaningful swing low and whether price then fails to return to the previous pattern before treating the reversal idea as stronger.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. What is the practical difference between an early warning and a trade trigger?**

**Model answer:** A warning tells you to pay attention and update the possible scenarios. A trigger is the observable condition your plan requires before you consider entering. Treating a warning as a trigger can lead you to trade before there is enough evidence.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Mark the first warning of a weakening advance, the first structural change and one condition that would invalidate your reversal idea.

**A strong response includes:** Three distinct stages—warning, structural evidence and an observable trigger or invalidation.

## Lesson 2.5 — When Price and Volume Disagree: Finding Clues Others May Overlook

**What this should help you do:** Turn a mismatch into testable possibilities rather than an automatic signal.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Price makes a marginal new high while volume is lower than on the previous high. What should your chart note say?**

**Model answer:** Record the exact mismatch: price made a higher high while the data source recorded less activity than during the earlier push. It may indicate weakening participation, or price may be moving easily through the area. The next question is whether price can hold the new high and continue, not whether the lower bar automatically means a fall.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. How do you make a useful decision when price and volume seem to disagree?**

**Model answer:** Write the observation, list at least two explanations, then identify which future price behaviour would favour each. If neither condition appears, the correct classification can remain “inconclusive.” This gives you a plan for gathering evidence instead of feeling forced to choose a direction.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Choose a mismatch and complete: “I observe ___. One explanation is ___. Another is ___. I would favour the first if ___. I would favour the second if ___.”

**A strong response includes:** A neutral observation, two reasonable explanations and clear conditions that help distinguish them.

# Module 3 — Find Trade Setups at the Levels That Matter

## Lesson 3.1 — Stop Chasing Price: Finding Potential Setups in an Established Trend

**What this should help you do:** Identify a place worth watching and keep it separate from the condition that would actually allow an entry.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. A market is trending upward and price has already travelled through a large green candle. How do you decide whether to chase it?**

**Model answer:** Do not use the size or colour of the candle alone. Check where price is relative to the recent structure and next opposing area, then ask where the trade idea would fail and whether a sensible risk remains. If price is far from a useful invalidation point or close to resistance, the setup may no longer offer acceptable trade geometry.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. What is the difference between a setup area and an entry trigger?**

**Model answer:** The setup area is a location worth watching because it has a reason to matter, such as a former range boundary or pullback zone. The trigger is the specific price behaviour you decided to wait for before considering the trade. Reaching the area is not the same as satisfying the trigger.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Mark one potential setup area on a trend chart. Then write a separate trigger and invalidation; do not enter just because price reaches the area.

**A strong response includes:** A reason for the area, a visible trigger and a separate point where the idea would no longer make sense.

## Lesson 3.2 — Is the Pullback Running Out of Steam? Reading Moves Against the Trend

**What this should help you do:** Compare a pullback with the move that came before it and decide what evidence is still needed.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. After a strong upward move, price pulls back in smaller candles with lower volume. What else do you need to check?**

**Model answer:** Compare the pullback's depth and speed with the prior advance, check whether meaningful swing structure remains intact, and watch the important area price is approaching. Lower volume can fit a quiet pullback, but it can also occur before the decline continues. The holding or breaking of structure and later price response matter.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. What would weaken the idea that the original trend is resuming?**

**Model answer:** Price breaking the level that was expected to hold, continuing to make progress against the original trend, or failing to reclaim the broken area would weaken the continuation idea. Decide which of those observations matters for your chosen timeframe before the event develops.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Compare two pullbacks after similar advances. For each, mark depth, speed, relative activity, the important level and the first observation that would support or weaken continuation.

**A strong response includes:** More than a low-volume label; it uses structure and explains what must happen next.

## Lesson 3.3 — Trading Near Support and Resistance: What to Look for Before Entry

**What this should help you do:** Mark useful zones and plan for more than one possible reaction.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Why draw a support or resistance zone instead of a single perfect price?**

**Model answer:** Price often turns, pauses or changes behaviour across an area rather than at the exact same number every time. A zone represents the part of the chart where previous reactions occurred. It is a reference for observation, not a guarantee that price will stop there again.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. Price reaches resistance. What are two reasonable scenarios to prepare for?**

**Model answer:** Price may fail to stay above the zone and move away, or it may move through the zone and sustain the new prices. The candles and subsequent bars help distinguish these cases. Write the trigger and invalidation for each scenario separately; the alternative should not automatically become a trade just because the first idea fails.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Draw one support zone and one resistance zone from previous price behaviour. For the zone nearest current price, write a rejection scenario, a sustained-break scenario and a no-trade condition.

**A strong response includes:** A justified zone, alternative outcomes and conditions that could be observed on the chart.

## Lesson 3.4 — Finding Opportunities at the Edges of a Trading Range

**What this should help you do:** Recognise the boundaries of a range and avoid treating every excursion as a confirmed breakout.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Price has moved between a similar upper area and lower area several times. What should you mark, and what should you avoid assuming?**

**Model answer:** Mark the upper and lower zones that contain the repeated reactions and note the time period over which the range is visible. Avoid assuming that every future visit must turn there, or that one move through a boundary confirms a lasting breakout. The next bars help show whether price returns inside or sustains the move outside.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. Price briefly trades above the range, then closes back inside. What is the next useful question?**

**Model answer:** Ask whether price can reclaim and hold above the boundary or whether attempts to return above it fail. A quick return inside is evidence that the first move did not hold; later behaviour tells you whether the failure is developing into a wider reversal or simply a temporary fluctuation.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Label five events on a range chart: upper rejection, lower rejection, ambiguous overshoot, sustained breakout and failed breakout. For each, state what evidence you would wait for next.

**A strong response includes:** The boundary, close/return behaviour and post-event evidence; not an automatic trade at the edge.

## Lesson 3.5 — Before You Enter: Separating Promising Setups From Weak Ones

**What this should help you do:** Use a consistent checklist to filter out attractive-looking but poorly structured ideas.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. What should you check before treating a chart pattern as a trade setup?**

**Model answer:** Check the broader price structure, why the location matters, what price and volume actually show, which alternative explanation is possible, the trigger, the point where the idea fails, and whether the next opposing area leaves enough room for the plan. A pattern is only one part of that decision. If an important answer is unknown, do not quietly treat it as a yes.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. A setup later moves in the direction you expected, but your trigger never appeared. Was skipping it a mistake?**

**Model answer:** Not necessarily. If the plan required a trigger and it did not occur, skipping the trade followed the rule. Review whether the rule was clear and sensible across examples; do not judge the decision only by what price did afterward.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Score three candidate charts as “consider,” “wait,” or “reject.” Beside each decision, list the strongest evidence, the missing information and the condition that would change your answer.

**A strong response includes:** Reasons tied to the checklist and a defensible no-trade or wait decision when information is missing.

# Module 4 — Spot False Breakouts Before You Chase Them

## Lesson 4.1 — Is the Breakout Real? What Volume Can Tell You

**What this should help you do:** Evaluate whether a move beyond a range is being sustained instead of deciding from the first candle alone.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. A candle closes above a range boundary on elevated volume. What can you say now, and what must you wait to learn?**

**Model answer:** You can say price closed beyond the marked boundary and the feed recorded elevated activity relative to the chosen baseline. That supports investigating an upside breakout, but does not confirm that the market will stay outside. Follow-through, a successful retest or a return inside the range will add important evidence.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. A breakout occurs on ordinary volume but holds above the range for several bars. How should you assess it?**

**Model answer:** Do not reject it solely because volume was ordinary. Check whether the comparison was appropriate and whether price can maintain its progress. The quality of the price response matters; volume is context rather than a universal threshold.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Freeze a chart after its first breakout candle. Write what is known, two possible outcomes and the specific behaviour that would support each before revealing later bars.

**A strong response includes:** A factual description of the breakout candle, an alternative scenario and a future condition that can be checked.

## Lesson 4.2 — Breakout or Trap? Reading the Signs of a Failed Move

**What this should help you do:** Identify failed price behaviour without claiming to know that someone deliberately trapped traders.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Price moves above the range, returns inside and then fails to reclaim the upper boundary. What makes the failure reading stronger?**

**Model answer:** The return inside shows that the first attempt did not stay outside; the failed reclaim shows that price could not regain the old boundary; further downside progress adds evidence that the move is weakening. Keep the steps separate. If price later reclaims and holds above the boundary, the failure reading should be reconsidered.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. Does the chart prove that someone created the move to trap other traders?**

**Model answer:** No. It shows a move beyond the boundary that did not hold. It does not prove anyone's intention. Describe the observable sequence and plan around the price conditions rather than attributing a hidden motive.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** On a staged chart, mark the first warning, the stronger failure evidence and the condition that would invalidate the failed-breakout reading.

**A strong response includes:** A sequence of observed events and a willingness to update if price reclaims the area.

## Lesson 4.3 — When Volume Explodes at Market Extremes: Understanding Climax Activity

**What this should help you do:** Treat a major activity spike late in a move as a reason to investigate, not a promise of reversal.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. After a long advance, one bar has much higher volume but price makes little additional progress. What is the careful interpretation?**

**Model answer:** A lot of activity was recorded after a substantial move, but price did not retain much additional progress in that bar. This can be consistent with a climactic event, especially if progress remains difficult afterward, but it does not prove a top. Check where the candle closed, what happens next, and whether structure changes.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. The next bars consolidate and price then continues higher. Does that make the original volume spike meaningless?**

**Model answer:** No. The spike was still an unusually active event; it simply did not develop into the reversal scenario. A good method allows that outcome. Review whether the original interpretation was appropriately cautious and whether the conditions you required for a reversal ever appeared.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Compare two high-volume events after long moves—one followed by a structure change, one by continuation. For each, separate the initial warning from the later evidence.

**A strong response includes:** A reason to investigate the event, a counterexample and no claim that a climax guarantees reversal.

## Lesson 4.4 — Springs and Upthrusts: Recognising Failed Moves at Range Boundaries

**What this should help you do:** Understand the pattern names while relying on visible behaviour to assess the event.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Price falls below a trading-range low and then returns inside. What makes this a possible spring rather than a confirmed reversal?**

**Model answer:** The excursion below the lower boundary followed by a return inside fits the basic description of a spring. The name labels the event; it does not guarantee that price will rise. Check whether price can remain inside, whether the boundary holds on a retest, and whether later structure supports the recovery.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. What is the corresponding idea for an upthrust, and what would weaken it?**

**Model answer:** An upthrust is a move above a range high that returns inside. It becomes a weaker failure interpretation if price reclaims the upper boundary and holds above it, or if the supposed range was not well defined in the first place. Define the range before naming the event.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Compare one apparent spring and one apparent upthrust with examples that fail. First mark the range boundaries, then the excursion and return, then the later evidence.

**A strong response includes:** A clearly defined range, a correct description of the excursion and return, and a condition that could disprove the preferred idea.

## Lesson 4.5 — Before Calling a Reversal: What to Look for After the Breakout

**What this should help you do:** Build a step-by-step case for or against a reversal without using hindsight.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Price stops making progress beyond a range boundary and returns to test it. Is that enough to declare a reversal?**

**Model answer:** It is a warning that the breakout may be struggling, not a complete reversal case. Stronger evidence comes from a meaningful structure break in the other direction and price failing to return to the old pattern. Keep the warning, confirmation and entry trigger as separate decisions.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. Why define in advance what would make your reversal idea wrong?**

**Model answer:** It prevents the plan from changing every time a new candle appears. Invalidation tells you which price behaviour no longer fits the idea you started with. If that condition occurs, reassess rather than moving the rules simply to preserve the trade thesis.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Create two staged outcomes from the same initial breakout warning: a reversal develops, or the original trend resumes. At each stage, note whether you have a warning, confirmation, trigger or invalidation.

**A strong response includes:** Correctly staged evidence and a pre-defined invalidation condition.

# Module 5 — Find the Price Levels Worth Watching

## Lesson 5.1 — Where Has the Market Traded Most? Introducing Volume Profile

**What this should help you do:** Explain how a profile differs from a time-based volume histogram and use it as historical context.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. The same synthetic market data is shown once with vertical volume bars and once with a horizontal Volume Profile. What is different?**

**Model answer:** The vertical histogram shows recorded activity from period to period, so it helps answer when activity occurred. Volume Profile groups recorded activity by price over a selected period, so it helps answer where activity was concentrated. Both depend on the data and settings; neither tells you where price must turn.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. Price approaches a broad area of the profile. Can you treat it as guaranteed support or resistance?**

**Model answer:** No. The area shows that relatively more activity was recorded there during the selected period. Price may pause, turn away or move through. Use current candle behaviour and nearby structure to decide which scenario is gaining support.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** On one chart, identify one broad and one thin profile area. Describe each area's past activity, then list two possible ways price might behave when it returns.

**A strong response includes:** A correct description of the profile, its chosen period and the limit that historical activity does not guarantee future behaviour.

## Lesson 5.2 — Finding the Market's Reference Point: Point of Control and Value Area

**What this should help you do:** Read profile reference points and recognise that they depend on the selected data window and platform method.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. What do the Point of Control and Value Area tell you in a selected profile?**

**Model answer:** The Point of Control is the price row with the most recorded activity in that profile. The Value Area is the range containing the selected share of profile activity, according to the platform's calculation. These are descriptions of the selected past period, not automatic entry or exit signals.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. Why might these reference points move when you change the profile's start and end times?**

**Model answer:** Changing the selected window changes which price activity is included, so a different row or range may contain the largest or chosen share of activity. Before comparing profiles, keep the settings and windows comparable or explain the difference. A changed level may reflect changed inputs as well as changed market behaviour.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Make two profiles from the same data with different windows. Mark POC and Value Area on each, then describe what changed and which conclusions remain uncertain.

**A strong response includes:** A correct definition, a stated profile window and an explanation of why settings matter.

## Lesson 5.3 — Where Price May Stall — or Move Quickly: High- and Low-Volume Nodes

**What this should help you do:** Use areas of concentrated or limited past activity to frame scenarios, not to forecast a guaranteed reaction.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. A profile has a high-volume area near current price. What can you say before price reaches it?**

**Model answer:** You can say that the selected period contains more recorded activity around that price area than nearby portions of the profile. It is worth watching, but that does not make it guaranteed support or resistance. The response as price arrives will provide new evidence.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. Price enters a low-volume area. Must it travel quickly through it?**

**Model answer:** No. Less recorded activity was associated with that area in the selected period, which may mean price can sometimes move through it quickly. But conditions may have changed, and price can pause or turn. Watch the current price path rather than treating the profile as a script for what happens next.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Mark one high-volume area and one low-volume area. For each, write a continuation possibility, a stall/rejection possibility and the price behaviour that would distinguish them.

**A strong response includes:** A careful description of the historic distribution and a testable scenario rather than certainty.

## Lesson 5.4 — When the Market's Focus Shifts: Reading Changes in Value

**What this should help you do:** Compare similar profile windows and distinguish movement in recorded activity from a conclusion about market direction.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Across several comparable sessions, the main concentration of profile activity shifts higher. What can you infer?**

**Model answer:** More of the recorded activity is taking place at higher prices in the chosen windows. That can be consistent with the market accepting higher prices, but the profile alone does not prove that the shift will continue. Check price structure, whether price holds outside earlier value, and whether the same pattern persists in comparable periods.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. Why is it misleading to compare a short intraday profile directly with a full-day profile without explaining the difference?**

**Model answer:** They include different spans of market activity, so their shapes and reference points may differ for that reason alone. Compare similar windows when you want to evaluate change over time. If windows must differ, state the limitation before explaining what the difference might mean.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Compare three equal-length synthetic sessions. Record POC movement, overlap between value areas and price's position relative to prior value. Only after those observations, write a possible interpretation.

**A strong response includes:** A comparable-window method and a clear distinction between observed migration and a hypothesis about acceptance.

## Lesson 5.5 — Putting It Together: Reading Volume Profile Alongside Price Action

**What this should help you do:** Combine profile context with current candle behaviour without turning the profile into a standalone signal.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Price approaches a profile area that mattered in the past. What should you read alongside the profile?**

**Model answer:** Read the current candles, the recent highs and lows, where price is relative to the area, and whether it holds beyond or returns through the area. The profile tells you where activity was concentrated in the selected period; current price behaviour helps show how the market responds now. Create at least two possible outcomes before choosing a preferred reading.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. Your planned reaction at a profile area does not appear and price moves cleanly through it. What should you do?**

**Model answer:** Update the analysis. The profile describes past activity; it does not force current price to respond in a particular way. Check whether your original trigger appeared, whether invalidation occurred and which new area or structure matters next. Do not reinterpret the plan after the fact as if the move through the level had always been expected.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** On a chart with one broad and one thin profile area, write two if/then scenarios around the nearest area. Reveal a pause, a rejection and a clean move-through as separate possible endings; update the reading for each.

**A strong response includes:** Profile context, current price evidence, alternative outcomes and willingness to revise the scenario.

# Module 6 — Put It All Together: Build a Complete Trading Plan

## Lesson 6.1 — Start With the Bigger Picture: What Is the Market Doing?

**What this should help you do:** Reduce chart clutter and identify the context relevant to a price-volume question.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Before interpreting the latest volume bar, what should you identify on the chart?**

**Model answer:** Identify whether price is trending, ranging or moving between those conditions; mark only a few meaningful swing points and price zones; and check the source and meaning of the volume data. This gives the bar context. Without it, the same volume event can be interpreted in several incompatible ways.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. Why can adding more lines and indicators make the analysis worse?**

**Model answer:** More markings can obscure the question being investigated and make it easier to find a reason for any outcome. Keep only the levels that have a clear basis in price structure or the selected profile. You should be able to explain why every line is there and what decision it helps you make.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Simplify an over-marked chart to a few meaningful levels, visible volume and any relevant profile references. Write a five-sentence context note including one uncertainty and two possible scenarios.

**A strong response includes:** A clear market description, justified levels, a known data source and a question the chart has not answered yet.

## Lesson 6.2 — Read the Evidence: What Are Price and Volume Telling You?

**What this should help you do:** Keep observations separate from the story you build around them, and update your reading when new evidence arrives.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. How would you describe a high-volume candle that closes near the middle of its range?**

**Model answer:** First record the facts: the volume relative to a stated baseline, the candle's range, its close location and where it formed on the chart. Then offer a possible reading and at least one alternative, such as two-sided trade or event-related activity. The chart alone does not prove a specific participant action.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. New bars appear to challenge your first interpretation. Should you change your notes?**

**Model answer:** Update your interpretation, but preserve what you originally observed. A sound process allows the hypothesis to change as new evidence arrives; it does not rewrite history to make the first guess look right. Keep the original note, add the new event and explain why the new evidence changed—or did not change—your view.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Record an initial reading, reveal two more bars, and write an update separately. Highlight the exact new observation that affected your interpretation.

**A strong response includes:** Factual initial notes, an explicit update based on new evidence and no hindsight rewriting.

## Lesson 6.3 — Build Your Trade Scenario: What Needs to Happen Next?

**What this should help you do:** Turn a chart reading into conditional possibilities with clear triggers and invalidation.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. What makes an if/then scenario more useful than “I think price will rise”?**

**Model answer:** An if/then scenario defines the conditions that would make the idea actionable. For example: “If price holds above the previous range high and then closes above the recent swing, I will assess a continuation setup; if it returns inside and cannot reclaim the boundary, I will drop that idea.” The conditions can be checked on the chart and do not depend on confidence alone.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. If your main scenario fails, should you automatically trade the opposite direction?**

**Model answer:** No. The first scenario becoming weaker does not automatically satisfy the opposite setup's trigger. The alternative needs its own supporting evidence, entry condition and invalidation. If neither scenario meets its rules, waiting is a complete decision.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** From an ambiguous breakout chart, write a continuation scenario, a failure scenario and a no-trade condition. Make each trigger and invalidation observable.

**A strong response includes:** Two distinct scenarios, separate triggers and a condition under which no trade is taken.

## Lesson 6.4 — Plan the Trade Before You Take It

**What this should help you do:** Connect chart invalidation to risk, sizing, target logic and the real costs of executing the trade.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. Why should you determine the point where the idea fails before calculating position size?**

**Model answer:** The distance between planned entry and invalidation helps determine how much can be lost per unit if the idea fails. Position size must then fit the risk limit and the instrument's contract or tick-value rules. Do not choose the size first and then move the invalidation point closer just to make the numbers look acceptable.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. A chart setup looks convincing, but the next opposing area is very close and spread/slippage is large. Is it still a good trade?**

**Model answer:** It may not be. The chart idea can be coherent while the actual trade has poor reward relative to risk or unacceptable execution costs. Estimate risk, potential target room and likely costs before entry; skip or redesign the setup if it no longer meets the plan. The course does not prescribe one risk percentage for everyone or every instrument.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Complete a trade ticket for a synthetic setup using the supplied contract/tick value. Then increase the spread or slippage and decide whether the plan still qualifies. Explain the decision.

**A strong response includes:** An invalidation-based risk calculation, target logic, costs and a willingness to cancel a technically attractive setup.

## Lesson 6.5 — The Complete Walkthrough: From Chart Analysis to Trade Plan

**What this should help you do:** Produce a complete, reviewable plan and judge the process separately from the eventual result.

**Level 1 — Notice and understand · Q1. What should a complete volume-based trade plan contain before the outcome is known?**

**Model answer:** It should record market context and data limitations, visible price-volume evidence, a main and alternative scenario, a trigger, invalidation, cancellation/no-trade conditions, risk and target logic, and management rules. Afterward, preserve that original plan so you can compare what you expected with what the market actually did.

**Level 2 — Compare and explain · Q2. Your plan correctly required a trigger that never appeared, but price later moves in your expected direction. Did your no-trade decision fail?**

**Model answer:** Not automatically. If the trigger never appeared, you followed the process you had defined. Review whether the condition was clear and whether it makes sense across a collection of examples. Do not judge the quality of a decision only by whether a hypothetical position would have made money.

**Level 3 — Apply on a new chart (practice):**** Complete the context note, evidence sheet, two scenarios, trigger, invalidation, risk ticket and post-event review on a staged capstone chart. Keep the original notes visible when the later outcome is revealed.

**A strong response includes:** A complete conditional plan, risk controls, an honest record of what was known at each stage and process-based review.

# Course-wide questions

## 1. Can volume tell me exactly who is buying or selling?

**Model answer:** No. It tells you about activity recorded by a specific data source. Price helps you describe the result of that activity, but the chart normally cannot identify every participant or explain their private reasons. Use careful wording: “price made upward progress on elevated recorded activity” is a stronger analysis than “institutions bought here” when the chart cannot establish that claim.

**Transfer practice — Apply (Level 3):**** Rewrite one confident participant story as an observation, a possible explanation and a condition that would change your view.

## 2. Is high volume always bullish and low volume always bearish?

**Model answer:** No. A high-volume bar can accompany progress, stalled price or a failed move. Low volume can occur in a pause, an ordinary quiet period or a move through an area with little activity. Ask what price did, where it happened and what the next bars confirmed.

**Transfer practice — Apply (Level 3):**** Find one high-volume bar with progress and one with little progress. Describe the difference without labelling volume itself as bullish or bearish.

## 3. Does this course give guaranteed entry signals?

**Model answer:** No. It teaches a process for reading evidence and forming conditional trade scenarios. A valid-looking setup can fail, and an outcome cannot be known with certainty in advance. A plan should state what would support the idea, what would weaken it, where it fails and when to stand aside.

**Transfer practice — Apply (Level 3):**** Explain one chart setup that you would watch but not yet enter, and name the missing trigger.

## 4. Is skipping a trade a failure?

**Model answer:** No. If the evidence is mixed, the trigger is missing, data quality is poor or the risk no longer fits your rules, skipping can be the disciplined decision. The purpose of the process is not to generate constant activity; it is to make decisions you can explain and review.

**Transfer practice — Apply (Level 3):**** Identify one condition that would make you cancel a setup even when the chart still looks attractive.

## 5. Can I copy a course example directly onto a live market?

**Model answer:** Use examples to learn the reasoning, not as a ready-made live signal. Instruments, sessions, volume feeds, costs and price structure differ. Repeat the process on the chart in front of you, verify what its data means and use your own defined risk rules.

**Transfer practice — Apply (Level 3):**** List three things you would verify before applying a lesson to a new instrument or market.

## 6. How do I know I have truly understood a lesson?

**Model answer:** You can explain the central idea in your own words, point to the chart evidence on a new example, give at least one reasonable alternative, and say what you would watch next. You should also be able to recognise a counterexample where the same initial clue leads to a different outcome. Memorising the name of a pattern is not enough.

**Transfer practice — Apply (Level 3):**** Choose an unfamiliar chart and teach its price-volume story aloud in one minute, using only observations you can point to.

## Instructor / product owner quality gate

Before adding a question to a lesson, ask again:

> **If a learner answers this correctly, what can they now do on a chart that they could not do before?**

Keep the question only if its answer demonstrates a useful chart-reading skill, corrects a likely misconception, improves a trade-planning decision or helps the learner recognise uncertainty. Otherwise, replace it with a scenario or remove it.

A strong Q&A section should give the learner something valuable to revisit after the video: a clear explanation, a way to practise, and a standard for checking their own reasoning. It should never feel like a box-ticking quiz placed at the end of a lesson.
