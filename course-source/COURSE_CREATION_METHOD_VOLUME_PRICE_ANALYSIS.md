# Reusable Course Creation Method
## The method used to design *Read Volume. Find Trade Setups.*

**Purpose:** Capture the course-design, teaching, writing and asset-production method so it can be repeated for other TradeThesis courses without reconstructing it from conversation history.

**Source course:** course-source/volume-price-analysis/  
**Working branch:** my-new-branch

## 1. The central teaching philosophy

### Teach the learner; do not hand the learner a narration script

The product is a learning experience the student can work through independently. The primary manuscript must speak directly to the student. It is not a script intended for the learner to read aloud, and it must not make the learner reconstruct an instructor's spoken explanation from production notes.

A teacher's voice is expressed through the order in which ideas are introduced, patient explanations of difficult distinctions, anticipation of beginner misunderstandings, worked examples, counterexamples that show the limits of a rule, useful prompts and feedback, and a calm, honest attitude toward uncertainty.

It is not expressed by writing “what the instructor says”, adding presenter cues, or turning every paragraph into a simulated spoken monologue. Narration may be written later if the course eventually includes video, but it is an optional production output—not the source of student learning content.

### Build understanding, not a collection of labels

Help students explain why an interpretation is reasonable and what could change it. Do not merely teach labels to memorise or patterns to treat as automatic signals.

In this course, for example, the student is taught to distinguish a visible candle/volume observation from an interpretation about pressure, absorption or a failed breakout. The method generalises to other subjects: distinguish what is known, what is inferred, what remains uncertain, and what evidence would help.

### Beginner-friendly does not mean shallow

Keep useful technical depth. Remove repetition, hidden assumptions, unnecessary jargon and narration-only filler. Replace them with clearer explanations, worked cases, plausible alternatives, meaningful practice and feedback explaining why reasoning is supported or limited.

A shorter lesson is not automatically better, and a long lesson is not automatically deep. Ask whether each section helps the student understand, practise or transfer the skill.

### Make uncertainty and no-action choices legitimate

A course about interpreting evidence must not present uncertain patterns as guarantees. Teach learners to state competing explanations, identify what would distinguish them and recognise when evidence is insufficient. A defensible “not enough information yet” or “wait” is a valid learning outcome—not a failure to guess.

### Put the visual evidence in front of the student

Do not assume students can imagine a chart, diagram, calculation, interface, sequence or physical process from prose alone. When seeing evidence is part of the skill, supply the evidence in the lesson: a clearly labelled figure, readable chart, diagram, worked table or interactive case.

An asset is not decorative reinforcement added after writing. It is part of how the concept is taught. Students should be able to see the exact event being discussed and connect the explanation to visible evidence.

## 2. The course-design sequence

Use these stages in order. They provide enough structure to keep the course coherent without over-engineering production.

### Stage A — Define the learner and the practical outcome

Write a concise course promise:
- Who is the learner?
- What does the learner know at the start?
- What should the learner be able to understand or do at the end?
- What is outside scope?
- What will the learner make, decide, explain or perform to demonstrate progress?

For this course, the outcome is not “memorise more volume patterns”. It is to read recorded activity with price, recognise relevant context, form conditional scenarios, construct a reasoned plan and review a decision. It is not a signal service or a promise of trading success.

The scope guard matters. Do not add another indicator, module or framework just because it is available. Include it only when it contributes to the course outcome.

### Stage B — Design learning progression before writing lessons

Build the module sequence from prerequisites to application. Each module should answer a distinct question and prepare the learner for the next one. Avoid modules that repeat the same ideas under different titles.

The progression used in this course is:

1. **Read the Market Behind the Candles** — describe candle outcome, activity, context and data limitations.
2. **Spot Buying and Selling Pressure** — judge achieved price progress, retained progress and effort/result relationships.
3. **Find Trade Setups at the Levels That Matter** — locate a question worth investigating and separate setup area, trigger and invalidation.
4. **Spot False Breakouts Before You Chase Them** — test a move beyond a boundary against later behaviour and structural evidence.
5. **Find the Price Levels Worth Watching** — use Volume Profile as a map of past activity at price, with explicit calculation-window limits.
6. **Put It All Together: Build a Complete Trading Plan** — integrate context, evidence, scenarios, triggers, invalidation, risk, management and review.

The sequence moves from observation to interpretation to location, testing, contextualisation and a complete planning task. For another subject, preserve the logic—foundations, discrimination, situated application, failure cases, broader context and integrated performance—while adapting it to the domain.

### Stage C — Define outcomes and practice for every lesson

For each lesson, state the skill the learner should be able to demonstrate at the end. Make the outcome observable. “Understand volume” is too broad; “compare a candle's price result with a stated activity baseline and explain one limitation” is testable.

Plan the main idea and why it matters, the likely beginner misunderstanding, a worked case, a contrasting case or plausible alternative, a learner task, feedback/model answer, a transfer task, and the visual or interaction needed to teach it.

Do this before polishing prose. Otherwise, a lesson can read well but offer the learner nothing to do with the information.

## 3. The repeatable student-facing lesson pattern

Treat this pattern as a flexible scaffold, not a rigid template to fill mechanically. Combine or omit sections when they do not help that particular lesson.

1. **Lesson title and outcome.** Tell students what they will learn to inspect, explain or do.
2. **A meaningful question.** Start with a practical situation, decision, uncertainty or common mistake.
3. **Plain-English explanation.** Explain the idea before introducing a specialist label. Build one step at a time.
4. **Evidence in view.** Show the chart, diagram, table, interface or worked data needed. Refer to a numbered figure if that reference improves navigation.
5. **Worked reasoning.** Explain what can be observed, what it may mean, what else could explain it and what would matter next.
6. **Comparison or counterexample.** Show how a similar-looking case changes the conclusion—or why the same evidence may support multiple interpretations.
7. **Supported practice.** Ask students to identify or compare features, with hints or feedback.
8. **Independent application.** Give a new case in which the student uses the idea without being told the result.
9. **Feedback or model answer.** Explain the reasoning and its limits, not only whether a choice was correct.
10. **Common mistake.** Correct a misconception that matters to the lesson.
11. **Takeaway and transfer.** End with a usable question, checklist or decision rule and a task that connects the idea to a fresh case.

The lesson should read naturally as teaching material. Do not display authoring metadata such as “Level 1”, “Level 2”, “easy”, “guided”, “independent”, timing cues or production labels to the student unless that information genuinely helps the learner. Difficulty progression can guide the writer and production team without being exposed as internal scaffolding.

## 4. Progressive practice: from recognition to transfer

The production team can use **Notice → Compare → Apply** as an internal practice framework. Student-facing headings should use natural, task-oriented language, not internal level labels.

### Recognition

First ask the learner to point out visible features or state a fact. Give enough context to reduce unnecessary cognitive load. Do not ask for a sophisticated interpretation before the learner knows what to look at.

Example in this course: identify candle direction, compare recorded volume against a stated baseline and note the preceding price sequence.

### Comparison

Then show a second case that resembles the first in one way but differs in another important way. Ask the learner to name the difference and explain why it matters.

Example: two similarly sized candles with different location, prior structure or relative volume; or two profile windows whose Point of Control changes because the underlying periods differ.

### Application

Finally, present a new case or withhold later information. Ask the learner to write an initial reading, list alternatives and specify what evidence would strengthen or weaken each interpretation. Reveal the later outcome only after the initial note is submitted where hindsight would undermine the exercise.

Practice must be genuine—not just a heading followed by a trivial multiple-choice check. Provide helpful feedback and a model answer explaining acceptable reasoning. Do not pretend that a text-length threshold semantically grades the learner's answer.

For subjects with one verifiable answer, use automatic correctness checks where appropriate. For interpretation tasks, accept reasoned alternatives and assess the method, not whether the learner guessed the eventual outcome.

## 5. Writing rules: clear, calm and intellectually honest

### Language and structure

- Use everyday meaning before specialist terminology.
- Define new terms when the student first needs them.
- Keep paragraphs focused; use tables and bullets when they improve comparison or scanning.
- Do not turn every sentence into a bullet list.
- Avoid duplicated explanation, inflated introductions and repeated conclusions.
- Keep terminology consistent across lesson text, figures, exercises, feedback, worksheets and later modules.
- Refer to a figure by number only when it improves clarity; do not clutter every paragraph with figure references.
- Separate student-facing explanations from internal design rationales, source notes, answer keys and asset-build instructions.

### Evidence and interpretation

Use this reasoning sequence where the topic involves interpreting observations:

1. **Observation:** What is directly visible or measured?
2. **Interpretation:** What is one plausible explanation?
3. **Alternative:** What else could explain the same facts?
4. **Discriminator:** What future evidence could help distinguish the explanations?
5. **Decision:** What is justified now, and what remains unresolved?

Do not write the interpretation as if it were an observed fact. Qualify claims to match the evidence. Where data quality, source, context or calculation settings affect meaning, state them clearly.

### Avoid false authority

- Do not make a visual pattern sound deterministic if it is not.
- Do not infer hidden intent, identity, causation or future outcomes without evidence.
- Do not use jargon as a substitute for explanation.
- Do not imply that students should always act after completing an exercise.
- Do not make a wrong outcome prove that the student's process was necessarily wrong, or a favourable outcome prove it was sound.

## 6. Visual and asset design as part of instruction

### Build each figure from the lesson's actual teaching need

For each lesson, ask what the learner must be able to see. Construct the asset around that question. Do not write generic paragraphs first and later add unrelated stock imagery.

In this course, each lesson needs a primary visual and a corresponding practice exercise. The current visual package model includes:
- an editable SVG figure;
- underlying OHLCV or profile data;
- an accessible text description;
- a standalone ChartLab that displays evidence, stages the task and reveals model reasoning;
- contrasting outcomes where later evidence matters.

For other courses, replace OHLCV with the relevant evidence: diagrams, interface captures, source extracts, worked calculations, anatomy, timelines, maps or illustrations of physical processes. Keep the same principle: supply the evidence the learner needs.

### Figure numbering and references

Use consistent publication-style figure numbering by module/chapter. The convention in this course is **Figure 1.1, Figure 1.2, …** for figures in Module 1, then **Figure 2.1, Figure 2.2, …** for Module 2, continuing by module. Assign numbers deterministically, update the asset manifest and use the same number in the image, accessible description and any useful lesson reference.

Do not print internal production notes such as “synthetic data” or “synthetic example” as a banner inside every figure. Keep the figure clean and instructional. Put any necessary provenance or qualification in the figure note, alt-text description, source-data file or surrounding lesson text. Do not present fictional examples as historical evidence: be clear in surrounding material that examples are illustrative.

### What an effective chart or diagram includes

For a price/volume chart:
- a readable price scale;
- visible candle bodies and wicks;
- a distinct volume pane aligned to each interval;
- an explained baseline;
- only meaningful levels or shaded zones;
- a callout to the event the learner should inspect;
- readable axis labels and sufficient spacing;
- consistent up/down encoding supplemented by text or shape, not colour alone;
- no decorative indicators that obscure the intended distinction.

For a profile chart, show the actual horizontal activity-by-price distribution alongside price, label the selected period and explain calculated references. Do not substitute an ordinary candle chart for a Volume Profile.

For other subjects, define the equivalents: scale, labels, legend, focus element, source and what is not represented.

### The same initial evidence, different endings

Where an interpretation depends on follow-through, show the initial chart first. Ask the learner to make an initial record. Only then reveal contrasting continuations from the same opening case. This makes hindsight visible and teaches students to update reasoning as evidence arrives.

### Accessibility and readability

- Provide meaningful alt text and a longer description for complex figures.
- Ensure labels remain readable on a phone and at 200% zoom.
- Avoid relying on colour alone to convey meaning.
- Make interactive charts keyboard-accessible, with a visible focus state and sensible focus order.
- Explain visual content in text as well as visually.
- Test the rendered asset; SVG markup in a repository has not automatically passed visual QA.
- If the chart is not readable at its intended display size, redesign it rather than expecting the student to infer tiny labels.

### Asset integrity

Every invented visual should have the source data and editable master required to regenerate or correct it. Use licensed sources when real-world assets are required, and keep provenance/rights notes. Never present an invented chart as a genuine historical snapshot.

## 7. Interactive learning components

Use an interaction when it creates meaningful learner work: comparing evidence, answering a question, annotating a chart, testing a scenario, calculating a value or reviewing the impact of new information.

A useful ChartLab includes a short purpose and clear instructions, the chart/evidence on screen, a concrete task, a response area or meaningful interactive action, helpful hints/model explanation, a counterexample where it teaches something, reasoning-focused feedback, a reset/retry path, responsive layout, accessibility support, and an explicit statement if responses are not stored or automatically graded.

Do not build an elaborate app when a well-labelled figure and short task teach the idea better. Reuse a small, consistent interaction pattern and vary the evidence to fit each lesson. Do not simply clone the same generic chart into 30 lessons; every visual and task must be grounded in the lesson's content.

For a multi-step course, internal planning might name the stages Notice, Compare and Apply. In the learner interface, use meaningful headings like “Look closely”, “Compare the cases” and “Try a new case”—not internal complexity levels or production tags.

## 8. Checks, answers and assessment

For each lesson:
- include factual checks where there is a definite observable answer;
- include interpretive prompts where more than one reading is reasonable;
- show model reasoning and explain why alternatives are weak or still possible;
- make the rubric distinguish evidence, interpretation, alternative, future discriminator and decision;
- provide feedback that teaches rather than merely says “correct”;
- include transfer to a new case, not only repetition of the example already shown.

At module level, assess whether the learner can use the module's concepts together. At course level, the capstone should require the whole decision process to be performed in sequence with relevant evidence visible.

A completion gate is not a grade. A task requiring 30 characters in a text box has not assessed understanding. Either implement real grading logic and test it, or state transparently that the response is only recorded or completed and is not automatically judged.

## 9. Timing without padding

For this course the planning target was around 12–16 minutes for a typical lesson, with a hard minimum of more than 10 minutes and an ordinary upper range of 20 minutes unless the complexity and practice justify more. Adapt the time target to another course's purpose and audience.

Do not validate time from narration word count alone. Measure the actual learner journey: reading the explanation, inspecting the figure, completing the task and reviewing feedback/knowledge checks. Record the rendered version, device, approximate task time, learner confusion and whether help was required. Pair duration with understanding; long time-on-page can mean confusion, not depth.

If a lesson is too short, add meaningful practice, a worked case, a counterexample or a necessary step. Do not pad with repeated paragraphs or reintroduce narration-only content to inflate word count.

## 10. Production organisation and source of truth

Keep student material and production notes separate.

### Student-facing source

Contains the lesson title, explanations, useful figure references, worked reasoning, tasks, feedback, common mistake and takeaway. It should read as finished instructional content.

### Asset/production source

Contains figure briefs, chart specifications, asset-generation details, datasets, captions/alt text, answer keys where needed, validation checklists, timing results, source/rights notes and implementation details.

### Course-level source

Contains the course rationale, intended audience, scope, module map, editorial rules, terminology and this reusable method.

When these sources conflict, do not quietly let a production brief overrule a clear teaching lesson. Reconcile the difference and update the relevant files deliberately. Keep the manifest truthful; distinguish “file created” from “rendered and tested”.

## 11. Checkpoint discipline and release gates

Save work to GitHub at meaningful intervals, especially before a multi-file build or after a complete lesson/module package. Use clear commit messages. For this course, work stays on my-new-branch; do not merge into main, publish or deploy without explicit approval.

After each batch:
1. Confirm that expected files exist.
2. Check their titles and lesson mapping.
3. Verify figure numbers and references.
4. Check data/visual alignment and staged outcome logic.
5. Ensure student-facing pages do not display internal authoring labels.
6. Update the build log with completed packages and known gaps.
7. Do a focused source-level structural check.

Before release, separately test the actual browser version, responsive sizing, zoom, keyboard focus, assistive-technology output, learner comprehension, time-on-task and interaction/storage behaviour. Do not imply those checks passed merely because files were committed.

Avoid endless speculative redesign. Once the teaching method and component pattern are sound, produce in batches, checkpoint regularly and perform a focused validation pass per batch.

## 12. Replication checklist for the next course

- [ ] Define learner, outcome, prerequisite assumptions and exclusions.
- [ ] Design the module progression from foundations to integrated application.
- [ ] Write an explicit scope guard.
- [ ] Set the student-first lesson standard before drafting.
- [ ] Give each lesson an observable outcome and identify a likely beginner misunderstanding.
- [ ] Plan the worked example, counterexample, task, feedback and transfer task.
- [ ] Identify figures/diagrams needed to teach the actual idea; do not expect learners to imagine the evidence.
- [ ] Build source data and accessible descriptions alongside visuals.
- [ ] Use consistent figure numbering and refer to figures only when helpful.
- [ ] Keep Notice → Compare → Apply as internal practice design; use natural student-facing headings.
- [ ] Build meaningful self-study interactions without unnecessary application complexity.
- [ ] Align answers and assessment with the lesson content.
- [ ] Measure the rendered experience rather than assuming duration from word count.
- [ ] Separate student source, production source and course-level editorial documentation.
- [ ] Checkpoint on the intended feature branch in batches.
- [ ] Validate exact output and record what has and has not been tested.
- [ ] Do not claim completion until content, visuals, accessibility, interaction and learner use meet agreed criteria.
